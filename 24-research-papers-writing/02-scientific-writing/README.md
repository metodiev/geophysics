# Module 24-02 — Scientific Writing

**Stages:** intermediate → doctoral · **Prerequisites:** module
[24-01 Reading the Research Literature](../01-reading-research-literature/README.md);
[24-02-01](01-writing-principles.md) assumes you can already find and appraise a
paper.

This module treats scientific writing as a **budget problem with an argument inside
it**. Every container has a limit — a title of a dozen words, an abstract of
$250$, a Methods section that must fit the decisions it has to record — and the
work of writing is deciding what the budget buys. Each lesson therefore measures
something a writer actually controls, and reports the measurement rather than the
advice: sentence-length tails and readability arithmetic, sectional word budgets
and the specificity index, parameter elasticities and the reproduction interval
that an unreported parameter leaves a reader, the separation between $p$-values
and effect sizes, caption self-containment and data-ink ratios, and the diminishing
returns of successive revision passes.

The organising thread is **consistency between claim and evidence, at every
length**. The same claim is rendered at four lengths: title, abstract, Results and
Discussion. Each rendering has its own failure mode. A title that names no method
is unfindable; an abstract that drops the uncertainty of a null result is
misleading; a Results sentence without a sample size is unusable; a Discussion
claim without a falsification condition is unfalsifiable. The module's lessons
build the four renderings in that order and check them against each other.

All corpora, figures, caption rates, elasticities and corpora statistics in this
module are **SYNTHETIC**, generated by the seeded demonstrations with chosen
teaching parameters. No published paper, abstract, caption or manuscript was
measured. Where a lesson uses a published convention or formula — IMRaD structure,
the Flesch reading-ease index, the genre-analysis "niche" move, the Bonferroni
correction — it is attributed in the text and used as a stated definition, not as a
measurement of a corpus.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

1. **Rewrite** a passage so that sentences obey given-before-new ordering and
   **measure** the resulting change in length statistics and readability.
2. **Allocate** a word budget across a paper's sections from its claim, and
   **diagnose** an unbalanced draft from its proportions.
3. **Rank** the parameters of a method by elasticity and **state** the interval an
   unreported parameter leaves a reader, together with the tolerance at which you
   claim reproducibility.
4. **Separate** significance, effect size and precision, and **compute** the
   winner's-curse inflation and the family-wise error rate of a set of tests.
5. **Write** a title with all four retrieval elements and an abstract whose numbers
   are traceable to the Results.
6. **Apply** the separated-figure test to a figure and caption, and **justify**
   ink and colour choices with measurable criteria.
7. **Plan** revision as four targeted passes and **measure** how much of the text
   actually changed.
8. **Write** a point-by-point response to reviewers and **estimate** the cost of an
   unaddressed point.

## Suggested path

**If you are writing your first paper:** 02 (structure) → 03 (Methods) → 05
(abstract and title), running each demonstration. The section budgets in 02 are the
plan; 03 and 05 are the two places where the plan most often fails.

**If you are revising a manuscript under review:** 07 → 01 → 04. Start with the pass
structure and the response-letter arithmetic; then apply the sentence-level rules to
the changed sections only, and check every claim against 04's reporting rules.

**If you are preparing figures:** 06 → 02 §5. The separated-figure test and the
data-ink ratio are cheap and they change a paper more than most prose editing.

**If you supervise or review:** 03 §4 (the four-part decision sentence) and 04 §7
(the submission check) are the two lists worth giving to a student, because both are
checkable in ten minutes.

## Mastery checkpoint

1. Take $300$ words of your own prose and apply the four passes of lesson 07,
   recording words removed per hour.
2. Allocate a word budget for a paper you are writing and justify each section's
   share.
3. Rank your method's parameters by elasticity and state the tolerance at which you
   claim reproduction.
4. Rewrite one Results sentence as estimate + interval + sample size, and one
   Discussion sentence with a falsification condition.
5. Write a four-element title and verify that every number in your abstract appears
   in the Results.
6. Apply the separated-figure test to your figures and fix the weakest caption
   element in each.

## Common pitfalls across this module

1. **Polishing prose before the structure is right.** Structural changes invalidate
   prose work; sequence the passes.
2. **Quoting a $p$-value as a magnitude.** The demonstration shows a $p$ of
   $10^{-10}$ on an effect of $0.20$ standard deviations.
3. **Reporting the parameters you remember.** Rank by elasticity, not by familiarity.
4. **Trimming adjectives to fit a word limit.** The words are in whole sentences.
5. **A caption that describes the figure instead of arguing it.** Without the
   comparison sentence, a reader must guess the point.
6. **Leaving a reviewer's point unaddressed.** Each one costs about $0.6$ of a review
   round.
7. **Quoting these synthetic numbers as field norms.** Every table in this module
   describes a teaching generator.
