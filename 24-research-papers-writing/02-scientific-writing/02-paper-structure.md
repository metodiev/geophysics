# Paper Structure: IMRaD and Its Variants

A paper's structure is a word budget attached to an argument. The four IMRaD
blocks carry different jobs, and the job determines how many words each deserves
and which sentences are allowed inside it. This lesson works the structure from
the writer's side: what each section must accomplish, how the sectional budget is
distributed in a measured corpus, how often authors merge or add sections, and
where the numbers end up.

The organising idea is the **specificity gradient**. A paper narrows from a wide
context, through a specific method and result, back to wide consequences. Word
counts follow the argument, not the other way round: if the Discussion is shorter
than the Introduction, the paper has usually reported a number without saying what
changed.

> **Data provenance.** All numbers are **SYNTHETIC**, generated by the
> accompanying demonstration from chosen parameters. Nothing here is a survey of
> real manuscripts, and the quartiles below must not be quoted as journal norms.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Assign** a word budget to each section of a paper from its claim and its
   evidence, and **justify** the budget in terms of the section's job.
2. **Compute** sectional shares and the specificity index
   $(\text{Methods}+\text{Results})/(\text{Introduction}+\text{Discussion})$ for a
   manuscript.
3. **Diagnose** an unbalanced draft from its proportions and **state** which
   section is doing too little work.
4. **Choose** a structural variant (merged Results and Discussion, Theory section,
   setting section) and **state** the consequence of the choice for a reader.
5. **Locate** the quantitative content of a paper and **explain** why Results
   carries most of the numbers and the Discussion almost none.
6. **Write** the four transitions that hold a paper together: context to gap, gap
   to method, method to result, result to consequence.

## 1. Jobs, not headings

Each block has a job, and the job constrains the prose:

| Block | Job | Allowed | Forbidden |
|---|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Establish the gap and the question | Citing prior work, defining scope | Presenting your results |
| Methods | Make the result reproducible | Past tense, parameters, equations | Interpretation, hedging of outcomes |
| Results | Present evidence with uncertainty | Numbers, tables, figures, statistics | Causal claims, comparison with other work |
| Discussion | Interpret, compare, limit | Speculation marked as such, limitations | New results |

The forbidden column is what makes the structure useful: a reader who finds a new
number in the Discussion knows the structure has been broken, and a reviewer who
finds an interpretation in the Results knows the comparison has not been done yet.

## 2. Worked example: a measured budget

The demonstration generates $300$ manuscripts with a mean length of
$6577 \pm 2155$ words. The budget is

| Block | Words | Share of the paper |
|---|---|---|
| Abstract | $247$ | $3.8\%$ |
| IMRaD body | $4999$ | $76.0\%$ |
| Captions, acknowledgements, references | $1331$ | $20.2\%$ |

and inside the body:

| Section | Words | Share of body | Between-paper s.d. of share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Introduction | $981$ | $19.6\%$ | $15.0\%$ |
| Methods | $1417$ | $28.4\%$ | $18.7\%$ |
| Results | $1213$ | $24.3\%$ | $18.7\%$ |
| Discussion | $1388$ | $27.8\%$ | $18.3\%$ |

The specificity index is $1.425 \pm 1.076$, so the narrow waist
($\text{Methods}+\text{Results}$) is about $1.4$ times the wide ends
($\text{Introduction}+\text{Discussion}$) in word count. $65.0\%$ of papers have a
Discussion longer than their Introduction.

**How to use these numbers.** They are a sanity check, not a target. A Methods
share of $8\%$ in a paper claiming a new processing chain is a warning; a Methods
share of $45\%$ in a method paper is correct. The useful arithmetic is the
comparison with *your own* claim: how many words does each claim need to be
either established or limited?

## 3. The four transitions

A paper's coherence lives almost entirely in four transitions, each of which
occupies one to three sentences:

1. **Context → gap.** Name what is established, then what is missing. The niche
   move in the genre-analysis tradition (Swales, *Genre Analysis*, Cambridge
   University Press, 1990) is the second half of this.
2. **Gap → method.** Say what would have to be measured to close the gap, then say
   that you measured it.
3. **Method → result.** State the primary result with its uncertainty, before the
   secondary results. A reader who stops here should have the paper's point.
4. **Result → consequence.** State what changes if the result holds, and what would
   change if it does not.

Most rejected drafts are weak at transition 2 or transition 4. A weak transition 2
produces a paper that reads as an exercise; a weak transition 4 produces a paper
that reports without concluding.

## 4. Variants and their consequences

The demonstration's corpus contains $28.7\%$ papers with a merged Results and
Discussion, $13.7\%$ with a separate Theory section and $32.7\%$ with a Geological
setting section; $59.7\%$ have at least one variant and only $0.3\%$ have all
three. Variants are normal, but each has a consequence:

- **Merged Results and Discussion.** Shortens the paper and lets each result be
  interpreted next to its evidence. The cost: the reader can no longer find "the
  interpretation" in one place, and the uncertainty of a number and the
  speculation about it can blur together.
- **Separate Theory section.** Standard in method papers. The cost: the reading
  order becomes theory-before-motivation unless the Introduction explicitly says
  why the theory is needed first.
- **Setting or regional section.** Necessary when geology controls the result. The
  cost: it delays the Methods by a page, and reviewers of a methods paper will skip
  it — which is an argument for moving the material to the supplement unless it
  changes the interpretation.

## 5. Where the numbers live

Assigning numbers per 1000 words by section ($1.5$, $6.0$, $14.0$ and $4.0$ for
Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion), the demonstration finds:

| Section | Numbers per paper | Share of the paper's numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | $1.5$ | $4.5\%$ |
| Methods | $8.5$ | $26.2\%$ |
| Results | $17.0$ | $52.2\%$ |
| Discussion | $5.6$ | $17.1\%$ |

The Introduction and Discussion together carry $21.6\%$ of the numbers in $47.4\%$
of the body words; Results alone carries $52.2\%$. This is a structural prediction
you can use while writing: **if a claim is quantitative, its number belongs in
Results, and if it is qualitative, its justification belongs in the Discussion.**
Writing a number into the Discussion is usually a sign that a result was left out
where the reader expected it.

## 6. Writing to the budget

A workable order of operations:

1. Write the claim of the paper in one sentence, with scope and uncertainty.
2. Write the four transitions.
3. Allocate words: the sum of the four section budgets should equal your target
   body length, and the Methods budget should be the number of words needed to
   reproduce, not the number of words you enjoyed writing.
4. Draft the Results first, because it constrains everything else.
5. Draft the Discussion last, trimming it to the claims the Results support.

## Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **The corpus is synthetic and its parameters chosen.** The word counts and
   shares are teaching values, not norms from any journal or field.
2. **Article type dominates the budget.** A methods paper, a case study and a data
   descriptor have different correct proportions; the table above describes
   research articles reporting new measurements.
3. **Journal limits override taste.** Where a journal caps Methods or total length,
   the cap, not the argument, sets the budget; report the compromise.
4. **The numbers-per-section rates are assigned, not measured.** They illustrate
   where quantitative content belongs, not how often it appears.
5. **Proportions are diagnostic, never decisive.** A short Discussion can be
   correct for a short letter.

## The honest summary

Structure is a budget attached to an argument. The measured corpus shows a
specificity index of $1.425$ and a Discussion longer than the Introduction in
$65.0\%$ of papers, with Results carrying $52.2\%$ of the quantitative content.
Those are diagnostics; the writing decision is the four transitions, and no
proportion table will write them for you.

## Mastery checkpoint

For a paper you are planning:

1. Write the claim in one sentence with scope and uncertainty.
2. Write the four transitions in four to twelve sentences.
3. Allocate a word budget to the four sections and sum it against your target.
4. For each figure and table, name the sentence in Results that will present it.
5. State which structural variant you are using and what it costs the reader.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Writing the Introduction last and leaving it long.** The Introduction is a
   budget, and an unbudgeted one expands.
2. **Announcing results in the Introduction.** It breaks the argument and it
   removes the reason to read the Results.
3. **Burying the primary result among secondary ones.** The first result sentence
   of Results should be the paper's point.
4. **Merging Results and Discussion without marking the boundary.** In a merged
   section, start interpretive sentences with a marker that separates them from
   the numbers.
5. **Letting the Discussion introduce new results.** If a number appears only in
   the Discussion, a reviewer will ask for it to be moved and rerun.
6. **Treating proportions as targets.** They are warnings, not instructions.
