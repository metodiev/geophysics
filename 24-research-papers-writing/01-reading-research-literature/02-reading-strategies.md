# Reading Strategies: Skim, Map, Deep-Read, Critique

You will never read every paper that touches your project, and pretending
otherwise is the fastest route to reading nothing well. The practical skill is
**triage**: deciding, in a few minutes and with a stated error rate, which
papers deserve the expensive pass. This lesson treats the decision as a
measurement problem with a cost, a yield and two kinds of error, and shows what
each strategy actually buys.

The organising idea is that reading has **four passes with four different
outputs**. A skim produces a label; a map produces a structure; a deep read
produces a reproduction; a critique produces a judgement. Each pass costs an
order of magnitude more than the last, so the question is never "should I read
this?" but "which pass, given what I need it for?"

> **Data provenance.** All numbers in this lesson are **SYNTHETIC**. The reading
> queue, relevance labels and screening performance are generated by the
> accompanying demonstration; the minutes per pass and the information yields
> are chosen teaching values, not measured human-factors results.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Define** the four passes — skim, map, deep-read, critique — and **state**
   the output and the cost of each.
2. **Compute** precision, recall and the number needed to screen for a triage
   rule, and **interpret** each in reading terms.
3. **Compare** the total hours of a brute-force reading plan with a
   triage-then-deep-read plan, and **quantify** the relevant papers that the
   triage loses.
4. **Estimate** an information yield per hour for each pass and **choose** a pass
   for a stated purpose.
5. **Predict** how the value of triage changes with the prior relevance rate, and
   **explain** why triage matters least in your own subfield.
6. **Write** a structured three-line reading log entry that survives a year.

## 1. Four passes, four outputs

| Pass | Time per paper | Output | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skim | 3 min | A label: keep, maybe, drop | Is this about my problem at all? |
| Map | 10 min | A structured note: claim, method, data, result, caveat | What exactly did they do, and do I believe the design? |
| Deep read | 45 min | A reproduction: I could redo this | How did they get this number, and can I check it? |
| Critique | hours | A written judgement | Is the inference valid, and what would falsify it? |

The numbers are illustrative. What matters is the **ratio**: mapping costs about
three times a skim and a deep read about fifteen times a skim. A strategy that
spends deep-read time on papers that a skim would have dropped is spending the
scarce resource on the wrong object.

## 2. Triage is a binary classifier, so it has the usual errors

A triage rule reads the title and abstract and returns *screen positive*
(promising) or *screen negative* (drop). Compared with the truth — you would have
found the paper useful — it has four outcomes:

$$N_{\mathrm{TP}},\ N_{\mathrm{FP}},\ N_{\mathrm{FN}},\ N_{\mathrm{TN}},$$

from which

$$\text{precision} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{TP}}}{N_{\mathrm{TP}}+N_{\mathrm{FP}}},
\qquad
\text{recall} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{TP}}}{N_{\mathrm{TP}}+N_{\mathrm{FN}}},
\qquad
F_1 = \frac{2\,\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}}{\text{precision}+\text{recall}}.$$

Precision answers "of the papers I deep-read, how many were worth it?" Recall
answers "of the useful papers, how many did I keep?" The **number needed to
screen**, $1/\text{precision}$, is the practical cost: papers read per useful
paper found.

## 3. Worked example: a 240-paper queue

The demonstration builds a queue of $240$ papers with a $25\%$ relevance prior
($58$ useful papers) and screens it with sensitivity $0.90$ and specificity
$0.82$ — reasonable values for a competent title-and-abstract judgement.

**Confusion counts.** $N_{\mathrm{TP}} = 55$, $N_{\mathrm{FP}} = 30$,
$N_{\mathrm{FN}} = 3$, $N_{\mathrm{TN}} = 152$. Hence

$$\text{precision} = \frac{55}{85} = 64.7\%,
\qquad
\text{recall} = \frac{55}{58} = 94.8\%,
\qquad
F_1 = 0.769 .$$

The number needed to screen is $1.55$ papers per useful hit: about one deep read
in three is a paper that triage should have dropped.

**Cost.** Deep-reading everything is $240 \times 45\ \mathrm{min} = 180.0\
\mathrm{h}$, or $186\ \mathrm{min}$ per useful paper found. Skimming all $240$
costs $12.0\ \mathrm{h}$; adding a deep read of the $85$ screen positives gives

$$12.0\ \mathrm{h} + 85 \times 0.75\ \mathrm{h} = 75.8\ \mathrm{h},$$

a saving of $57.9\%$ of the hours, at the cost of $3$ useful papers missed. A
fuller map pass for every paper instead costs $115.8\ \mathrm{h}$ and finds the
same $55$ — mapping everything is rarely worth it; mapping the *screen
positives* is the right use of that pass.

## 4. Information yield per hour

Time spent is only half the accounting. Let the fraction of the usable content a
pass captures be $y_{\text{pass}}$: $0.30$ for a skim, $0.65$ for a map, $0.95$
for a deep read. Then the yield per hour is

$$\eta_{\text{pass}} = \frac{y_{\text{pass}}}{t_{\text{pass}}} \times 60\ \mathrm{min/h},$$

giving $6.00$, $3.90$ and $1.27$ content-units per hour for skim, map and deep
read respectively. Skimming is nearly five times more efficient *per unit of
content* — and useless if you need the content that only a deep read recovers.
Efficiency and sufficiency are different questions, and the honest strategy uses
each pass for the purpose it is efficient at.

Over the whole queue, brute force captures $228.0$ content-units for $180.0\
\mathrm{h}$; the two-pass strategy captures $152.8$ content-units for $75.8\
\mathrm{h}$, that is $67.0\%$ of the content for $42.1\%$ of the time. The
question "is triage worth it?" therefore has a number attached: you trade $33\%$
of the marginal content for $58\%$ of the time.

## 5. When triage stops paying

The value of triage depends on the prior relevance rate $p$. With the same
screen, the demonstration reports:

| Prior relevance $p$ | Papers screened positive | Two-pass cost (h) | Brute force (h) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $5\%$ | 47 | 47.2 | 180.0 |
| $25\%$ | 90 | 79.5 | 180.0 |
| $50\%$ | 130 | 109.5 | 180.0 |
| $75\%$ | 187 | 152.2 | 180.0 |
| $100\%$ | 213 | 171.8 | 180.0 |

In a field where almost everything in the queue is relevant, triage saves
almost nothing and still risks missing a paper. That is the quantitative version
of a familiar experience: the skim-and-triage habit helps most in an unfamiliar
literature and least in your own.

## 6. The reading log entry

A useful log entry fits in three lines and is written *during* the map pass, not
after:

1. **Claim** — the result with its scope and uncertainty, in the paper's own
   units.
2. **Basis** — the data, the method and the sample size, in one clause.
3. **Caveat and use** — the limitation the authors state or omit, and why you
   filed it (method to reuse, number to compare, claim to test).

An entry that records only the claim cannot be used eighteen months later,
because the claim without its basis is indistinguishable from a memory.

## Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **The screening performance is modelled, not measured.** Sensitivity $0.90$
   and specificity $0.82$ are chosen; real triage performance varies with the
   reader's expertise, the abstract's quality and the field, and should be
   calibrated per reader.
2. **Relevance is binary and known.** In practice relevance is graded and
   discovered late: a paper triaged as irrelevant may become essential when the
   project changes.
3. **The time costs are averages.** Reading speed varies by an order of magnitude
   between a familiar and an unfamiliar method.
4. **The information yields are a one-parameter summary of a many-sided thing.**
   A deep read may yield nothing new, or everything, depending on the paper's
   relation to your question.
5. **The queue is closed.** Real queues grow while you read them, which is the
   subject of [05 Keeping Up](05-keeping-up-with-literature.md).

## The honest summary

Triage is a classifier with a precision and a recall, and reading plans should
be described the same way as any other detection scheme: with the threshold, the
cost and the error rate. The demonstration shows a two-pass strategy recovering
$67\%$ of the content in $42\%$ of the time while missing $3$ of $58$ relevant
papers. It cannot tell you whether those three mattered; only your project can.

## Mastery checkpoint

For the next ten papers you open:

1. Record the pass you actually performed and the minutes it took.
2. Record whether a later pass changed your label. If a skim label never changes,
   your triage is calibrated; if it changes often, it is not.
3. Compute your own precision over the ten: useful deep reads divided by deep
   reads attempted.
4. Write one three-line log entry per screen positive.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Deep-reading the first promising paper.** The first paper in a new field is
   usually not the best one; triage the queue before committing.
2. **Treating the abstract as evidence.** Screening on abstracts is fine;
   *citing* on abstracts is not.
3. **Confusing reading volume with reading progress.** Papers skimmed are not
   papers understood.
4. **Letting the queue grow without a cap.** An uncapped alert stream defeats any
   triage rule; see [05 Keeping Up](05-keeping-up-with-literature.md).
5. **Recording claims without bases.** A claim whose data and sample size are not
   written down cannot be weighed later.
6. **Never re-reading the triage negatives.** A literature review is the moment
   to revisit them, because the question has changed.

## Where this is used

- **Starting a PhD:** the first six months are a triage problem; stating your
  precision and recall prevents an unproductive reading spiral.
- **Writing a review article:** a documented search and screen is the difference
  between a review and an opinion; see
  [04 Systematic Literature Search](04-literature-search.md).
- **Preparing a grant application:** the competition scan is a triage exercise
  with a deadline.
- **Reviewing:** the four passes are the same passes a referee performs, in the
  opposite order of detail; see
