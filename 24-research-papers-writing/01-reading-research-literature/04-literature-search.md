# Systematic Literature Search and Reference Management

An unsystematic search produces a bibliography that reflects what you happened to
find. A systematic search produces one you can describe, repeat and defend —
including its error rates. This lesson turns searching into a measurable
procedure: a query has a precision, a recall and a screening cost, and a
reference library has a duplicate rate that can be estimated rather than wished
away.

The organising idea is that **a search is a query with a documented protocol**.
The protocol names the sources, the cut-off date, the query strings, the
inclusion criteria and the screening decisions — and, crucially, the number of
records each step removed. A protocol makes the review's coverage auditable;
without one, "we searched the literature" is a claim that cannot be checked.

> **Data provenance.** All numbers are **SYNTHETIC**. The corpus, relevance
> labels, query behaviour and duplicate pairs are generated by the accompanying
> demonstration. No database was queried and no real bibliometric distribution
> is quoted.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** a narrative search from a systematic protocol, and **list**
   the elements a protocol must state.
2. **Compute** precision, recall and F1 for keyword, phrase and Boolean queries
   on the same corpus, and **explain** why no single query maximises both.
3. **Design** a query by combining a broad recall-oriented term with a
   precision-oriented restriction, and **measure** the screening load it creates.
4. **Estimate** a duplicate rate from title similarity, **choose** a threshold,
   and **state** the false-merge risk at that threshold.
5. **Structure** a reference library so that records are traceable to the query
   that found them and to the decision that kept them.
6. **Report** a search so that another reader could reproduce the corpus.

## 1. Protocol before query

A defensible protocol states, in advance:

- **Sources** — which databases, indexes and preprint servers, with their
  coverage dates.
- **Query strings** — the exact syntax, so that the record counts can be
  reproduced.
- **Inclusion and exclusion criteria** — language, date, document type, minimum
  sample or instrument specification.
- **Screening stages** — title and abstract first, then full text, with the
  number of records removed at each stage.
- **Handling of duplicates and of grey literature** — preprints, theses,
  conference abstracts.
- **Yield accounting** — a flow of numbers from records retrieved to studies
  included.

The value of the protocol is that it makes the *yield* reportable. A review that
reports "48 studies were included" without the preceding numbers has told you
its conclusion but not its filter.

## 2. Queries are classifiers

Every query partitions the corpus into retrieved and not retrieved. Against the
set of truly relevant records the four counts are the usual ones, and

$$\text{precision} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{TP}}}{N_{\mathrm{TP}}+N_{\mathrm{FP}}},
\qquad
\text{recall} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{TP}}}{N_{\mathrm{TP}}+N_{\mathrm{FN}}},
\qquad
F_1 = \frac{2PR}{P+R}.$$

Two design intuitions follow directly:

- **Broadening** a query (adding synonyms with OR) increases recall and adds false
  positives, so precision falls.
- **Restricting** a query (AND, field limits, proximity) increases precision and
  loses true positives, so recall falls.

This is a trade-off, not a defect. The practical consequence is that the query
design is decided by the *cost of a missed paper* relative to the *cost of
screening a false positive*.

## 3. Worked example: five queries on a 4000-record corpus

The demonstration builds a $4000$-record corpus with $240$ relevant records
($6.0\%$ prior prevalence) and applies five queries.

| Query | Retrieved | TP | FP | FN | Precision | Recall | F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| single keyword | 1441 | 132 | 1309 | 108 | $9.2\%$ | $55.0\%$ | 0.157 |
| exact phrase | 184 | 75 | 109 | 165 | $40.8\%$ | $31.2\%$ | 0.354 |
| method keyword | 834 | 111 | 723 | 129 | $13.3\%$ | $46.2\%$ | 0.207 |
| region keyword | 441 | 88 | 353 | 152 | $20.0\%$ | $36.7\%$ | 0.258 |
| Boolean AND | 108 | 40 | 68 | 200 | $37.0\%$ | $16.7\%$ | 0.230 |

Read the table as a set of instruments, not a ranking. The single keyword finds
more than half the relevant corpus and buries it in $1309$ irrelevant records —
$1.55\ \mathrm{h}$ of screening at $1.5\ \mathrm{min}$ per record just to reach the
$1441$. The Boolean AND retrieves $108$ records and finds $40$ of the $240$
relevant papers: efficient screening, unacceptable coverage for a review. F1
prefers the exact phrase ($0.354$), which is a statement about the balance of
the two errors under equal cost weighting — and equal weighting is a choice.

**Combining queries.** The union of the broad and narrow queries retrieves
$1543$ records, finds $159$ relevant ($\text{precision } 10.3\%$, recall
$66.2\%$); the intersection retrieves $82$, finds $48$ ($\text{precision }
58.5\%$, recall $20.0\%$). Screening the union costs

$$1543 \times 1.5\ \mathrm{min} = 2314\ \mathrm{min} = 38.6\ \mathrm{h},$$

which is the honest price of a recall-oriented review search and should be
planned for, not discovered.

**Typical sequence.** Run the union; de-duplicate; screen titles and abstracts
against the criteria; map the survivors; then use the intersection-style query
as a *cluster detector* for the densest part of the topic, not as the search.

## 4. De-duplication is a threshold decision

Records from several sources overlap: the same paper appears with a DOI-less
record, a different capitalisation, an added subtitle, or a preprint version.
Exact-match de-duplication catches only identical identifiers; similarity-based
de-duplication is needed, and it has both error types.

Similarity is computed on shingles — sets of consecutive words or characters —
with the Jaccard coefficient

$$J(A,B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}.$$

The demonstration builds $240$ true duplicate pairs (one word replaced, one
adjacent pair transposed) and $120$ unrelated pairs, then reports detection and
false merges by threshold:

| Threshold | Duplicates caught | False-merge rate |
|---|---|---|
| 0.30 | $142/240 = 59.2\%$ | $0.0\%$ |
| 0.40 | $84/240 = 35.0\%$ | $0.0\%$ |
| 0.50 | $35/240 = 14.6\%$ | $0.0\%$ |
| 0.60 | $7/240 = 2.9\%$ | $0.0\%$ |
| 0.70 | $1/240 = 0.4\%$ | $0.0\%$ |

The mean similarity of true pairs is $0.340 \pm 0.132$; of unrelated pairs,
$0.002 \pm 0.009$. The separation is wide but the true pairs are *not* tight:
identical-topic titles with one swapped word already drop below $0.5$. At
threshold $0.30$ the rule catches $59.2\%$ of duplicates with no false merges on
this corpus; the remaining $40.8\%$ must be found by eye or by matching
identifiers such as DOI and abstract text.

The lesson is not the particular threshold. It is that **de-duplication has a
miss rate you can estimate** and that a library with no de-duplication step has a
duplicate rate you have not measured.

## 5. Reference management as data management

A library is a small database, and the practices are the same:

- **Stable keys.** Let the reference manager own its own citation key; do not
  reuse a key when a record is corrected.
- **One record per work.** Merge duplicates rather than deleting one, so the
  surviving record keeps every identifier.
- **Provenance fields.** Record the source database, the query that found the
  record and the date. When a review's coverage is challenged, this is the
  evidence.
- **Screening decision.** Record included and excluded, with one-line reasons.
  An exclusion reason that cannot be written in a line is usually an inclusion.
- **A version you control.** Plain-text or BibTeX under version control survives
  a vendor change, an account loss and a laptop failure; a vendor-only library
  does not. The tooling is covered in
  [05-03 Collaborative Writing](../../22-data-engineering-reproducibility/05-collaboration-and-project-management/03-collaborative-writing.md).

## Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **The corpus and query behaviour are synthetic.** Real query performance
   depends on the index's tokenisation, its ranking and the field's vocabulary;
   the numbers here illustrate the arithmetic, not any database's performance.
2. **Relevance is binary and fixed.** In practice relevance depends on the
   review's inclusion criteria, which sharpen as the search proceeds.
3. **Similarity is a proxy for identity.** Two records can be identical in
   content and dissimilar in title, and two distinct records can share a title
   pattern (for example a recurring annual dataset report).
4. **The screening cost is a single average.** Screening a record with an
   informative abstract is much faster than one without.
5. **Recall is bounded by the index, not by the query.** A paper absent from every
   source you searched cannot be retrieved by any Boolean string, and this
   limitation is invisible in the query's recall.

## The honest summary

Search is measurable: each query has a precision, a recall and an hour cost, and
the choice between them is a statement about which error you would rather pay
for. De-duplication has a miss rate and a false-merge rate, both estimable. What
the demonstration cannot supply is the coverage of the underlying index — a
number no search protocol can compute from the inside.

## Mastery checkpoint

For a topic you are currently searching:

1. Write the protocol in six bullets, exactly as a reader would need to repeat it.
2. Run one broad and one restrictive query and record the record counts.
3. Estimate the screening hours for the union at your own screening speed.
4. Export the union, de-duplicate at a stated threshold, and report how many
   records the threshold removed and what it merged.
5. Write the yield line: records retrieved → after de-duplication → screened →
   included.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Searching once.** A single query is a sample; protocols iterate and record
   each iteration's yield.
2. **Chasing recall with no screening budget.** A union that retrieves $38.6\
   \mathrm{h}$ of records must be planned for.
3. **De-duplicating by eye.** Manual merging is inconsistent and untraceable;
   automate with a threshold and record the merges.
4. **Citing a record that was never screened.** Records carried from a
   bibliography into a review bypass the inclusion criteria.
5. **Losing the provenance.** Without the query and date fields, a library cannot
   be audited or regenerated.
6. **Treating preprints and published versions as distinct works.** Decide once,
   and apply it consistently (see
   [06-02 Persistent Identifiers](../06-reproducible-publication/02-persistent-identifiers.md)).

## Where this is used

- **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses:** the protocol is the method, and the
  yield accounting is reported in the paper.
- **A thesis literature chapter:** the protocol makes the chapter defensible
  against "you missed X".
- **Grant applications:** a documented competition scan is stronger than a list.
- **Reference managers in practice:** deduplication and provenance are the
  features that matter; see
  [22-03-06 Metadata Standards](../../22-data-engineering-reproducibility/03-data-formats-and-engineering/06-metadata-standards.md).
