# Anatomy of a Research Paper

A research paper is not a report of everything you did. It is a **structured
argument** whose sections carry different epistemic jobs, and reading it well
means knowing which job each section is doing. This lesson maps that structure
and, more usefully, measures it: how long the sections actually are, where the
citations sit, and what the shape tells you before you have understood a single
equation.

The organising idea is the **hourglass**. A paper begins wide (the problem
matters because of a broad context), narrows to a specific question, method and
result, and then widens again to consequences. The Introduction and Discussion
are the wide ends; Methods and Results are the narrow waist. Each half of the
hourglass licenses a different kind of sentence, and the most common reading
failure is accepting a wide claim on the strength of narrow evidence.

> **Data provenance.** All models and numbers in this lesson are **SYNTHETIC**.
> The corpus described below is generated by the accompanying demonstration; no
> published paper was measured and no bibliometric figure is quoted from a
> real survey. Chosen teaching parameters are always labelled as such.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Name** the sections of an IMRaD paper and **state** the job each section
   performs in the argument.
2. **Compute** sectional word budgets and reference densities for a paper, and
   **compare** them with the corpus distribution measured by the demonstration.
3. **Explain** the hourglass structure and **identify** the sentences in a paper
   that are licensed by the evidence and those that are not.
4. **Locate** a paper's claim in the Introduction–Methods–Results–Discussion
   chain, and **predict** where a specific figure or number will be found.
5. **Distinguish** the abstract, the extended abstract, the conference paper and
   the letter as different containers for the same argument.
6. **Diagnose** an unfinished paper from its internal proportions, for example a
   Discussion shorter than the Introduction.

## 1. Why the structure is standard

The four-part Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion (IMRaD) arrangement is
a nineteenth-century convention for reporting *experimental* results that became
near-universal in the twentieth century. Its persistence has three reasons:

1. **It separates what was known from what was done from what was found.** A
   reader can enter at any section and knows what kind of statement to expect.
2. **It assigns responsibility.** The Methods section is a promise: given these
   materials and procedures, anyone should obtain these results. The Results
   section is the evidence for that promise.
3. **It makes rejection cheap.** A reviewer can find the novelty claim
   (Introduction), the evidence (Results) and the generalisation (Discussion)
   without reconstructing the whole narrative.

The convention is a container, not a law. Many geophysics papers use variants:
a combined "Results and Discussion" section, a "Data and Methods" merge, a
"Theory" section inserted between Introduction and Methods, or a
"Geological setting" section that sits before the Methods. The variants are
legitimate; what matters is that each block still has a stated job.

## 2. The five functional blocks

| Block | Question it answers | Typical licence |
|---|---|---|
| Title and abstract | What did you find, and why does it matter? | Tightest: one claim, no hedging about scope |
| Introduction | Why is this question worth asking, and what is already known? | Broad; cites prior work heavily |
| Methods | What exactly did you do, and can it be repeated? | Narrow; past tense; no interpretation |
| Results | What did you observe, with what uncertainty? | Narrow; no causal language beyond design |
| Discussion and Conclusions | What does it mean, and what would falsify it? | Broad again; must mark speculation as speculation |

A useful reading rule: **interpretation belongs to the Discussion and nowhere
else.** When you find an interpretive sentence in Results, it is either a sign
of loose drafting or a sign that the author is smuggling a claim past the
evidence.

## 3. Worked example: measuring the shape

The demonstration generates 400 manuscripts with a lognormal length
distribution centred on $6200$ words and a Dirichlet draw for the sectional
budget. The printed results are the ones quoted here.

**Length and references.** Mean manuscript length $6387 \pm 2169$ words
(mean $\pm$ one standard deviation across papers), median $6123$ words, so the
mean exceeds the median by $4.3\%$: the length distribution is right-skewed.
The mean reference count is $44.8 \pm 15.7$ references, a density of
$7.02$ references per 1000 words. The correlation between length and reference
count is $r = +0.966$, which is close to 1 only because the generator tied them;
in a real corpus the correlation is much weaker and is worth checking, because a
paper that is long *and* sparsely cited is usually padding.

**Sectional budget.** The IMRaD body is $4982 \pm 1692$ words, distributed as

$$N_{\text{Intro}} = 985,\quad N_{\text{Meth}} = 1487,\quad
N_{\text{Res}} = 1177,\quad N_{\text{Disc}} = 1333 \ \text{words},$$

that is $20.1\%$, $29.6\%$, $24.1\%$ and $26.2\%$ of the body respectively. The
Methods share is the largest, which is the signature of a paper written to be
reproduced rather than to be admired. A paper whose Methods share is $8\%$ is
telling you, in its proportions, that the results cannot be checked.

**Where the citations sit.** Introduction $17.6$ references placed, Methods
$6.8$, Results $6.9$, Discussion $13.5$. As densities, the medians are
$20.35$, $4.81$, $6.21$ and $10.82$ references per 1000 words for
Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion respectively. The Introduction and
Discussion between them hold $69.5\%$ of the citations while holding only
$46.5\%$ of the body words. Citations cluster where the argument is made, not
where the data are described: the Methods section cites the technique once and
then stops.

**The hourglass, measured.** Discussion minus Introduction is $+349 \pm 1091$
words, and $62.7\%$ of the papers have a Discussion longer than their
Introduction. The remaining $37.3\%$ are not necessarily defective — a
letter-length paper may stop at the result — but a full-length paper in that
group has usually finished before saying what changed.

## 4. What each section lets you check

Reading a paper is cheaper if you check each section against the thing it can
actually support.

- **Title.** Look for the object, the method and the scope. "Seismic velocity
  structure beneath the northern Andes from ambient-noise tomography" names all
  three. "Insights into crustal dynamics" names none, and you will have to read
  the abstract to learn what was measured.
- **Abstract.** Look for a number. An abstract with a stated result and a stated
  uncertainty is a different artefact from one that promises a result.
- **Introduction.** Look for the gap sentence — the sentence that says what is
  not yet known. In the genre-analysis tradition this is the "niche" move of the
  Create A Research Space model (Swales, *Genre Analysis*, Cambridge University
  Press, 1990). If you cannot find it, the novelty claim is missing.
- **Methods.** Look for the parameters. Section
  [03 Reproducibility](../03-research-methods-and-rigour/04-reproducible-research-practice.md)
  turns "are the parameters there?" into a countable audit.
- **Results.** Look for the uncertainty attached to every number. A result
  without an error bar is a point estimate, not a measurement.
- **Discussion.** Look for the falsification statement — what observation would
  have contradicted the conclusion.

## 5. Containers: the same argument in different sizes

| Container | Typical length | What it can carry |
|---|---|---|
| Research paper | 4000–8000 words | One complete argument with data |
| Letter or rapid communication | 1500–3000 words | One result, minimal background |
| Conference paper | 2–8 pages | Preliminary result, often superseded |
| Extended abstract | 1–2 pages | A promise with one figure |
| Review article | 8000–20000 words | A map of a field, no new data |
| Preprint | as the paper | Identical content, before peer review |

The container constrains what can be verified. A one-page extended abstract
cannot carry the parameter list that makes a result reproducible, and it should
not be cited as if it did.

## Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **The IMRaD shape is a convention, not a physical law.** It is closest to
   observational and experimental work; theory papers, review articles,
   case studies and data descriptors have their own shapes.
2. **The measured proportions are synthetic.** They describe the generator's
   parameters. Real sectional budgets vary strongly by journal, article type and
   discipline, and the numbers here should never be quoted as journal norms.
3. **The citation model assigns citations to sections, not to claims.** It
   measures where citations are placed, not whether the cited work supports the
   citing sentence. That is a reading judgement, not a counting one.
4. **The length distribution is lognormal by construction.** Real manuscript
   lengths are right-skewed, but the particular parameters used here are chosen.
5. **A departure from the shape is a signal, not a verdict.** Unusual proportions
   are a reason to look more carefully, not evidence of a defect.

## The honest summary

A paper's structure is checkable and its proportions are informative. The
sections do different jobs, the citations concentrate where the argument is
made, and the hourglass predicts which sentences the evidence licenses. What
counting cannot tell you is whether the argument is *true*: the demonstration
measures where citations are placed, never whether they support the clause they
sit in. That step requires reading the cited work, and it is the subject of
[03 Critiquing Methods, Data and Inference](03-critiquing-methods.md).

## Mastery checkpoint

Without notes, take any paper in your field and, in under fifteen minutes:

1. Write the claim of the paper in one sentence with its scope (region, depth,
   period, magnitude).
2. Mark where in the structure that claim is *earned*, and where it is merely
   *asserted*.
3. Count the references in the Introduction and in the Methods; compute each as
   references per 1000 words.
4. State which single parameter, if unstated, would stop you reproducing the
   result.

If you cannot do (4), the paper is not reproducible for you, and that is a
finding about the paper, not about you.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Reading linearly and stopping at the Results.** The claim lives in the
   Discussion; the caveats are usually in the last paragraph of the Discussion.
2. **Treating the abstract as a summary of the paper.** The abstract is the
   paper's own advertisement; the Results section is the evidence.
3. **Assuming a section heading guarantees its contents.** A "Results and
   Discussion" heading mixes the two licences and needs reading, not skimming.
4. **Counting references as a proxy for quality.** Reference count measures
   engagement with a literature, not the correctness of the engagement.
5. **Reading the Methods as boilerplate.** The Methods section is where a paper
   is falsifiable; it is the highest-value read for a practitioner.
6. **Ignoring the figure captions.** A self-contained caption often carries the
   result, the uncertainty and the units (see
   [06 Figures and Captions](../02-scientific-writing/06-figures-and-captions.md)).

## Where this is used

- **Reading for a literature review:** the structure tells you where to look for
  the gap statement before you invest in the equations.
- **Writing your first paper:** the proportions give you a target budget, so the
  draft is complete before it is polished.
- **Reviewing a manuscript:** the section-by-section check is the fastest route
  to a fair review; see
  [05 Peer Review Practice](../03-research-methods-and-rigour/05-peer-review-practice.md).
- **Preparing a talk:** the hourglass is also the shape of a good 12-minute
  presentation; see
  [04-01 Conference Talks](../04-presentation-and-communication/01-conference-talks.md).
- **Building a reading log:** recording the claim, evidence and caveats in three
  lines is the entry that later becomes your own Introduction.
