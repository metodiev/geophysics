# Critiquing Methods, Data and Inference

A critique is not a summary with adjectives. It is a **checkable audit** of three
separate things: whether the methods can be repeated, whether the data can
support the claim, and whether the inference follows from the result. The three
fail independently, so a paper can be reproducible and wrong, or correct and
irreproducible. This lesson converts the audit into a scoring procedure with
measurable properties — and states plainly what such a score cannot see.

The organising idea is **separation of concerns**. Data quality, method
reporting and inferential validity are different questions with different
evidence. A paper's conclusion can be weakened by any one of them, and a reader
who collapses them into a single impression of "rigour" has no way to say which
one failed or what would fix it.

> **Data provenance.** All numbers are **SYNTHETIC**. The audited corpus,
> per-item reporting rates and the detectability model are generated by the
> accompanying demonstration; the rates describe a plausible literature and are
> chosen teaching values, not measurements of any real journal.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Separate** the three critique axes — reproducibility, data adequacy and
   inferential validity — and **name** the evidence each requires.
2. **Construct** a reporting checklist, **compute** per-item compliance and
   **interpret** the joint compliance of the reproducibility-critical items.
3. **Explain** why the joint rate of several required items is governed by the
   weakest item rather than the average one.
4. **Score** a corpus with a weighted audit and **report** the score with its
   dispersion rather than as a single number.
5. **Distinguish** a defect that leaves a textual trace from one that does not,
   and **state** what additional evidence (data, code) would be needed.
6. **Write** a critique that names which axis failed, with the evidence, and what
   would falsify the reviewer's own judgement.

## 1. Three axes, three kinds of evidence

| Axis | Question | Evidence needed | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reproducibility | Could I redo this? | Parameters, code, data, versions | Result depends on unstated choices |
| Data adequacy | Can these data answer the question? | Coverage, noise, sampling, provenance | Resolution too coarse for the claimed feature |
| Inferential validity | Does the claim follow? | Design, uncertainty, multiplicity, falsifiability | Correlation reported as cause |

Reproducibility is the cheapest axis to check from the text alone, which is
exactly why it is over-weighted in review: it is visible. Inferential validity
is the most expensive and the most consequential, and it is invisible in a
reporting checklist. A critique that reports only the first axis is a
documentation review.

## 2. Turning reporting into a countable audit

Choose $K$ reporting items, each with a binary indicator for each paper, and
measure the per-item rate

$$\hat p_k = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{1}[\text{item } k \text{ reported in paper } i].$$

A weighted score aggregates them:

$$S_i = 100 \times \frac{\sum_k w_k \mathbb{1}[k \in i]}{\sum_k w_k},\qquad
\bar S \pm \operatorname{SD}(S).$$

The dispersion matters. A mean of $65$ with a standard deviation of $16$ means a
third of the corpus sits below $60$: a mean alone would hide them.

## 3. Worked example: the audit on 150 papers

The demonstration audits $150$ synthetic papers against $10$ items with chosen
reporting rates. The measured rates are the sample fractions, so they differ
slightly from the chosen ones — that difference is sampling noise, not error.

**Per-item compliance (measured).** Problem statement $98.0\%$; data provenance
$87.3\%$; data availability $61.3\%$; acquisition parameters $78.7\%$;
processing parameters $69.3\%$; software and version $52.0\%$; code availability
$44.0\%$; uncertainty and error bars $70.7\%$; parameter choices justified
$52.7\%$; limitations discussed $72.0\%$. Mean compliance over all items is
$68.6\% \pm 14.9\%$ between papers.

**The critical subset.** Take four items as reproducibility-critical: data
availability, processing parameters, code availability, and uncertainty. Their
measured rates are $61.3\%$, $69.3\%$, $44.0\%$ and $70.7\%$. The fraction of
papers reporting **all four** is

$$\frac{16}{150} = 10.7\%,$$

whereas multiplying the rates as if the items were independent predicts
$13.7\%$. The joint rate is dominated by the weakest item: raising code
availability from $46\%$ to $90\%$ moves the joint rate from $10.7\%$ to
$28.0\%$ — a factor of $2.6$ — while the average compliance rises by only about
five points. **The binding constraint is the weakest required item**, which is
the practical reason a journal's single mandated item (data statement, say)
changes behaviour more than a long list of encouragements.

**Weighted score.** With weights $1$–$3$ per item as in the demonstration, the
mean audit score is

$$\bar S = 64.8 \pm 16.3 \ \text{points (out of 100)},$$

and $49$ of $150$ papers score below $60$ points ($32.7\%$). Reviewer effort for
the corpus at three reviewers and four hours each is $1800\ \mathrm{h}$, about
$45$ working weeks — the reason reviewer fatigue is a structural feature of the
system, not a moral failing of reviewers.

## 4. What the audit cannot see

The audit measures what is *written*. Suppose a defect of the weakest item's kind
occurs in $35\%$ of papers ($54$ of $150$). A reader can detect it from the text
with probability $0.50$ when the item is reported and $0.15$ when it is not — a
deliberately generous assumption, since a reported parameter can still be wrong.
The demonstration finds

$$\text{visible defects} = 19/54 = 35.2\%,$$

leaving $35$ papers whose defect leaves no textual trace. Those defects are
found only by running the code or the data, if they are found at all. This is a
one-line justification for the practice in
[24-03-04 Reproducible Research Practice](../03-research-methods-and-rigour/04-reproducible-research-practice.md):
the parts of a paper that protect a reader are not the parts that read well.

## 5. Writing the critique

A critique that is usable by an author has five parts, and each is short:

1. **Claim as understood.** Restate the conclusion and its scope in your own
   words. If you cannot, the paper's claim is not yet clear, and that is the
   finding.
2. **Axis and evidence.** Name which of the three axes is at issue and quote the
   sentence, table or omission that establishes it.
3. **Consequence.** State what the weakness *changes*: a number, a conclusion, a
   scope limit. A weakness with no consequence is a preference.
4. **Test.** State what would settle it — a rerun, a larger sample, a stated
   parameter.
5. **Confidence.** State your own uncertainty in the critique, including what you
   did not check.

Part 5 is the one that distinguishes a critique from a verdict, and it is the
one most often omitted.

## Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **The checklist is a proxy, not a measurement of reproducibility.** A paper
   reporting every item may still be irreproducible, and one reporting few may
   be reproducible because the method is standard.
2. **The rates are synthetic.** They place the corpus in a plausible range for
   teaching; they are not claims about any journal, field or decade.
3. **Detection probabilities are modelled.** $0.50$ and $0.15$ are chosen values
   that make the arithmetic of invisibility concrete; real detection depends on
   the reader's expertise and on access to data.
4. **Item independence is an approximation.** Reporting behaviours correlate
   (authors who document one thing document others), which is why the measured
   joint rate ($10.7\%$) falls below the independent prediction ($13.7\%$).
5. **Weighting is a value judgement.** The weights change the score ranking; the
   lesson reports the consequence of the weights rather than defending them.

## The honest summary

A reporting audit is cheap, reproducible and honest about a narrow thing: what
is documented. It shows that joint compliance is bounded by the weakest required
item, and that a third of the modelled defects leave no textual trace at all.
It cannot tell you whether the physics is right. That requires the data, and a
reader who has both the checklist and the data should say which one produced the
judgement.

## Mastery checkpoint

Take one paper you are currently reading and produce, in one page:

1. The claim, its scope and its uncertainty, in your own words.
2. A six-item checklist drawn from the paper's own method, with a tick or a cross
   for each, and the joint compliance.
3. One inferential threat, stated as "this would matter if …", with the
   observation that would settle it.
4. One sentence stating what you did not check and why.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Auditing documentation and calling it a critique.** The checklist is one
   axis of three.
2. **Reporting a mean compliance without its dispersion.** The tail is the
   interesting part.
3. **Treating the joint rate as the product of the marginal rates.** Correlated
   reporting makes the product optimistic by a factor of about $1.3$ here.
4. **Assigning a weakness with no consequence.** "They should have used method
   X" is a preference until you say what changes.
5. **Concluding that an unreported defect is absent.** Absence of a textual trace
   is not absence of the defect.
6. **Critiquing a paper you have not reproduced on a reproducibility axis.** State
   what you did not run.

## Where this is used

- **Peer review:** the three axes structure a referee report; see
  [05 Peer Review Practice](../03-research-methods-and-rigour/05-peer-review-practice.md).
- **Journal clubs:** the audit gives a club a shared instrument instead of a
  shared impression.
- **Writing your own Methods:** the checklist runs in reverse as a submission
  check; see
