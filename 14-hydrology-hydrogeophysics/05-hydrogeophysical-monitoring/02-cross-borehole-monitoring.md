# Cross-Borehole Monitoring and Tracer Tests

**All models and data in this module are SYNTHETIC.** The aquifer, the tracer
injection, the borehole geometry, the electrode levels and the noise level in
this lesson are chosen teaching values, generated by the demonstration in
[`code/cross_borehole_monitoring_demo.py`](./code/cross_borehole_monitoring_demo.py).
No field site, tracer test, borehole or published survey appears anywhere in it.

Surface arrays see the subsurface through a window a few tens of metres wide and
a few metres deep, and their resolution degrades smoothly with depth until it
disappears into a smeared background. Putting electrodes in boreholes changes
the geometry fundamentally: the current and the potential fields are now
generated *inside* the target volume, and the measurements are sensitive to what
lies between the boreholes rather than to what lies near the surface. The price
is that the target volume is fixed by where you could drill, and the acquisition
is intrusive, expensive and irreversible.

Because cross-borehole surveys are rare and expensive, they are almost always
deployed for *monitoring*: a baseline survey, then a sequence of repeat surveys
while a known perturbation — an injection, a pumping test, a tracer — moves
through the volume. The known perturbation is what makes the interpretation
possible. This lesson follows one synthetic tracer from its injection to its
tomographic image, and it keeps two numbers in view throughout: the detection
threshold that defines the tracer's "arrival", and the number of independent
numbers the data actually contain.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Derive** the two-dimensional advection-dispersion solution for an
   instantaneous point injection and **verify** it by mass conservation.
2. **Explain** why the peak of a breakthrough curve arrives earlier than the
   centre-of-mass time, and **quantify** the difference for a given dispersion.
3. **Show** that a "tracer arrival time" is a statement about a detection
   threshold, and **compute** the velocity spread that different thresholds
   produce from the same curve.
4. **Compute** the recovered mass fraction from a truncated breakthrough curve
   and **state** the integration window with the result.
5. **Build** the petrophysical link from tracer concentration to bulk
   conductivity, using a stated ionic-mixing model and Archie's law, and
   **compute** the detection limit in concentration.
6. **Construct** a cross-borehole ray-coverage matrix, **determine** its rank,
   and **state** the dimension of the null space of the inverse problem.
7. **Interpret** a Tikhonov-regularised cross-hole image by its resolution
   trace, its amplitude recovery and its misfit to an alternative truth, and
   **report** the drift level at which a systematic artefact becomes
   comparable with a weak plume.

## 1. Why the geometry matters, and what it costs

A surface array with a maximum spacing $a$ has a depth of investigation of
order $a/2$ at best, and its sensitivity kernel is a broad banana that spreads
laterally as it descends. A cross-borehole array with electrodes at depths
$z_1$ and $z_2$ in two boreholes separated by $L$ produces a sensitivity that
peaks between the electrodes and is confined to the interval. Three consequences
are worth stating before any mathematics:

- **Resolution is concentrated, not uniform.** The best-resolved part of the
  volume is the region crossed by many ray paths; the corners and the volume
  outside the borehole plane are barely constrained.
- **The target volume is a design choice made by a driller.** A plume that
  migrates outside the plane between the boreholes is invisible, and the image
  will not warn you: it will report a smooth anomaly in the region it can see.
- **The survey is destructive and unrepeatable in the sense that matters.**
  Boreholes change the flow field (a preferential path, a mixing zone at the
  wall), and the array cannot be moved to a better place afterwards.

Monitoring across boreholes is therefore used where a *known* experiment is
taking place — a forced-gradient tracer test, an infiltration experiment, a
thermal or saline injection — because the experiment supplies the prior
information that the geometry cannot.

## 2. The tracer model: advection, dispersion and the breakthrough curve

Take a homogeneous aquifer of thickness $b$ with a uniform seepage velocity $v$
in the $x$ direction and a point injection of mass $M$ per metre of aquifer
thickness at the origin at $t = 0$. The two-dimensional advection-dispersion
equation

$$\frac{\partial c}{\partial t} + v\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}
= D_L\frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial x^2} + D_T\frac{\partial^2 c}{\partial z^2},
\qquad D_L = \alpha_L v, \quad D_T = \alpha_T v,$$

has the exact solution

$$c(x,z,t) = \frac{M}{4\pi t\sqrt{D_L D_T}}
\exp\left(-\frac{(x-vt)^2}{4D_L t}\right)
\exp\left(-\frac{z^2}{4D_T t}\right),$$

valid for $t > 0$. That it conserves mass can be checked directly:
$\int\!\int c\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}z = M$ for every $t > 0$, because each
Gaussian factor integrates to $\sqrt{4\pi D t}$ and the prefactor is exactly the
inverse product of the two. In the demonstration this check is performed
numerically as the mass-recovery test of Section 4.

In the demonstration the SYNTHETIC aquifer has $K = 5.0\ \mathrm{m\,d^{-1}}$,
gradient $i = 0.10$, effective porosity $n_e = 0.25$, so

$$v = \frac{Ki}{n_e} = \frac{5.0 \times 0.10}{0.25}
= 2.000\ \mathrm{m\,d^{-1}},$$

with $\alpha_L = 0.50$ m and $\alpha_T = 0.05$ m, hence
$D_L = 1.0000$ and $D_T = 0.1000\ \mathrm{m^2\,d^{-1}}$. The monitoring
borehole is at $x = 5.0$ m, so the centre-of-mass arrival time is
$x/v = 2.500$ d.

### 2.1 The peak is not the centre of mass

The demonstration's breakthrough curve at $x = 5$ m peaks at
$c_{\max} = 0.2224\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ at $t = 2.050$ d — **18% earlier than
$x/v = 2.500$ d**. The reason is in the prefactor: $c \propto t^{-1}$ competing
with the Gaussian decay $\exp(-(x-vt)^2/4D_L t)$. The curve is skewed, and the
mode is always ahead of the mean for a positively skewed breakthrough. A tracer
"arrival time" is therefore not a single physical time; it is a chosen statistic
of a skewed distribution. That is the subject of the next subsection, and it is
the main honest difficulty of tracer hydrology.

### 2.2 Arrival time is a threshold, and the velocity spread is large

Suppose the analyst defines the arrival time as the first time the concentration
exceeds a fraction of the peak. The demonstration computes this for four
thresholds:

| threshold | $c$ (kg m⁻³) | first arrival (d) | implied velocity (m d⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1% | 0.00022 | 0.490 | 10.204 |
| 1.0% | 0.00222 | 0.610 | 8.197 |
| 5.0% | 0.01112 | 0.750 | 6.667 |
| 50.0% | 0.11121 | 1.240 | 4.032 |

The same experiment yields velocities differing by a factor **2.53** depending
on the detection rule alone, and every one of them exceeds the true seepage
velocity of $2.00\ \mathrm{m\,d^{-1}}$ — the last because even the 50% point
sits on the leading edge of a skewed curve. Two conclusions follow, and both are
practical:

1. **A quoted tracer velocity is a statement about a threshold.** The threshold
   must be reported, and comparisons between sites or campaigns are only
   meaningful at a common threshold.
2. **The first arrival is a leading-edge quantity and is dominated by
   heterogeneity.** It is set by the fastest path, not by the mean flow, and in
   a heterogeneous aquifer it will move between campaigns and between injection
   points. The peak or the temporal centroid are more stable; neither is
   unbiased.

## 3. The petrophysical link: concentration to conductivity

A conservative ionic tracer changes the pore-water conductivity approximately
linearly in the concentration, at low concentration:

$$\sigma_w(c) = \sigma_{w,0} + \Lambda c,
\qquad \Lambda = \frac{\Lambda_m}{M_{\text{mol}}},$$

with $\Lambda_m$ the limiting molar conductivity and $M_{\text{mol}}$ the molar
mass of the salt. For NaCl, $\Lambda_m = 0.0126\ \mathrm{S\,m^2\,mol^{-1}}$ and
$M_{\text{mol}} = 0.05844\ \mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}$ (both standard constants), so

$$\Lambda = \frac{0.0126}{0.05844}
= 0.2156\ \mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}\ \text{per}\ (\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}).$$

Archie's law in conductivity form for a saturated medium gives the bulk
conductivity $\sigma_b = \sigma_w\phi^{m}$. With the demonstration's
$\sigma_{w,0} = 0.050\ \mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}$, $\phi = 0.25$ and $m = 1.8$:

| $c$ (kg m⁻³) | $\sigma_w$ (S m⁻¹) | $\rho_w$ ($\Omega$ m) | $\sigma_b$ (S m⁻¹) | change |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00000 | 0.05000 | 20.00 | 0.00412 | — |
| 0.00100 | 0.05022 | 19.91 | 0.00414 | $+0.43\%$ |
| 0.00500 | 0.05108 | 19.58 | 0.00421 | $+2.16\%$ |
| 0.05000 | 0.06078 | 16.45 | 0.00501 | $+21.56\%$ |
| 0.22242 (peak) | 0.09795 | 10.21 | 0.00808 | $+95.91\%$ |

The peak of the breakthrough curve nearly doubles the bulk conductivity, which
is easy to see. A $0.005\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ plume changes it by $2.2\%$,
which is the detection problem. Two structural remarks:

- **The link is multiplicative.** A factor in $\Lambda$, in $\phi^m$ or in the
  background $\sigma_{w,0}$ scales the whole mapping. A wrong background
  conductivity biases every concentration in the same direction, and the time
  lapse does not remove it if the background itself drifted.
- **The link is a model, not a measurement.** The linear mixing law is exact
  only at infinite dilution and for a single salt; a real pore water has several
  ions, and the excess conductivity of the mixture is not the sum of the parts.
  The petrophysically coupled joint inversion of
  [18-07-03](../../18-inverse-problems/07-joint-and-multi-physics-inversion/03-petrophysical-joint-inversion.md)
  exists precisely because this link carries its own uncertainty.

## 4. Mass recovery, and the integration window

Because the model conserves mass, the mass crossing the monitoring plane can be
computed as the flux integral

$$M_{\mathrm{rec}}(t_{\max}) = \int_0^{t_{\max}}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} v\,c(x_m,z,t)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}t .$$

For the demonstration, with $M = 2.0$ kg per metre of aquifer thickness:

| $t_{\max}$ (d) | recovered mass (kg/m) | fraction of $M$ |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 1.1606 | 0.5803 |
| 5 | 1.8399 | 0.9199 |
| 8 | 1.9907 | 0.9953 |
| 15 | 2.0000 | 1.0000 |
| 120 | 2.0000 | 1.0000 |

The recovery approaches $M$ from below, and the deficit for a truncated record
is the slowly moving tail: stopping the integration at 5 d loses 8% of the
tracer. **A reported mass recovery is meaningless without its integration
window**, because the missing mass has no signature that distinguishes it from
a leak, a sorbed fraction or a lost injection. In a real test the interesting
quantity is exactly the deficit, and attributing it requires a stated window,
a stated analytical correction and an uncertainty on both.

## 5. Ray coverage, rank and the null space

The cross-hole geometry is fixed by the boreholes. In the demonstration the
modelled section is $10.0\ \mathrm{m} \times 5.0\ \mathrm{m}$, discretised into
$40 \times 20 = 800$ cells, with 6 electrode levels in each borehole giving
$6 \times 6 = 36$ ray pairs. Each ray is normalised to unit length so that the
datum $d_i$ is the path-averaged change in $\log_{10}\rho$ along ray $i$.

The data kernel $G$ is therefore $36 \times 800$. Its singular values run from
$0.2778$ to $0.108$, and its **rank is 36**: every ray is an independent
linear combination, but there are only 36 of them. The system

$$d = G\,m$$

therefore has a null space of dimension at least $800 - 36 = 764$. This is not
an artefact of a poor implementation or of a bad inversion; it is the geometry.
The consequence is stated most sharply in the language of resolution: the model
resolution matrix $R = (G^{\mathsf T}G + \alpha L^{\mathsf T}L)^{-1}G^{\mathsf T}G$
has

$$\operatorname{tr} R \le 36$$

for every $\alpha$, so at most 36 of the 800 cell values are determined by the
data. The remaining 764 are supplied by whatever regularisation the analyst
chose. **In a cross-hole image, most of the picture is prior.** The honest
response is not to abandon the method — the 36 determined numbers are
information, and they are information about the *interior* — but to report
$\operatorname{tr}R$ and the regularisation weight alongside the image.

## 6. Inversion of a synthetic plume, and what the prior is doing

The demonstration images the plume at $t = 5.000$ d (twice the centre-of-mass
arrival), where the peak concentration is $0.09980\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and the
true peak change in $\log_{10}\rho_b$ is $-0.15544$ dex, i.e. a 30.1% drop in
resistivity. The data are the 36 ray averages plus noise of sd
$0.010$ dex; the true data have rms $0.04489$ dex, so this is a high
signal-to-noise experiment.

| $\alpha$ | data rms (dex) | model rms vs truth | amplitude | correlation | $\operatorname{tr}R$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $10^{-6}$ | 0.00000 | 0.07700 | 1.7634 | 0.5537 | 35.99 |
| $10^{-4}$ | 0.00029 | 0.07574 | 1.7383 | 0.5582 | 35.40 |
| $10^{-2}$ | 0.00626 | 0.04281 | 1.1619 | 0.7078 | 19.41 |
| $10^{-1}$ | 0.00883 | 0.02803 | 0.8551 | 0.7567 | 8.83 |
| $10^{0}$ | 0.01097 | 0.03321 | 0.5791 | 0.5662 | 3.58 |
| $10^{1}$ | 0.01380 | 0.03895 | 0.4294 | 0.3089 | 1.37 |

Read the table honestly.

- **The data do not choose $\alpha$.** Every value from $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-1}$
  fits the data at or below the noise sd. Across that range the recovered
  amplitude changes by a factor of two ($1.76 \to 0.86$). A discrepancy-principle
  choice of the weight is therefore bounded above by a factor of about 100 with
  no data-driven preference within it.
- **$\operatorname{tr}R$ falls from 36 (the rank) to 8.8** as $\alpha$ rises: the
  weight is directly choosing how much of the image is prior.
- **The correlation with the truth peaks at $\alpha = 10^{-1}$ (0.757)**, i.e.
  the "best-looking" image is the one in which four fifths of the information
  has been replaced by smoothness. That is an artefact of a smooth synthetic
  truth, and Section 6.1 shows it.

### 6.1 The prior decides: the same data from a blocky truth

Replace the smooth Gaussian plume with a sharp $1\ \mathrm{m} \times 1\ \mathrm{m}$
box of the same rms ($0.05234$ dex) and invert with the same smoothness penalty:

| $\alpha$ | data rms (dex) | model rms vs box | correlation | amplitude |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $10^{-2}$ | 0.00580 | 0.04833 | 0.4238 | 0.3484 |
| $10^{0}$ | 0.01548 | 0.04901 | 0.3619 | 0.1351 |

The blocky truth fits the same rays just as well (data rms 0.0058 dex against
noise 0.010 dex) and is recovered four times less accurately than the smooth
truth (correlation 0.42 against 0.71, amplitude under a third). **The data do
not contain the difference between the two truths; the regulariser does.** A
cross-hole image should therefore always be accompanied by the answer to the
question "what would a different, equally admissible truth have looked like, and
would I have recognised it?"

### 6.2 What the data do and do not determine

Projecting the truth onto the row space of $G$ separates it into a part the rays
can see and a part they cannot:

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| rms of the truth | 0.05234 dex |
| rms of its projection onto the row space | 0.03552 dex (68%) |
| rms of the invisible (null-space) part | 0.03844 dex |
| correlation of the row-space part with the truth | 0.4244 |

And the inversion at $\alpha = 10^{-2}$ correlates $+0.26$ with the
data-determined part but $+0.58$ with the *invisible* part. The smooth prior
reproduces the part of the truth that no ray samples better than the part the
rays measured. This is the quantitative form of the statement that the image is
largely interpolation, and it is the single most important diagnostic to carry
out on any cross-hole data set before interpreting its shape.

## 7. Detection limits and a drifting background

Two practical numbers close the loop.

- **Detection limit in concentration.** A uniform concentration $c$ shifts every
  ray by $\log_{10}(1 + \Lambda c/\sigma_{w,0})$ dex. Requiring this to exceed
  the noise once and three times gives $c_{\min} = 0.00540$ and
  $0.01659\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ respectively. Anything weaker is not detectable
  by this array at this noise, whatever the inversion appears to show.
- **A drifting background.** Suppose every ray reading drifts systematically
  with electrode depth, peak to peak $0.010$ dex (rms $0.00353$ dex) — the
  signature of an unmonitored seasonal change in the boreholes during the
  monitoring period. The resulting artefact has rms $0.00453$ dex, i.e. **9% of
  the plume's rms amplitude**, with extremes $-2.25\%$ to $+2.52\%$ in
  resistivity. Time-lapse differencing cancels a *stable* background, not a
  drifting one, and averaging repeat surveys does not remove a systematic error:
  the remedy is to measure the drift (permanent electrodes, repeat baselines,
  independent water-level and temperature records), not to hope.

## 8. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Homogeneous, isotropic, steady flow.** The solution above assumes a single
   uniform velocity. Real aquifers are heterogeneous, and heterogeneity
   *increases* apparent dispersivity, so fitted $\alpha_L$ values are
   scale-dependent and must not be extrapolated.
2. **Instantaneous point injection.** A finite-duration injection convolves the
   solution with the injection history; at early times the difference is not
   small.
3. **Conservative tracer.** Sorption adds a retardation factor and decay adds an
   exponential; both change the curve in ways that can mimic dispersion.
4. **A single mobile phase.** Dual-porosity media (fractured rock, aggregated
   soil) produce long tails that no single $\alpha_L$ reproduces, and the fitted
   "dispersivity" becomes a curve-fitting parameter rather than a property.
5. **Straight-ray linearisation.** The cross-hole data are modelled as
   path-averages of $\Delta\log_{10}\rho$. This is valid for small, smooth
   perturbations; for a strong contrast the current paths redistribute, and the
   linear inversion will place the anomaly at the wrong location as well as at
   the wrong amplitude.
6. **A depth-uniform petrophysical link.** $\Lambda$, $\phi$, $m$ and
   $\sigma_{w,0}$ are taken as constants; a stratigraphic change in any of them
   produces a time-lapse anomaly indistinguishable from a plume.
7. **Stable borehole and coupling conditions.** See Section 7.

The regime of best validity is a shallow, granular, single-porosity aquifer with
a short injection of a conservative tracer and a well-logged borehole pair. The
regime of worst validity is a fractured aquifer with a long injection, where the
transport model, the petrophysical model and the linearised tomography are all
wrong at once and in the same direction — towards a smooth, plausible,
over-confident image.

## 9. Worked examples

### Example 1 — the breakthrough curve by hand

Take the demonstration's numbers: $M = 2.0$ kg/m, $D_L = 1.0000$,
$D_T = 0.1000\ \mathrm{m^2\,d^{-1}}$, $x = 5.0$ m, $v = 2.000$ m/d, and consider
the centreline $z = 0$.

At the centre-of-mass time $t = x/v = 2.500$ d the exponential factors are both
1, so

$$c = \frac{2.0}{4\pi(2.5)\sqrt{1.0 \times 0.1}}
= \frac{2.0}{4\pi \times 2.5 \times 0.31623}
= \frac{2.0}{9.9346} = 0.2013\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}.$$

At $t = 2.050$ d the prefactor is larger and the Gaussian is still near 1:

$$c = \frac{2.0}{4\pi(2.05)(0.31623)}
\exp\!\left(-\frac{(5 - 4.10)^2}{4 \times 1.0 \times 2.05}\right)
= 0.24552 \times e^{-0.09878} = 0.22245\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}},$$

which is the demonstration's maximum, $0.2224\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. **The peak
is 18% earlier than $x/v$ and 10% higher than the centre-of-mass concentration**:
the curve is not symmetric and no single time characterises it.

### Example 2 — the velocity a threshold reports

Suppose the analyst calls the arrival time the moment the concentration first
reaches 1% of its peak, $0.00222\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. From the demonstration
that is $t = 0.610$ d, so

$$v_{\text{arr}} = \frac{x}{t} = \frac{5.0}{0.610} = 8.197\ \mathrm{m\,d^{-1}},$$

a factor $8.197/2.000 = 4.1$ above the true seepage velocity. Using the 50%
threshold instead gives $5.0/1.240 = 4.032\ \mathrm{m\,d^{-1}}$, still a factor
2.0 high. **The threshold changes the answer by more than the aquifer
heterogeneity the test was designed to measure**, which is why a tracer test
report should state the threshold, the peak concentration and the sampling
interval, and why the temporal moment (centroid) is preferred when the tail can
be sampled.

### Example 3 — how many numbers are in the data?

A cross-hole survey has $n_b$ electrode levels per borehole in two boreholes.
The number of ray pairs is $n_b^2$, and if the section is discretised into
$n_c$ cells, the rank of the data kernel is at most $\min(n_b^2, n_c)$. For
$n_b = 6$ and $n_c = 40 \times 20 = 800$: $36$ rays, rank $\le 36$, null space
$\ge 764$. Adding two more levels per borehole raises the ray count to $64$ and
the rank to $64$ — a 78% increase in information for a modest increase in
acquisition cost. That arithmetic, and not the resolution of the inversion
grid, is what a survey design trades against.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Quoting a tracer velocity without its threshold.** The demonstration shows
   a factor of 2.5 between two common choices.
2. **Treating the peak as the mean.** Breakthrough curves are skewed; the peak
   is early and the centroid needs a tail you may not have sampled.
3. **Reporting a mass recovery without an integration window.** The deficit is
   the interesting quantity and it has no unique attribution.
4. **Reading a cross-hole image cell by cell.** At most $\operatorname{tr}R$
   values are determined, and for a 36-ray survey that is 36 of 800.
5. **Choosing the smoothness weight to make the image look right.** The
   smooth-truth experiment gives correlation $0.71$ and the blocky one $0.42$ at
   the same weight.
6. **Assuming time-lapse differencing removes all systematics.** A drift of a
   third of the noise level produced an artefact of 9% of the plume amplitude.
7. **Converting a Darcy flux to a seepage velocity with the total porosity.**
   Tracer transport uses the effective porosity, which is smaller, and the error
   propagates directly into every velocity.
