# Time-Lapse ERT for Infiltration and Transport

**All models and data in this module are SYNTHETIC.** The soil, the pore water,
the infiltration depths, the noise level and the inversion weights in this
lesson are chosen teaching values, generated by the demonstration in
[`code/time_lapse_ert_demo.py`](./code/time_lapse_ert_demo.py). No field site,
no published survey and no measured catchment appears anywhere in it.

A time-lapse ERT survey takes the same electrode array back to the same line,
again and again, and subtracts one resistivity image from the next. The
subtraction is what makes the method powerful: everything that does not change —
the layering, the geology, the electrode contact, most of the coupling error —
cancels, and what remains is small enough to see. The subtraction is also what
makes the method dangerous, because the same cancellation that removes the
static background removes the evidence of what the background *was*, and a
difference image cannot tell you whether the change was in the water content,
the water chemistry, the temperature or the electrodes.

This lesson is about that second half. It derives the forward response of a
moving wetting front, measures how large it is against a stated noise level,
and then shows — quantitatively — that the same difference data are produced by
a family of different (saturation, salinity) pairs. The non-uniqueness is not a
footnote. It is the reason a monitoring programme needs a second measurement,
and it is why the interpretation of a time-lapse resistivity image is a
statement about a *petrophysical model* as much as about the subsurface.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Derive** the wetting-front response of a Wenner array over a two-layer
   earth, and **verify** the resulting series against its two analytic limits.
2. **Map** a saturation change to a bulk resistivity with Archie's law, and
   **explain** why the mapping is a model and not a measurement.
3. **Compute** the time-lapse ratio data, and **decide** whether a given change
   is detectable against a stated relative noise level.
4. **Contrast** the depth sensitivity of different electrode spacings, and
   **state** which spacings a monitoring array must include for a given front
   depth.
5. **Quantify** the equivalence between a saturation change and a pore-water
   salinity change, and **name** the independent measurements that break it.
6. **Estimate** the bias introduced by an unmodelled temperature change.
7. **Interpret** a Tikhonov-regularised time-lapse inversion by its resolution
   and correlation structure, and **report** the regularisation weight as part
   of the answer.

## 1. What the measurement actually is

A four-electrode measurement returns a **transfer resistance**

$$R_t = \frac{\Delta V}{I} \qquad (\Omega),$$

and dividing it by a purely geometric factor gives an **apparent resistivity**

$$\rho_a = k\,R_t, \qquad k \ \text{a function of the electrode geometry only.}$$

$\rho_a$ is not the resistivity of anything. It is the resistivity of the
homogeneous half-space that would produce the same transfer resistance, and for
a heterogeneous earth it is a weighted average of the resistivities around the
array. In a time-lapse survey we divide one such average by another:

$$r(a,t) = \frac{\rho_a(a,t)}{\rho_a(a,t_0)},$$

the **time-lapse ratio**. Ratio data are used rather than differences because
the ratio removes a multiplicative component of the coupling error and is
dimensionless: it can be compared between arrays, between campaigns and between
sites.

Two remarks that matter for everything below.

- The ratio is a *single number per spacing per time*. Whatever hydrological
  process we claim to have imaged must be expressible as a change in that
  number, and the sensitivity of each number to the process is different.
- Because we divide, a change in the electrode contact impedance that does not
  scale out, and a change in the pore-water conductivity, enter the ratio
  additively in the logarithm. That is the whole ambiguity of Section 7 in one
  sentence.

## 2. From resistivity to water content: the petrophysical link

Archie's law for an unsaturated clean sand relates the bulk resistivity of the
medium to the pore-water resistivity, the porosity and the saturation:

$$\rho = \rho_w\,\phi^{-m}\,S^{-n},
\qquad S = \frac{\theta}{\phi},$$

with $m$ the cementation exponent, $n$ the saturation exponent and $\theta$ the
volumetric water content. In logarithmic form,

$$\log \rho = \log\rho_w - m\log\phi - n\log S,$$

which shows the structure of the problem: **the log-resistivity is a linear
function of three quantities, and a change in any one of them looks the same to
first order unless an independent measurement separates them.**

In the demonstration the SYNTHETIC soil is $\rho_w = 10\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$,
$\phi = 0.35$, $m = 1.80$, $n = 2.00$. Two states are generated:

| state | $S$ | $\theta$ | $\rho$ ($\Omega$ m) |
|---|---|---|---|
| initial | 0.45 | 0.158 | 326.78 |
| after wetting | 0.85 | 0.297 | 91.59 |

so a wetting from $\theta = 0.158$ to $0.297\ \mathrm{m^3\,m^{-3}}$ multiplies
the bulk resistivity by $0.2803$, a drop of $72.0\%$. Note the size: a change in
water content of about $0.14\ \mathrm{m^3\,m^{-3}}$ produces a resistivity
change of a factor of 3.6. Resistivity is a *sensitive* proxy for water content.
It is not a *unique* one.

## 3. Forward modelling a moving wetting front

Consider the simplest useful idealisation: infiltration from the surface creates
a wetted layer of thickness $h$ with resistivity $\rho_1$ over an unwetted
half-space of resistivity $\rho_2$, and the array is a Wenner array of spacing
$a$ on the surface. The apparent resistivity is then exactly

$$\rho_a = \rho_1\left[1 + 4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} k^n
\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (2nh/a)^2}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{4 + (2nh/a)^2}}\right)\right],
\qquad k = \frac{\rho_2 - \rho_1}{\rho_2 + \rho_1},$$

where $k$ is the reflection coefficient at the wetting front. The series
converges quickly because $|k| < 1$.

### 3.1 Two independent checks on the series

The demonstration checks the series against the two limits that can be computed
by hand, and this is the reason to trust the numbers that follow:

- $a/h \to 0$ (the array samples only the top layer): every bracket tends to
  $0$ and $\rho_a \to \rho_1$. Measured relative error $4.6\times10^{-13}$.
- $a/h \to \infty$ (the array samples the half-space): the bracket tends to
  $1 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac12$ and $\rho_a \to \rho_1(1+k)/(1-k) = \rho_2$.
  Measured relative error $2.1\times10^{-11}$.

The second limit also gives a purely analytic identity to check against,
$(1+k)/(1-k) = \rho_2/\rho_1 = 3.567901$ for the demonstration's pair of
resistivities; the series reproduces it to six significant figures.

### 3.2 The response as a function of front depth and spacing

With $\rho_1 = 91.588$ and $\rho_2 = 326.778\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$ (the
demonstration's wet and dry states) and a uniform baseline profile giving
$\rho_a = 326.78\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$ at every spacing, the time-lapse ratio for
a front at depth $h$ is (rows are the front depth, columns the Wenner spacing):

| $h$ \ $a$ (m) | 0.50 | 1.00 | 2.00 | 4.00 | 8.00 | 16.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.25 | 0.4881 | 0.6776 | 0.8416 | 0.9402 | 0.9818 | 0.9951 |
| 0.50 | 0.3477 | 0.4881 | 0.6776 | 0.8416 | 0.9402 | 0.9818 |
| 1.00 | 0.2934 | 0.3477 | 0.4881 | 0.6776 | 0.8416 | 0.9402 |
| 2.00 | 0.2822 | 0.2934 | 0.3477 | 0.4881 | 0.6776 | 0.8416 |

Read the table the way the physics dictates.

- The **smallest spacing always shows the largest change**, because as
  $a \to 0$ the measurement samples only the wetted top layer and
  $r \to \rho_1/\rho_2 = 0.2803$. It is not that short spacings are "deeper";
  it is that long spacings blur the shallow anomaly with unchanged material.
- **What the shape of the ratio curve carries is the depth of the front.**
  A 0.25 m front and a 2.00 m front give the *same* ratio at different
  spacings (0.4881 at $a = 0.50$ m and at $a = 1.00$ m respectively). A single
  spacing cannot tell them apart; the pattern over spacings can.

## 4. Detection against a stated noise level

The demonstration assumes a $1\%$ relative random error on $\rho_a$ at every
spacing (one standard deviation) — the level a careful time-lapse survey of a
stable array can reach, and a *stated teaching value*, not a field claim. Then
the signal-to-noise of the best single spacing is enormous:

| front depth (m) | best spacing (m) | $\lvert 1-r\rvert$ | detections at 1 sd |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.25 | 0.50 | 0.5119 | 51 |
| 0.50 | 0.50 | 0.6523 | 65 |
| 1.00 | 0.50 | 0.7066 | 71 |
| 2.00 | 0.50 | 0.7178 | 72 |

so a wetting front is easy to detect if the right spacing is present. The
instructive failure is the other end of the array:

| front depth (m) | $r$ at $a = 16$ m | $\lvert 1-r\rvert$ | detectable at 1%? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.25 | 0.9951 | 0.0049 | no |
| 0.50 | 0.9818 | 0.0182 | yes |
| 1.00 | 0.9402 | 0.0598 | yes |
| 2.00 | 0.8416 | 0.1584 | yes |

**A monitoring array that omits short spacings is blind to the shallowest,
most dynamic layer of the soil**, and it is blind silently: the data look
perfectly good, and the inversion will happily report a smooth image with the
near-surface change averaged away. This is why a time-lapse monitoring design
differs from an exploration design: the target is a *change*, its depth is known
approximately from the hydrology, and the spacings are chosen around that depth.

## 5. Assumptions and regime of validity

Every number above rests on the following, and each one is falsifiable:

1. **One-dimensional horizontal layering.** The wetting front is flat and
   infinite. A real infiltration front is neither, and a dipping or funnel-shaped
   front changes both the amplitude and the apparent depth of the anomaly.
2. **Archie's law applies with stated exponents.** The link from $\rho$ to
   $\theta$ is a petrophysical model calibrated for clean, clay-free, granular
   media. Clay adds surface conduction, which makes $\rho$ depend on the
   pore-water conductivity far more weakly than Archie predicts; in a clayey
   soil the same saturation change can produce a tenth of the resistivity
   change.
3. **The pore-water resistivity is constant in time.** This is the assumption
   Section 7 attacks.
4. **Temperature is constant in time.** Section 6 quantifies the error when it
   is not.
5. **Electrode coupling and geometry are stable.** Time-lapse differencing
   removes a stable coupling error; a drift does not cancel, and it enters
   exactly where the geophysical signal lives.
6. **The array is small compared with the lateral uniformity of the soil.**
7. **Frequency dependence is neglected.** At the frequencies of a standard ERT
   system, induced polarisation is small for a clean sand; for clay, sulphide or
   organic-rich soil it is not, and the complex conductivity carries additional
   — sometimes better — information.

The regime in which the method is at its most reliable is a clean sandy profile,
a large water-content change, a stable array, and an independent measurement of
at least one of temperature, salinity or water content. Outside that regime the
method still works as *detection*; what it loses is *quantification*.

## 6. Temperature as a confounder

The conductivity of pore water rises with temperature at roughly $2\%$ per
kelvin for typical groundwater, a *stated teaching value* used in the
demonstration. Three numbers follow from it.

- A $+3\ \mathrm{K}$ warming multiplies $\rho_w$ by
  $(1-0.020)^3 = 0.9412$, i.e. $-5.9\%$.
- The full observed drop of $r = 0.2934$ would require $60.7\ \mathrm{K}$ of
  warming, so a large wetting signal cannot be mistaken for a temperature change.
- But an *unaccounted* $3\ \mathrm{K}$ biases the inferred saturation from
  $S = 0.8500$ to $S = 0.8762$, i.e. $+3.08\%$, or
  $\theta = 0.2975 \to 0.3067\ \mathrm{m^3\,m^{-3}}$.

So temperature is not a threat to the detection of a wetting front; it is a
threat to the *fractional accuracy* of the water content attached to it, at
about the same level as the measurement noise. The conclusion is not a caveat
but a design instruction: a monitoring programme that wants water content to
$0.01\ \mathrm{m^3\,m^{-3}}$ must measure temperature in the same soil, or model
it with a stated seasonal amplitude.

## 7. The equivalence class: saturation or salinity?

Here is the honest difficulty of the whole subject in one calculation. Suppose
the measured time-lapse ratio at a suitable spacing is $r = 0.2934$. From
Archie, every pair $(S,\rho_w)$ satisfying

$$\left(\frac{S}{S_0}\right)^{n}\frac{\rho_w}{\rho_{w,0}} = r$$

reproduces the data exactly. Five members of that family, computed by the
demonstration:

| split $x$ | $\rho_w$ ($\Omega$ m) | $S$ | $\theta$ | interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 10.0000 | 0.831 | 0.291 | all saturation |
| 0.25 | 7.3598 | 0.713 | 0.249 | mostly saturation |
| 0.50 | 5.4166 | 0.611 | 0.214 | equal split |
| 0.75 | 3.9865 | 0.525 | 0.184 | mostly salinity |
| 1.00 | 2.9340 | 0.450 | 0.158 | all salinity |

Every member produces the same bulk resistivity, $95.8766\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$
to four decimals. A wetting front and a salinity front are *the same
measurement*. The water content inferred from the "all saturation" member is
nearly twice the water content of the "all salinity" member, and both are
consistent with the data.

This is not a limitation that better hardware removes. It is a consequence of
the physics: resistivity depends on the *product* of a pore-geometry term and
the pore-fluid conductivity, and a single scalar per cell cannot separate a
product. The remedies are all additional information:

- **Chemistry.** A water sample from a piezometer, or a borehole conductivity
  log, fixes $\rho_w$ at a point and can be interpolated.
- **Mass balance.** If the tracer was injected and its mass is known, the total
  change is constrained even where the local values are not.
- **Another physical property.** Surface NMR measures water content almost
  directly; GPR measures water content through the permittivity; seismic velocity
  responds to saturation through the bulk modulus. Any of these breaks the
  product.
- **A time constraint.** An infiltration front moves downward in a way salt does
  not; a repeated survey over days resolves a front from a plume.

## 8. The inverse problem, and what regularisation does and does not fix

The forward model above maps $(h,\rho_1,\rho_2)$ to six ratios. Inverting it
from three different starting models recovers the truth exactly, with a data rms
of $0.0$ in $\log_{10}$ units, because six data and three parameters make the
problem overdetermined. That is a *validation of the code*, and it is the only
thing it validates: the truth was generated by the same equations the inversion
uses.

Two properties of the same inversion matter more than its accuracy.

**(a) The parameter trade-off.** The correlation matrix of the Jacobian columns
is

$$\begin{pmatrix}
+1.0000 & -1.0000 & -0.2676\\
-1.0000 & +1.0000 & +0.2676\\
-0.2676 & +0.2676 & +1.0000
\end{pmatrix}$$

for the parameter order $(\log_{10}\rho_1, \log_{10}\rho_2, h)$. The
$\rho_1$–$\rho_2$ correlation is $-1.0000$: the data depend on the two
resistivities almost only through their ratio, so a systematic bias in one is
absorbed invisibly by the other. That is the inversion's image of the
equivalence in Section 7.

**(b) Underdetermination in depth.** With only two spacings ($a = 0.5$ and
$2.0$ m) and three parameters, an entire family of thicknesses fits to machine
precision:

| $h$ (m) | best-fit $\rho_1$ | best-fit $\rho_2$ | rms (2 data) | rms (6 data) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.50 | 81.479 | 203.510 | $0$ | 0.102 |
| 0.75 | 88.850 | 244.575 | $0$ | 0.059 |
| 1.00 | 91.588 | 326.778 | $0$ | 0.000 |
| 1.25 | 92.852 | 515.541 | $0$ | 0.077 |
| 1.50 | 93.535 | 1204.369 | $0$ | 0.175 |

(all rms in dex of $\log_{10}\rho_a$; $0.010$ dex is $2.3\%$ in $\rho_a$).
Above $h \approx 1.6$ m the family terminates, because even the two shortest
spacings stop sensing the half-space and $\rho_2$ becomes unbounded. The
six-spacing misfit is what discriminates, and it discriminates depth far more
weakly than resistivity.

**(c) What regularisation costs.** Adding an $L_2$ penalty that pulls the
thickness toward 2 m:

| weight $\alpha$ | $\rho_1$ | $\rho_2$ | $h$ (m) | data rms (dex) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $10^{-4}$ | 91.514 | 326.554 | 0.997 ($-0.3\%$) | $1.7\times10^{-4}$ |
| $10^{-2}$ | 86.759 | 311.912 | 0.842 ($-15.8\%$) | $1.1\times10^{-2}$ |

A weight large enough to change the answer changes it to a *wrong* answer — a
$16\%$ bias in depth — while making the data fit almost imperceptibly worse
($2.6\%$ in $\rho_a$, comparable with the noise). Regularisation is not a
technicality; it is a decision with a cost, and the weight must be reported with
the result or the result is not reproducible.

## 9. Worked examples

### Example 1 — the wetting front, by hand

Using the SYNTHETIC soil of Section 2, a front reaches $h = 1.00$ m and the
Wenner array has $a = 1.00$ m, so $x = 2nh/a = 2n$ and

$$k = \frac{326.778-91.588}{326.778+91.588} = +0.5622,
\qquad
S_{\text{series}} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} k^n
\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+4n^2}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{4+4n^2}}\right).$$

Evaluating term by term:

| $n$ | $k^n$ | bracket | term |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.5622 | $0.44721-0.35355 = 0.093661$ | 0.052656 |
| 2 | 0.31607 | $0.24254-0.22361 = 0.018929$ | 0.005983 |
| 3 | 0.17769 | $0.16440-0.15811 = 0.006285$ | 0.001117 |
| 4 | 0.099900 | $0.12403-0.12127 = 0.002767$ | 0.0002764 |
| 5 | 0.056164 | $0.09950-0.09806 = 0.001445$ | 0.0000812 |
| 6 | 0.031575 | $0.08305-0.08220 = 0.000845$ | 0.0000267 |

Summing through $n = 6$ gives $S_{\text{series}} = 0.060140$, the neglected
tail being about $9\times10^{-6}$. Then

$$\rho_a = 91.588\,(1 + 4 \times 0.060140) = 91.588 \times 1.24056
= 113.62\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m},
\qquad r = \frac{113.62}{326.78} = 0.3477,$$

which is exactly the entry in the $a = 1.00$ m column, $h = 1.00$ m row of the
ratio table and the value the demonstration prints. **Note how slowly the series
converges at $a/h = 1$**: $k = 0.5622$ still gives a term of $2.7\times10^{-5}$
at $n = 6$, so a three-term truncation would already have failed in the fourth
decimal. Always truncate against $k^n$, not against $n$.

### Example 2 — the doubling of the water content that is not there

A survey reports that $r = 0.2934$ at $a = 0.5$ m and interprets it as a wetting
front, using a fixed $\rho_w = 10\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$, obtaining
$S = 0.831$, $\theta = 0.291\ \mathrm{m^3\,m^{-3}}$ against an initial
$\theta = 0.158$. A second survey line, 20 m away, has a borehole conductivity
log showing that the pore-water conductivity in this interval rose by a factor
of $3.4$ between the campaigns (a lateral inflow of higher-salinity water).
Recomputing with $\rho_w = 10/3.4 = 2.94\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$ gives
$\theta = 0.158\ \mathrm{m^3\,m^{-3}}$: **no water-content change at all**. The
entire anomaly is dissolved salt. Both interpretations fit the resistivity data;
only the chemistry excluded one of them.

### Example 3 — designing the array

The hydrology predicts that infiltration raises the water content of the top
0.5 m by about $5\%$ relative, and the study must be able to see it at the 3-sd
level with $1\%$ noise on $\rho_a$. With $n = 2$, a $5\%$ rise in $\theta$ is a
$5\%$ rise in $S$, so $\rho$ falls by a factor $1.05^{-2} = 0.9070$, i.e.
$-9.30\%$: the wet-layer resistivity goes from $326.78$ to
$296.40\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$. The demonstration computes the apparent
resistivity for a 0.5 m layer at two spacings:

| spacing | $\rho_a$ baseline | $\rho_a$ wetted | $r$ | $\lvert 1-r\rvert$ | at 1% noise |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.50 m | 326.78 | 301.86 | 0.9238 | 0.0762 | 7.6 sd |
| 8.00 m | 326.78 | 326.31 | 0.9986 | 0.0014 | 0.1 sd |

The same hydrological change is a 7.6-sd signal at one spacing and
indistinguishable from noise at another. **Detection is a property of the array
and the process together**, and a monitoring design that does not reason from
the expected depth of the change cannot claim to have looked for it. The design
conclusion for this target is to keep a spacing of the order of the layer
thickness (here 0.5 m) and to improve the noise level by repeat stacking, since
the signal scales with the water-content change while the noise does not.

$S_0 = 0.45$, $\phi = 0.35$, $n = 2.00$, $\rho_w = 10\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$; a

## Common pitfalls

1. **Reading a difference image as a water-content map.** The image is a
   resistivity-change map. The water content is one member of an equivalence
   class, and the class must be named.
2. **Choosing the regularisation weight by taste.** The demonstration shows a
   $16\%$ depth bias at $\alpha = 10^{-2}$ with a barely visible data penalty.
   Report $\alpha$, or the inversion is not reproducible.
3. **Designing the array for the target, not the layer.** A missing short
   spacing does not degrade the image gracefully; it removes the shallow signal
   silently.
4. **Assuming the coupling cancels.** Differencing removes a *stable* error. A
   drifting electrode or a seasonal temperature change does not cancel and
   appears as a change in the subsurface.
5. **Forgetting that Archie needs clean granular media.** In clay-rich soil the
   surface conduction dominates and the sensitivity to saturation collapses.
6. **Reporting a time-lapse result without a baseline quality control.** The
   ratio data are only as good as the repeatability of the two surveys.
