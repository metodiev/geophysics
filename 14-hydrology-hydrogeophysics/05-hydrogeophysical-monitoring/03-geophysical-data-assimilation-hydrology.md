# Assimilating Geophysical Data into Hydrological Models

**All models and data in this module are SYNTHETIC.** The soil, the rainfall
series, the petrophysical parameters, the observation error, the systematic
error and the filter configuration in this lesson are chosen teaching values,
generated by the demonstration in
[`code/geophysical_data_assimilation_hydrology_demo.py`](./code/geophysical_data_assimilation_hydrology_demo.py).
No real weather record, soil, catchment, station or data set appears anywhere in
it.

The previous two lessons estimated a state — a water content, a concentration —
from a geophysical measurement. Suppose you manage to do that well, with an
honest error bar. What then? A hydrological forecast is produced by a model,
and the geophysical estimate is worth exactly as much as the model's ability to
absorb it. **Data assimilation** is the discipline that combines them: it
propagates a probability distribution of the state through the forecast model,
compares it with the observation, and returns a distribution that is consistent
with both. When the observation is the water content that an ERT survey
estimated, the comparison is only as good as the *petrophysical operator* that
turned resistivity into water content — and that operator is a model too.

This lesson derives the ensemble Kalman filter on a small two-layer soil-moisture
model, and then spends most of its length on two ways the assimilation can
produce a confident wrong answer: a biased observation operator and a biased
forcing. Both are non-unique to hydrology; both are the reason a monitoring
programme needs measurements that the assimilation does not use.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Write** the data-assimilation problem as Bayes' theorem over a state
   trajectory, and **separate** the forecast model, the observation operator and
   the error models.
2. **Derive** the systematic error a wrong petrophysical exponent introduces into
   an ERT water-content estimate, and **show** that it is multiplicative and
   state-dependent.
3. **Derive** the Kalman gain from the minimum-variance update, and **perform**
   a one-dimensional analysis update by hand.
4. **Explain** how an observation of one layer updates an unobserved layer
   through the ensemble cross-covariance, and **quantify** the improvement.
5. **Diagnose** an inconsistent assimilation with the innovation statistics
   (mean, variance, normalised innovation squared) and with the ratio of ensemble
   spread to actual error.
6. **Measure** the dependence of the state error on the observation interval and
   **state** why the resulting curve, not the technique, sets the survey design.
7. **Demonstrate** that a biased observation operator is transferred into the
   state with a *reduced* spread, and that a biased forcing is hidden by the
   analysis but exposed by the forecast.

## 1. The problem, stated properly

Let $x_k$ be the hydrological state at time $t_k$ (here a vector of water
contents in two soil layers), $M_k$ the forecast model that advances the state,
$\mathcal{H}$ the observation operator that predicts what a measurement would
read, and $y_k$ the measurement. The full probabilistic statement is

$$p(x_{0:K} \mid y_{1:K}) \propto
p(x_0)\prod_{k=1}^{K} p(x_k \mid x_{k-1})\;p(y_k \mid x_k),$$

that is: a prior on the initial state, a sequence of model transitions, and a
sequence of likelihoods. Everything practical follows from three choices, and
each carries its own error:

| ingredient | here | its error type |
|---|---|---|
| forecast model $p(x_k\mid x_{k-1})$ | a two-layer soil-moisture bucket | structural + parameter (epistemic) |
| observation operator $\mathcal{H}$ | Archie inversion of an ERT-derived resistivity | petrophysical (epistemic) |
| error models | Gaussian observation noise, multiplicative forcing noise | aleatory, *assumed* |

The crucial asymmetry is that a Kalman-type filter is designed to handle the
third row. It propagates and updates means and covariances, and it is exact for
linear-Gaussian systems. It has no mechanism to detect or correct an error in
the first two rows, and when such an error exists it will typically absorb it
into the state estimate and *reduce* the reported uncertainty while doing so.
Sections 6 and 7 measure exactly that.

## 2. The observation operator, and the error that is built into it

An ERT survey does not measure water content. It measures a resistivity
distribution, and the water content is obtained by inverting a petrophysical
relation. Take Archie's law,

$$\frac{\rho}{\rho_w} = \phi^{-m}S^{-n}, \qquad S = \frac{\theta}{\phi},$$

and suppose the analyst uses $m_{\text{asm}} = 1.60$ while the true exponent is
$m_{\text{true}} = 1.80$ (with $\phi = 0.40$, $n = 2.00$ as SYNTHETIC values).
The observed ratio $\rho/\rho_w$ is enlarged by $\phi^{-m_{\text{true}}}$, but
inverting with $m_{\text{asm}}$ attributes the whole of it to saturation:

$$S_{\text{obs}}
= \left(\frac{\rho}{\rho_w}\,\phi^{\,m_{\text{asm}}}\right)^{-1/n}
= S\,\phi^{\,(m_{\text{true}}-m_{\text{asm}})/n}
= S\,\phi^{0.10}.$$

For $\phi = 0.40$, $\phi^{0.10} = 0.9124$, so

$$\theta_{\text{obs}} = 0.9124\,\theta,
\qquad \text{bias} = -0.0876\,\theta .$$

Two properties of this error deserve emphasis, and the demonstration tabulates
both:

| $\theta$ (m³ m⁻³) | $S$ | inferred $S$ | bias (m³ m⁻³) | bias (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.080 | 0.200 | 0.1825 | $-0.0070$ | $-8.76$ |
| 0.140 | 0.350 | 0.3194 | $-0.0123$ | $-8.76$ |
| 0.240 | 0.600 | 0.5475 | $-0.0210$ | $-8.76$ |
| 0.320 | 0.800 | 0.7300 | $-0.0280$ | $-8.76$ |

- **It is multiplicative.** The bias is $-8.76\%$ of the true water content at
  every state, so it is $-0.0070$ when the soil is dry and $-0.0280$ when it is
  wet.
- **It is therefore between 0.7 and 2.8 times the measurement noise sd** of
  $0.010\ \mathrm{m^3\,m^{-3}}$ across the modelled range. A constant error model
  for this observation would be wrong by construction: the operator is more
  wrong exactly when it matters most, in the wet season.

This is the general situation, not a peculiarity of one exponent. Every
geophysical proxy is a petrophysical operator, and every petrophysical operator
has parameters that are calibrated, assumed, or borrowed from a different site.
The systematic part of their error is an *epistemic* quantity, which is why no
amount of filtering removes it.

## 3. The forecast model and the synthetic truth

The forecast model in the demonstration is deliberately minimal — a two-layer
bucket of thicknesses $D_1 = 0.20$ m and $D_2 = 0.60$ m, porosity $\phi = 0.40$,
residual content $\theta_r = 0.05$, saturated conductivity
$K_s = 0.30\ \mathrm{m\,d^{-1}}$ and a conductivity exponent $\beta = 6$:

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(D_1\theta_1\right) = P - \mathrm{ET}_1 - q_{12},
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(D_2\theta_2\right) = q_{12} - \mathrm{ET}_2 - q_{\text{out}},$$

with the unsaturated conductivity $K(\theta) = K_s\left[(\theta-\theta_r)/(\phi-\theta_r)\right]^{\beta}$,
a crop coefficient $K_c = 0.85$ applied to the potential evaporation in layer 1
and a fraction $0.30$ of it taken from layer 2. The forcing is a SYNTHETIC
150-day series: 396.0 mm of rain in 40 wet days, a maximum daily fall of
39.0 mm, and 509.6 mm of potential evaporation (mean
$3.40\ \mathrm{mm\,d^{-1}}$).

The truth run gives $\theta_1$ between 0.080 and 0.358 (mean 0.140) and
$\theta_2$ between 0.144 and 0.295 (mean 0.178) in $\mathrm{m^3\,m^{-3}}$. These
are **model states, not measurements of any soil**: they exist so that the
filter's error can be computed exactly, which is the only way to demonstrate an
assimilation method honestly.

## 4. The ensemble Kalman filter

For a state $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with forecast mean $\bar{x}^f$ and covariance
$P^f$, and an observation $y \in \mathbb{R}^m$ with operator $H$ and error
covariance $R$, the minimum-variance linear update is

$$x^a = x^f + K\left(y - H x^f\right),
\qquad
K = P^f H^{\mathsf T}\left(H P^f H^{\mathsf T} + R\right)^{-1},$$

$$P^a = \left(I - KH\right)P^f .$$

The **innovation** $d = y - Hx^f$ is where all the information is: its mean tests
the bias of the operator and the model, and its variance tests whether the
assumed error covariances are the right size. The ensemble Kalman filter
estimates $P^f$ from $N = 200$ sample members, each advanced with its own
perturbed parameters (conductivity lognormal with sd of $\ln K_s = 0.30$) and
its own perturbed rainfall (multiplicative, sd 0.25). Observations are
assimilated with perturbed-observation noise, every 2 days, which gives 75
updates in 150 days.

The filter is told the observation noise sd ($0.010\ \mathrm{m^3\,m^{-3}}$) and
is **not** told about any bias. That is the whole point.

### 4.1 The open loop

Running the model with $K_s$ 35% high and rainfall 2% high, and never
assimilating, gives

| variable | open-loop RMSE (m³ m⁻³) | as % of the mean state |
|---|---|---|
| $\theta_1$ | 0.01858 | 13.3% |
| $\theta_2$ | 0.00643 | 3.6% |

This is the number to beat, and it is produced by *stated* parameter errors, not
by noise.

### 4.2 The analysis with unbiased observations

Assimilating the petrophysical operator applied to the truth, with the correct
noise level:

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| analysis RMSE $\theta_1$ | 0.00589 |
| analysis RMSE $\theta_2$ | 0.00261 |
| improvement $\theta_1$ | 3.15× |
| improvement $\theta_2$ | 2.47× |
| innovation mean | $-0.00002\ \mathrm{m^3\,m^{-3}}$ |
| innovation sd | 0.01227 |
| mean normalised innovation squared $\chi^2$ | 0.741 |
| ensemble spread / actual rms error | 1.883 |

Three things to notice.

- **The unobserved layer improves by a factor of 2.5.** Only $\theta_1$ is
  observed; $\theta_2$ is corrected through the ensemble cross-covariance, which
  exists because the model couples the layers. That coupling is the entire
  mechanism, and it is a *model* statement: if the percolation term were wrong,
  the cross-covariance would be wrong in proportion and the apparent success in
  $\theta_2$ would be spurious.
- **The innovation mean is essentially zero**, as it must be for an unbiased
  operator — this is the diagnostic that will fail in Section 6.
- **The ensemble is over-dispersed**: the spread is 1.88 times the actual error,
  and $\chi^2 = 0.741 < 1$. The parameter perturbations are wider than reality,
  which makes the filter conservative (it trusts the data slightly too little).
  An over-dispersed ensemble is a *safe* error, and it is worth reporting:
  filtering consists of choosing error models, and the check above is how you
  know you chose them badly.

## 5. How often does the geophysics have to be repeated?

| interval (d) | updates | analysis RMSE $\theta_1$ | analysis RMSE $\theta_2$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 150 | 0.00475 | 0.00319 |
| 2 | 75 | 0.00589 | 0.00261 |
| 5 | 30 | 0.00783 | 0.00244 |
| 10 | 15 | 0.00710 | 0.00277 |
| 25 | 6 | 0.01147 | 0.00384 |

The error falls steeply from 25 days to about 5 days and then flattens: the
marginal value of an extra survey collapses. Most of the total benefit is
obtained with roughly one survey per week, and moving from weekly to daily buys
about 20% more accuracy for a seven-fold increase in cost. **The survey interval
is an economic decision informed by this curve, not a technical optimum**, and
the curve must be recomputed for the specific model, sensor and error model —
it is not a universal constant of hydrogeophysics.

Note the salt in the table: $\theta_2$ is *best* at a 5-day interval, not at 1
day, because the observation noise is partly absorbed by the ensemble in a way
that trades off against the model's own information. The differences are small,
but the lesson is that "more data" is not a monotone guarantee when the error
models are imperfectly known.

## 6. A biased observation operator: a confident wrong answer

Now let the analyst use $m_{\text{asm}} = 1.60$ while the truth is generated with
$m_{\text{true}} = 1.80$, so every observation carries the multiplicative bias of
Section 2. The filter is unchanged, and it behaves exactly as designed:

| quantity | unbiased | biased | open loop |
|---|---|---|---|
| analysis RMSE $\theta_1$ | 0.00589 | 0.01298 | 0.01858 |
| analysis RMSE $\theta_2$ | 0.00261 | 0.00748 | 0.00643 |
| mean state error $\theta_1$ | — | $-0.00658$ | — |
| innovation mean | $-0.00002$ | $-0.00811$ | — |
| $\chi^2$ | 0.741 | 1.099 | — |
| spread / actual error | 1.883 | 0.785 | — |

Read this carefully, because it is the central result of the lesson.

- The **state error is worse than the open loop** for $\theta_2$ (0.00748 against
  0.00643): the assimilation has made the model estimate *worse* than not
  assimilating, by dragging it towards a biased observation. For $\theta_1$ it
  is still better than the open loop, which is exactly what makes the failure
  dangerous — the analysis does not look broken.
- The **error is systematic**: the mean state error is
  $-0.00658\ \mathrm{m^3\,m^{-3}}$, which is not noise but a signed offset.
- The **ensemble becomes over-confident**: the spread falls from 1.88× the actual
  error to 0.79×, and $\chi^2$ rises to 1.10. The filter reports a *narrower*
  distribution around a *more wrong* value. This is the failure mode that ends
  up in a management report, because the reported uncertainty looks reassuring.
- The **diagnostic that exposes it is the innovation mean** ($-0.00811$ against
  $-0.00002$): a systematically non-zero innovation can only mean that either
  the observations or the model are biased. The $\chi^2$ change is weaker by
  comparison, and the spread ratio alone is ambiguous (over-dispersion is also
  possible from a bad parameter prior).

The remedy is not a better filter but a better operator, or an independent check
of it: a gravimetric or neutron-probe water content at one point, a
time-domain-reflectometry profile, a soil sample. If the bias field is known
(even approximately), estimating *it* — as a state variable in an augmented
filter, or as a calibration parameter — is the principled response, and it
requires the independent measurement.

## 7. A biased forcing: what the analysis hides and the forecast exposes

The asymmetric error model of Section 2 is one kind of structural error. The
other is in the forecast: hold the observation operator correct and instead
make the rainfall forcing 15% too low.

| forcing bias | analysis RMSE $\theta_1$ | 1-day forecast RMSE | 5-day forecast RMSE |
|---|---|---|---|
| none | 0.00589 | 0.00573 | 0.00519 |
| rainfall 15% low | 0.00597 | 0.00878 | 0.01564 |

The **analysis barely notices**: 0.00597 against 0.00589. The filter re-anchors
the state to the observation every two days, so a forcing error has almost no
time to accumulate before it is corrected. But the **forecast** — running the
model on its own from the analysis — degrades from 0.00519 to
0.01564 m³ m⁻³, a factor of 3.0 at five days.

This is the single most important reporting rule in this lesson: **an analysis
error is not a forecast error.** A system that is verified only against
observations will look excellent while it is being continuously corrected by
them, and its structural error will appear only in the forecasts that are
actually used. Any assimilation system must be verified, and reported, on
forecast skill at the lead time of interest.

## 8. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **The forecast model structure is right.** The bucket is a caricature: it
   ignores preferential flow, macropores, root water uptake as a function of
   depth and time, hysteresis, and lateral flow. Section 7 shows what happens to
   the analysis and to the forecast when only a *forcing* is wrong; a wrong
   *structure* is worse and is not diagnosable from the innovations alone.
2. **Observation errors are Gaussian, independent and unbiased.** The
   demonstration deliberately violates the third condition in Section 6.
3. **The ensemble is a fair sample of the prior.** With $N = 200$ members the
   sampling error in a covariance element is of order $1/\sqrt{N} \approx 7\%$,
   and spurious long-range correlations appear in high-dimensional problems.
   Localisation is the standard remedy in real applications.
4. **The observation operator is linear in the state over the increment.** The
   ERT water content is a nonlinear function of resistivity, and the operator
   here is treated as exact apart from its bias.
5. **The petrophysical parameters are constant in space and time.** A
   depth-varying $\phi$ or $m$ produces a state-dependent, spatially structured
   observation error that no scalar bias term captures.
6. **The state is the only uncertain quantity worth estimating.** Model
   parameters ($K_s$, $\beta$) are perturbed but not updated; a coupled
   state–parameter estimation (an augmented state) is often the better answer and
   is correspondingly harder to keep stable.

The regime of best validity is a well-instrumented site with a well-calibrated
petrophysical link, an independent check of that link, and a verification data
set the filter never sees. Outside it, the assimilation is a smoothing
interpolation between geophysical estimates, which may still be useful — but it
should not be reported as a validated predictive system.

## 9. Worked examples

### Example 1 — one update by hand

A single-layer version of the problem, in the scalar notation that makes the
mechanism visible. Suppose the ensemble forecast for $\theta_1$ has mean
$\bar{x}^f = 0.140$ and sd $\sigma_f = 0.020\ \mathrm{m^3\,m^{-3}}$, and an
unbiased observation arrives with value $y = 0.160$ and sd
$\sigma_o = 0.010$. Then

$$K = \frac{\sigma_f^2}{\sigma_f^2 + \sigma_o^2}
= \frac{4.0\times10^{-4}}{4.0\times10^{-4} + 1.0\times10^{-4}} = 0.80,$$

$$x^a = \bar{x}^f + K\left(y - \bar{x}^f\right) = 0.140 + 0.80(0.020)
= 0.156\ \mathrm{m^3\,m^{-3}},$$

$$\sigma_a = \sqrt{1-K}\,\sigma_f = \sqrt{0.20}\times0.020
= 0.0089\ \mathrm{m^3\,m^{-3}}.$$

The update moved the estimate 80% of the way to the observation and reduced the
uncertainty by a factor 2.24. Note that a *more confident* observation moves the
estimate *further* and reduces the uncertainty *more*: the gain is a
precision-weighted compromise, and it is entirely controlled by the assumed
error sizes.

### Example 2 — updating an unobserved layer

Keep the same layer-1 ensemble but add layer 2, with sd
$\sigma_2 = 0.010$ and a sample correlation between the two layers of
$\rho = 0.70$. The covariance is
$0.70 \times 0.020 \times 0.010 = 1.4\times10^{-4}$, so

$$K_2 = \frac{\operatorname{cov}(\theta_2,\theta_1)}{\sigma_f^2 + \sigma_o^2}
= \frac{1.4\times10^{-4}}{5.0\times10^{-4}} = 0.28,$$

and the layer-2 estimate moves by $K_2 \times 0.020 = 0.0056\ \mathrm{m^3\,m^{-3}}$
— 28% of the innovation, even though no measurement touched layer 2. Everything
about this correction is a *model* statement: it rests on the cross-covariance
that the ensemble produced, which in turn rests on the percolation term of the
bucket. The demonstrated 2.47× improvement in $\theta_2$ is this mechanism at
work over 75 updates.

### Example 3 — the diagnostic arithmetic

In the biased case the innovation mean is $-0.00811\ \mathrm{m^3\,m^{-3}}$ over
75 updates. The standard error of a mean is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, so with an
innovation sd of about 0.012 the standard error is
$0.012/\sqrt{75} = 0.00139$. The observed mean is therefore
$-0.00811/0.00139 = -5.8$ standard errors from zero: an unambiguous detection of
bias, in a data set in which every individual innovation looks acceptable. **This
is why the innovation mean, and not a plot of residuals, is the diagnostic to
compute automatically.**

## Common pitfalls

1. **Treating the petrophysical operator as exact.** A plausible exponent error
   gave a $-8.8\%$ multiplicative bias, worse than the observation noise when
   the soil is wet.
2. **Reading a small reported uncertainty as accuracy.** The biased filter
   reported a *narrower* distribution around a *more wrong* state.
3. **Verifying on observations only.** The analysis error barely moved under a
   15% rainfall bias; the 5-day forecast error tripled.
4. **Assuming more observations always help.** At a 5-day interval the unobserved
   layer was slightly better than at 1 day.
5. **Ignoring the cross-covariance's provenance.** The mechanism that improves an
   unobserved layer is a model statement, and a wrong model makes it a spurious
   improvement.
6. **Tuning the error covariances until the answer looks plausible.** The
   spread-to-error ratio and $\chi^2$ are the checks; tuning without them is
   fitting.
7. **Reporting an assimilation as a validated predictive system.** It is a
   combination of two fallible models. Say which one is doing the work.
