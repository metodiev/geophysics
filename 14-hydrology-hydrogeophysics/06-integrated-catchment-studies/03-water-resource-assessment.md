# Water Resource Assessment and Sustainable Yield

**All models and data in this module are SYNTHETIC.** There is no real aquifer,
well, stream, rainfall record or water-level record in this lesson. The
water-table series is generated by the model below, the chloride concentrations
are chosen teaching values, and every other number is a chosen parameter or a
consequence of them. All numbers are printed by
[`code/water_resource_assessment_demo.py`](./code/water_resource_assessment_demo.py).
No site is named and no published measurement is quoted.

A water-resource assessment answers a question that is partly physical and
partly not. The physical part is: how much water arrives, where it is stored, and
what happens to it when it is pumped. The non-physical part is: *how much
change to the rest of the system is acceptable, over what time, and to whom*. The
second question cannot be answered by geophysics, hydrology or any combination of
them, and pretending otherwise is the commonest failure of the field. What the
science can do is produce the quantity that the decision is made against — the
stream depletion caused by a given pumping rate, with its uncertainty — and that
is the deliverable of this lesson.

The lesson therefore works in two registers. First, the four physical estimates:
recharge by water-table fluctuation, recharge by chloride mass balance, the
capture of streamflow by a well, and the storage that all of them depend on.
Second, and at least as important, the propagation of their uncertainty and the
translation of the result into a decision with a named receptor, a named time
horizon and a named tolerance. The two recharge methods disagree by a factor of
2.6 in the demonstration, at the same site and from the same data; the honest
response is neither to average them nor to choose, but to report both and say
what each assumes.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Derive** recharge from a water-table fluctuation record, and **quantify**
   both its sensitivity to the assumed specific yield and its dependence on the
   definition of the rise window.
2. **Explain** that the uncorrected water-table fluctuation estimate is biased by
   the drainage that continues during the rise, and **sign** and size that bias.
3. **Derive** recharge from a chloride mass balance and **compute** the specific
   yield that would be needed to reconcile it with the fluctuation method,
   recognising the circularity of doing so.
4. **Derive** the Glover image-well solution for stream depletion and **compute**
   the fraction of pumpage captured from a stream as a function of time.
5. **Distinguish** capture from storage depletion and **compute** the cumulative
   volume taken from each.
6. **Propagate** uncertainties in transmissivity, storativity, distance and
   specific yield through a Monte Carlo and **report** the result as a
   distribution rather than a number.
7. **Formulate** a sustainable yield as a decision with a receptor, a horizon and
   a tolerance, and **show** why a recharge-limited and a capture-limited yield
   answer different questions.

## 1. What is being estimated, and against what

Three distinct quantities are routinely called "the yield", and conflating them
is the source of most of the disagreement in the literature:

| quantity | definition | what limits it |
|---|---|---|
| recharge-limited | the long-term average recharge over the assessment area | the water balance; mining storage if exceeded |
| capture-limited | the pumping rate whose induced change in some *other* discharge stays tolerable | the receptor, the horizon, the tolerance |
| economically limited | the rate that can be sustained at acceptable cost and water quality | hydraulics, chemistry, economics |

The demonstration computes the first two and shows that in its idealised case they
differ by a factor of 6.3. A sustainable yield is a *decision* about the
second and third rows; the first row informs it. Every defensible statement of a
sustainable yield therefore names its receptor (a stream, a wetland, a
neighbouring borehole, a saline interface), its horizon (one year, ten years,
one generation) and its tolerance (a percentage of a low-flow discharge, a
metres-of-head criterion, a water-quality limit). A yield without those three is
not a result; it is a number.

## 2. Recharge by water-table fluctuation

### 2.1 The method and its assumption

In an unconfined aquifer, recharge raises the water table. If the water table has
moved by $\Delta h$ and nothing else has happened, the water added per unit area
is

$$R = S_y\,\Delta h,$$

with $S_y$ the specific yield. The demonstration generates a SYNTHETIC record
from a stated water balance,

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{r(t) - k_d\,z}{S_y},
\qquad k_d = 0.15\ \mathrm{mm\,d^{-1}}\ \text{per m},$$

where $z$ is the water-table elevation above a fixed base level, $r(t)$ is
recharge in mm/d, applied in a 60-day wet season, and $k_d z$ is the drainage.
The true annual recharge totals over four years are 94.0, 99.6, 93.9 and
74.8 mm/a (mean **90.6 mm/a**), the true $S_y$ is **0.10**, and the water level is
recorded daily with a noise sd of 0.02 m.

Note the first honest complication: the annual minimum drifts by $-0.128$ m over
the four years, so the record is **not stationary**. The method's assumption — no
net change over the record — is not satisfied, and the analyst must either
detrend or report the bias.

### 2.2 The window dependence

The method needs a "rise", and a rise is defined by two dates. The demonstration
computes it over six windows:

| window (d) | rise (m) | $R$ with $S_y=0.05$ | $S_y=0.10$ | $S_y=0.15$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–365 | 0.8398 | 42.0 | 84.0 | 126.0 |
| 365–730 | 0.8514 | 42.6 | 85.1 | 127.7 |
| 730–1095 | 0.8444 | 42.2 | 84.4 | 126.7 |
| 1095–1460 | 0.6771 | 33.9 | 67.7 | 101.6 |
| 60–425 | 0.8944 | 44.7 | 89.4 | 134.2 |
| 30–395 | 0.8451 | 42.3 | 84.5 | 126.8 |

(all $R$ in mm/a). The measured rise is not a property of the aquifer alone: it
changes from 0.677 to 0.894 m — a factor **1.32** — with the window definition.
The physical reason is that a window that clips the drainage season or spans a
low-recharge year measures a different part of the cycle.

### 2.3 The specific yield, and the method's own bias

With the true $S_y = 0.10$ the water-year windows give
$R = 85.1\ \mathrm{mm\,a^{-1}}$ against an imposed mean of
$90.6\ \mathrm{mm\,a^{-1}}$: a bias of $-6.0\%$. The cause is physical and
systematic — **drainage continues during the rise**, so the water table rises by
less than $r\Delta t/S_y$, and the uncorrected method attributes the whole rise to
recharge. The bias has the sign of the drainage and shrinks as the rise becomes
faster relative to the drainage time constant.

Now the dominant issue. Changing $S_y$:

| assumed $S_y$ | $R$ (mm/a) |
|---|---|
| 0.05 | 42.6 |
| 0.10 | 85.1 |
| 0.15 | 127.7 |

A 50% error in $S_y$ is a 50% error in the answer, **because the method is a
multiplication**. The specific yield is the parameter the geophysics of this
folder is best placed to constrain (through water content, through NMR, through
the water balance of a pumping test) and it is the parameter the method cannot do
without. Without an independent estimate of $S_y$, the fluctuation method is not
a measurement of recharge; it is a restatement of an assumption. And then the
method's own $-6\%$ drainage bias cannot even be seen.

## 3. Recharge by chloride mass balance

The other standard method uses an environmental tracer whose atmospheric input
is known and whose behaviour in the subsurface is conservative. For chloride, in
steady state with no internal sources or sinks,

$$R = P\,\frac{\mathrm{Cl}_{\text{dep}}}{\mathrm{Cl}_{\text{gw}}}.$$

The reasoning is a mass balance: the chloride falling on the catchment in rain
must leave in recharge; evapotranspiration removes water but not chloride, so the
concentration in recharge is enriched with respect to deposition by exactly the
factor $P/R$.

The demonstration uses SYNTHETIC teaching values: $P = 800$ mm/a,
$\mathrm{Cl}_{\text{dep}} = 0.5$ mg/L and $\mathrm{Cl}_{\text{gw}} = 12.0$ mg/L:

$$R = 800 \times \frac{0.5}{12.0} = 33.3\ \mathrm{mm\,a^{-1}}.$$

**These concentrations are chosen teaching values, not measurements.** Real
atmospheric chloride deposition varies by more than an order of magnitude with
distance from the sea and with climate, and real groundwater chloride is affected
by rock weathering, fertiliser, road salt and mixing.

### 3.1 The disagreement, and the circular argument that hides it

The two methods, on the same synthetic aquifer:

| method | $R$ (mm/a) | ratio to the imposed truth (90.6) |
|---|---|---|
| water-table fluctuation, true $S_y$ | 85.1 | 0.94 |
| chloride mass balance | 33.3 | 0.37 |
| ratio of the two | **2.55** | — |

The fluctuation method is nearly unbiased; the chloride method is low by a factor
2.7. Forcing agreement would require $S_y = 0.0391$, i.e. a specific yield less
than half the true value: the analyst would calibrate the specific yield on the
chloride method and then present both as independent. **That is circular, and it
is common.** The correct statement is the pair, plus the reason for the
disagreement:

- The chloride method fails if deposition is spatially variable, if there is a
  rock or fertiliser source of chloride, if the groundwater sample is not
  representative of recharge, or if the system is not in steady state.
- The fluctuation method fails if $S_y$ is wrong, if the window is badly chosen,
  if there is a lateral flow component, or if the water table is pumped.

In a real study, either failure would be diagnosed by additional data: chloride
profiles in the unsaturated zone (giving a piston-flow check), a second
independent tracer (tritium, stable isotopes, CFCs), nested piezometers for the
lateral component, and a pump test for $S_y$.

## 4. Capture: the Glover image-well solution

The second half of the assessment is the *change* caused by pumping. For a
fully penetrating well at a distance $L$ from a straight, fully connected stream,
pumping at a constant rate $Q$, the fraction of the pumpage derived from the
stream after a time $t$ is, from the image-well (Glover) solution,

$$\boxed{\;\frac{Q_{\text{stream}}(t)}{Q}
= \operatorname{erfc}\!\left(L\sqrt{\frac{S}{4Tt}}\right),\;}$$

with $T$ the transmissivity, $S$ the storativity and $\operatorname{erfc}$ the
complementary error function. The reasoning: the stream is a constant-head
boundary, and the drawdown is the difference between the well and an image
injection well of the opposite sign at the reflected position; the flux into the
image well is the flux captured from the stream, and the argument above is the
similarity variable of the diffusion equation with a constant-head boundary.

Its limits are the whole story:

- $t \to 0$: argument $\to\infty$, $\operatorname{erfc} \to 0$: **all pumpage
  comes from storage**, and the stream has not noticed.
- $t \to \infty$: argument $\to 0$, $\operatorname{erfc} \to 1$: **all pumpage
  comes from capture**, and the aquifer is in a new steady state.
- The transition time scales as $L^2S/T$, so a well twice as far from the stream
  takes four times as long to affect it — a fact that has decided many well
  fields.

For the demonstration's SYNTHETIC parameters ($T = 200\ \mathrm{m^2\,d^{-1}}$,
$S = 0.10$, $L = 1500$ m, $Q = 1000\ \mathrm{m^3\,d^{-1}}$):

| time | $Q_{\text{stream}}/Q$ | depletion (m³/d) | cumulative volume (m³) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 y | 0.2145 | 214.5 | 31604 |
| 5 y | 0.5788 | 578.8 | 688872 |
| 10 y | 0.6946 | 694.6 | 1867684 |
| 30 y | 0.8207 | 820.7 | 7518285 |

The complement, $1 - \operatorname{erfc}(\cdot)$, is the fraction taken from
storage: **0.7855 at one year and 0.1793 at thirty**. Capture and storage depletion
are two names for the same water, and the time integral of the first column is
what a streamflow record would show as a long-term decline if the pumping were
the only change. The depletion reaches 10% of the pumpage at

$$t = \frac{L^2 S}{4T(1.1631)^2}
= \frac{(1500)^2(0.10)}{4(200)(1.35280)} = 207.9\ \mathrm{d} = 0.57\ \mathrm{yr},$$

where 1.1631 is the argument at which $\operatorname{erfc} = 0.1$.

## 5. Uncertainty: the distribution, not the number

The parameters above are estimated, not known. The demonstration draws 4000
samples with $T$ lognormal (sd of $\ln T = 0.30$), $S = 0.10 \pm 0.05$,
$L = 1500 \pm 250$ m and $S_y = 0.10 \pm 0.05$, and propagates them:

| quantity | 5% | median | 95% | spread |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| time to 10% depletion (y) | 0.115 | 0.516 | 1.401 | $\times12.1$ |
| depletion fraction at 5 y | 0.384 | 0.597 | 0.803 | — |
| depletion fraction at 10 y | 0.538 | 0.709 | 0.860 | — |
| recharge from WTF (mm/a) | 14.9 | 86.4 | 155.0 | $\times10.4$ |

The same propagation applied to the decision — the pumping rate permitted by a
10% tolerance on the low flow at a five-year horizon — gives a median of
**723 m³/d** with a 5–95% range of **538 to 1125 m³/d**.

Three observations.

- **The distributions are wide and skewed in opposite directions.** The depletion
  time spans a factor of 12 and the recharge a factor of 10 — on parameters whose
  standard deviations are 30%, 50 m/yr and 0.05 respectively. A single-number
  answer is the median of a very wide distribution.
- **The quantities are not equally uncertain.** The 10-year depletion fraction is
  comparatively well constrained (0.54–0.86) because at ten years the argument of
  the erfc is small and the function is flat there. Quantities that depend on the
  *transition* (like the time to 10%) are the badly determined ones — and
  unfortunately, the transition is exactly what a regulator wants to know.
- **The uncertainty is dominated by parameters that were not measured.** In a
  real study, $L$ comes from a map or a seismic line, $T$ from a pump test, $S$
  from the same test and $S_y$ from a handful of samples. Reporting the sampling
  distribution of the *answer* is more informative than reporting any of them
  individually, and it is the only form in which the answer can be used in a
  decision.

## 6. The decision

Now the two yields, computed for the demonstration's idealised case with a
low-flow stream discharge of $4320\ \mathrm{m^3\,d^{-1}}$ (a SYNTHETIC value,
equivalent to $0.05\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}$) and a tolerance of 10% of it:

- **Recharge-limited:** $R \times A$ with $R = 86.4$ mm/a (the Monte Carlo
  median) over $20\ \mathrm{km^2}$ gives **4737 m³/d**.
- **Capture-limited:** the permitted depletion is $0.10 \times 4320 =
  432\ \mathrm{m^3\,d^{-1}}$; with a depletion fraction of 0.5788 at five years,
  the permitted pumping is $Q \le 432/0.5788 = $ **746 m³/d**.

The recharge limit is **6.3 times** the capture limit. Both numbers are correct;
they answer different questions:

- 4737 m³/d answers "how much water arrives?" — exceeding it on average means
  mining storage, whatever the stream does.
- 746 m³/d answers "how much can be taken before the stream changes by more than
  the agreed amount, within the agreed time?" — the hydrologically binding
  constraint for a connected stream.

An assessment that reports the first as "the sustainable yield" has answered the
wrong question, and will be wrong by a factor of six in the direction of
over-abstraction. An assessment that reports the second must name the stream, the
10% figure, the low-flow discharge it is a fraction of, and the five-year
horizon; change any of those and the number changes. **The value of the science
is that it makes the dependence explicit**, so that the decision can be made on
the trade-off rather than on a number whose provenance is invisible.

### 6.1 The audit

The closing table of the demonstration is the reporting format this lesson argues
for:

| quantity | value | measured or assumed |
|---|---|---|
| specific yield | 0.10 | assumed in the fluctuation inversion |
| recharge (WTF, true $S_y$) | 85.1 mm/a | model-derived from a synthetic record |
| recharge (CMB) | 33.3 mm/a | assumed chloride values |
| time to 10% depletion | 208 d | assumed $T$, $S$, $L$ |
| capture-limited yield | 746 m³/d | chosen tolerance and low-flow value |

Five numbers, and not one of them is a measurement of the quantity it is used to
represent. That is not a defect of this idealised example; it is the normal
condition of water-resource assessment, and the reason the audit column matters
more than the value column.

## 7. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **The water table is a valid recharge recorder.** It fails in a confined
   aquifer (no water table), in a strongly pumped aquifer (the signal is
   withdrawal, not recharge), and where lateral flow dominates.
2. **$S_y$ is a constant, and its value is that of the *drainage* timescale.**
   The $S_y$ relevant to a seasonal rise is not the $S_y$ relevant to a 30-year
   average, and the difference can be a factor of two in fine-grained material.
3. **The chloride input is known and conservative.** Section 3 lists the ways it
   is not.
4. **The stream is fully connected (Glover).** A clogged streambed makes the
   boundary a *leakage* rather than a constant head, and the depletion then
   follows a different (and slower, sometimes bounded) curve. The Glover limit is
   an upper bound on the rate and a lower bound on the time.
5. **The aquifer is homogeneous, the well fully penetrating, and $Q$ constant.**
   Layered or fractured aquifers, partially penetrating wells and seasonally
   varying pumping all change the answer.
6. **No other stresses.** The solution is linear, so several wells can be
   superposed — but so can their impacts, and the assessment of a *field* is not
   the assessment of one well.
7. **The low-flow discharge and the tolerance are inputs, not outputs.** They are
   the decision, and they must be sourced from ecology, from water rights or from
   the community, not from the hydrogeologist.

The regime of best validity is a shallow, unconfined, homogeneous alluvial
aquifer with a fully connected gaining stream, a multi-year water-level record, a
pumping test, and a documented low-flow regime. Every departure from that
condition adds a stated uncertainty or an unstated bias, and the audit table of
Section 6.1 is how you tell which.

## 8. Worked examples

### Example 1 — recharge from a rise, by hand

A water-level record shows a seasonal rise of 0.85 m between the annual minimum
and the following maximum. With $S_y = 0.10$,

$$R = S_y \Delta h = 0.10 \times 0.85\ \mathrm{m} = 0.085\ \mathrm{m}
= 85\ \mathrm{mm\,a^{-1}},$$

which is the demonstration's 85.1 mm/a to the precision of the input.

The drainage term is not zero, and the demonstration isolates it by measuring the
same cycles over the recharge season itself (using the noiseless model states, so
that the closure is exact apart from arithmetic):

| year | $z$ start (m) | $z$ end (m) | $S_y\Delta z$ (mm) | drainage (mm) | sum (mm) | recharge (mm) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.521 | 2.275 | 75.3 | 17.1 | 92.5 | 94.0 |
| 2 | 1.456 | 2.267 | 81.1 | 16.8 | 98.0 | 99.6 |
| 3 | 1.450 | 2.208 | 75.8 | 16.5 | 92.3 | 93.9 |
| 4 | 1.409 | 1.991 | 58.2 | 15.4 | 73.5 | 74.8 |

The drainage runs at roughly $k_d z \approx 0.15 \times 1.9 = 0.28\
\mathrm{mm\,d^{-1}}$, so about 17 mm over a 60-day rise, and
$S_y\Delta z + \int k_d z\,\mathrm{d}t$ reproduces the imposed recharge to within
2%. **The correction is computable** if $k_d$ is known from the recession curve,
and it is the same order as the difference between the two methods of
Section 3. Note also why the "bias" printed for the windowed estimate is only
$-6.0\%$: a calendar window's minimum falls a few days before the rise begins,
which makes $\Delta z$ slightly larger and accidentally compensates part of the
drainage term. That compensation is a coincidence of the window choice, not a
cancellation of the bias.

### Example 2 — the chloride mass balance, and its sensitivity

With $P = 800$ mm/a and $\mathrm{Cl}_{\text{gw}} = 12.0$ mg/L:

$$R = \frac{800 \times 0.5}{12.0} = 33.3\ \mathrm{mm\,a^{-1}}.$$

If the deposition value is actually 0.8 mg/L (the analyst used a coastal value
for an inland site), then $R = 53.3$ mm/a; if the groundwater sample is diluted
by 20% by a low-chloride inflow, $\mathrm{Cl}_{\text{gw}} = 9.6$ gives
$R = 41.7$ mm/a. **A factor-1.6 error in either input moves the answer by the
same factor**, which is why the method is only defensible with local deposition
data and a sampled recharge end-member.

### Example 3 — stream depletion, by hand

For $L = 1500$ m, $T = 200\ \mathrm{m^2\,d^{-1}}$, $S = 0.10$ and $t = 1$ yr
$= 365$ d:

$$L\sqrt{\frac{S}{4Tt}} = 1500\sqrt{\frac{0.10}{4\times200\times365}}
= 1500\sqrt{3.4247\times10^{-7}} = 1500 \times 5.8517\times10^{-4} = 0.87775,$$

$$\operatorname{erfc}(0.87775) = 0.2145,$$

which is the demonstration's 1-year figure, so the depletion is
$0.2145 \times 1000 = 214.5\ \mathrm{m^3\,d^{-1}}$. Over the first year the
cumulative volume is $\int_0^{365}Q_{\text{stream}}\,\mathrm{d}t
= 31604\ \mathrm{m^3}$, i.e. an average depletion of
$31604/365 = 86.6\ \mathrm{m^3\,d^{-1}}$. Note that the *average* over the year
is only 40% of the year-end rate: reporting the end-of-year depletion as if it
applied throughout would overstate the first year's impact by a factor of 2.5.

### Example 4 — the error budget of a yield decision

The capture-limited yield is $Q \le Q_{\text{perm}}/\operatorname{erfc}(\cdot)$
with $Q_{\text{perm}} = 0.10 \times$ low flow $= 432\ \mathrm{m^3\,d^{-1}}$.
Propagating the parameter uncertainties of Section 5 through the same arithmetic
gives a median of $723\ \mathrm{m^3\,d^{-1}}$ with a 5–95% range of **538 to
1125 m³/d** (the range is asymmetric about the median because the division by a
fraction is nonlinear). The *decision* therefore has to be made on a range of
about a factor of two, and a licence issued at the Section 6 figure of
746 m³/d has a substantial probability of exceeding the agreed tolerance.
**The probability, not the number, is the decision input** — and note that the
median here (723 m³/d) differs slightly from the Section 6 value (746 m³/d),
which was computed with the nominal parameters rather than with the Monte Carlo
distribution. Both are defensible; only one of them carries a probability.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Quoting the recharge-limited yield as "the sustainable yield".** It was 6.3
   times the capture limit in the demonstration.
2. **Calibrating the specific yield on one recharge method and then presenting
   both as independent.** The required value was 0.039 against a true 0.10.
3. **Choosing the rise window for convenience.** A factor-1.32 spread between
   plausible windows.
4. **Forgetting the drainage correction.** A measurable $-6\%$ bias, with a sign
   that is always the same.
5. **Reporting the end-of-year depletion as the annual average.** It overstated
   the first year's volume by a factor of 2.5 here.
6. **Treating a clogged streambed as a connected one.** Glover is an upper bound
   on the rate and a lower bound on the time.
7. **Reporting a single number without its distribution.** The depletion time
   spanned a factor of 12 across plausible parameter values.

## Self-assessment checklist

- [ ] I can compute a recharge from a measured rise and state its two biases.
- [ ] I can compute a chloride mass balance and explain a disagreement with the
      fluctuation method without averaging the two.
- [ ] I can derive the Glover depletion fraction and compute it at several times.
- [ ] I can separate capture from storage depletion and compute both volumes.
- [ ] I can propagate parameter uncertainty and report a distribution.
- [ ] I can formulate a sustainable yield as a decision with a receptor, a
      horizon and a tolerance.
- [ ] I can write the audit table for my own assessment, with each number's
      provenance.
