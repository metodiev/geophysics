# Integrated Catchment Characterisation

**All models and data in this module are SYNTHETIC.** The catchment is an
idealised rectangle of chosen area; the rainfall, potential evaporation,
discharge and seismic travel times are generated by the models in
[`code/catchment_characterisation_demo.py`](./code/catchment_characterisation_demo.py).
No real catchment, gauging station, borehole, rain gauge or published number is
used anywhere in it, and no site is named.

"Catchment characterisation" means producing a description of a whole catchment
that is consistent — internally, and with every measurement made in it. The
consistency requirement is what makes the task hard and what makes it
interesting. A water balance can always be closed by adjusting the one term that
was not measured; a baseflow index can always be obtained by choosing a filter
parameter; a storage volume can always be computed by multiplying an area by a
thickness and a specific yield. Each of those operations is easy and each is a
statement about an assumption. The integrated description is only worth as much
as its weakest assumption, and finding out which assumption that is, is the
actual work.

This lesson builds a minimal catchment from scratch — a synthetic forcing series,
a three-store conceptual model, a hydrograph, five synthetic seismic refraction
soundings — and then follows four quantities through it: the water balance, the
baseflow index, the regolith thickness and the catchment storage. The pattern
that emerges is the honest content of the lesson: the geophysics constrains the
*geometry* well (a few per cent), the hydrology constrains the *fluxes* well, and
the quantity that the water-resource question actually needs — the storage — is
dominated by a parameter that neither of them measured.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Close** a catchment water balance over a record and **show**, by sensitivity
   analysis, that the closure is a property of the bookkeeping rather than
   evidence about the catchment.
2. **Separate** a hydrograph with a one-parameter digital filter and **report**
   the baseflow index as a range with its filter parameter.
3. **Derive** the two-layer refraction travel-time relations and **recover** a
   regolith thickness from an intercept time and a slope, including its
   sensitivity to the assumed overburden velocity.
4. **Propagate** the uncertainty of the geophysical thickness and of the specific
   yield into a catchment storage volume and **identify** the dominant term.
5. **Demonstrate** the equifinality of a recession, by fitting a one-parameter
   family of reservoir models to one recession and **showing** that the implied
   storage spans an order of magnitude and is unbounded at the limit.
6. **State** which quantities in an integrated study are measured, which are
   inferred, and which are assumed.

## 1. The integration problem

An integrated catchment study has four kinds of statement in it, and they are not
interchangeable:

| kind | example here | how it is obtained |
|---|---|---|
| measured | discharge, rainfall, travel times | an instrument, with an error |
| inferred | runoff coefficient, regolith thickness | a model applied to measurements |
| assumed | specific yield, drainage time constant | a value carried from elsewhere |
| decided | the model structure, the filter parameter | an analyst's choice |

A characterisation is *integrated* when a statement of one kind can be traced to
the inputs of another, and when the assumed and decided rows are written down.
The demonstration's Section 7 prints exactly that audit, and the rest of this
lesson is the derivation of its entries.

The idealised catchment is a rectangle of $A = 12.0\ \mathrm{km^2}$, simulated
for 730 days, with three stores: a soil store of capacity 60 mm, a quick
reservoir with time constant 1.20 d and a slow reservoir with time constant 55 d,
fed in the ratio 0.35 / 0.65 from the soil drainage. The synthetic forcing gives
1615.1 mm of rain (807.6 mm/a) on 218 wet days and 1607.3 mm of potential
evaporation (803.6 mm/a).

## 2. The water balance and what its residual means

Over a record of length $T$, the balance is

$$\underbrace{\sum P}_{\text{input}} -
\underbrace{\left(\sum Q + \sum \mathrm{ET}\right)}_{\text{output}}
= \Delta S,$$

with every term in millimetres of water over the catchment. For the
demonstration's 730-day record:

| term | value (mm) |
|---|---|
| $P$ | 1615.1 |
| $Q$ | 318.4 |
| ET | 1257.6 |
| $\Delta S$ | 39.18 |
| residual $P - Q - \mathrm{ET} - \Delta S$ | $1.7\times10^{-13}$ |

The residual is zero **because the stores were carried through the arithmetic**.
It is a check on the bookkeeping, not a measurement of anything: the discharge
was generated by the same model that generated the storage, so of course it
closes. The informative quantity is what an error in one term does to the
*implied* storage change, which is the fourth line of the balance:

| ET error | implied $\Delta S$ (mm) |
|---|---|
| $-20\%$ | $+290.69$ |
| $-10\%$ | $+164.93$ |
| $+10\%$ | $-86.58$ |
| $+20\%$ | $-212.33$ |

A 10% error in evapotranspiration moves the implied storage change by 125.8 mm,
which is **3 times the quantity being estimated** (39.18 mm). The magnitudes:
ET is 78% of rainfall here, so an error model for ET is worth far more than a
better rain gauge. Two practical consequences:

- A water balance never *measures* storage change; it converts the errors of the
  other terms into one. To measure storage change you need the storage — gravity,
  InSAR, or a dense water-level network.
- The runoff coefficient $Q/P = 0.1971$ and the evaporation ratio
  $\mathrm{ET}/P = 0.7786$ are the robust outputs, because they are ratios of
  large measured quantities, not residuals.

## 3. Hydrograph separation, and why the baseflow index is a range

The baseflow index (BFI) is the fraction of the total discharge that came from
storage rather than from the event. The standard practical tool is a recursive
digital filter. The Lyne–Hollick one-parameter filter is

$$q_{f,i} = \alpha q_{f,i-1} + \tfrac{1}{2}(1+\alpha)\left(q_i - q_{i-1}\right),
\qquad q_{b,i} = q_i - q_{f,i},$$

applied once forward and once backward and averaged; $\alpha \in [0,1)$ is the
**filter parameter** and it is chosen, not measured.

| $\alpha$ | baseflow volume (mm) | BFI | peak baseflow (mm/d) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.900 | 203.4 | 0.6387 | 1.836 |
| 0.925 | 197.4 | 0.6200 | 1.827 |
| 0.950 | 189.2 | 0.5943 | 1.817 |
| 0.975 | 173.9 | 0.5462 | 1.734 |
| 0.990 | 142.9 | 0.4488 | 1.588 |

Two facts must be reported together. First, the separated total is
$Q = 318.4$ mm in every case — the filter redistributes, it does not create —
so the BFI is a *split* of a known quantity. Second, the split varies by a factor
1.42 over a plausible range of $\alpha$: **BFI ranges from 0.449 to 0.639 at
fixed data**.

The remedy is an independent low-flow measurement: a seepage run with a current
meter, a salt-dilution gauging at low flow, a tracer, or a nested stream gauge
above and below a losing reach. Without one, the BFI is a modelling choice, and
the honest report is "BFI = 0.45–0.64 depending on the filter parameter, with
$\alpha = 0.925$ used for the headline figure". Note also that the peak baseflow
is nearly invariant across the range (1.59–1.84 mm/d) while the volume is not:
the filter changes the *duration* of the tail much more than its amplitude, and
the tail is where the volume is.

## 4. What the geophysics adds: the regolith thickness

The depth of the weathered zone over fresh bedrock is the geometry that controls
the storage. The cheapest and most common geophysical measurement of it is a
seismic refraction sounding: a hammer and a line of geophones, and the intercept
time of the refracted arrival.

### 4.1 The forward problem

For a two-layer model — a layer of thickness $h$ with velocity $v_1$ over a
half-space with $v_2 > v_1$ — the first arrival at offset $x$ is the smaller of
the direct and the head wave:

$$t_{\text{direct}} = \frac{x}{v_1},
\qquad
t_{\text{head}} = \frac{2h\cos i_c}{v_1} + \frac{x}{v_2},
\qquad \sin i_c = \frac{v_1}{v_2}.$$

The refracted branch is a straight line of slope $1/v_2$ and **intercept time**

$$t_i = \frac{2h\cos i_c}{v_1}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
h = \frac{t_i\,v_1}{2\cos i_c},$$

and it becomes the first arrival beyond the **crossover distance**

$$x_c = 2h\sqrt{\frac{v_2+v_1}{v_2-v_1}}.$$

### 4.2 The demonstration's synthetic soundings

The SYNTHETIC profile has $v_1 = 600$ m/s, $v_2 = 2500$ m/s and twenty offsets
from 2 to 40 m, with a picking error of 0.5 ms. Fitting all three parameters to
each noisy travel-time curve gives:

| site | true $h$ (m) | fitted $h$ (m) | fitted $v_1$ (m/s) | fitted $v_2$ (m/s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.20 | 1.548 | 693.3 | 2580.1 |
| 2 | 2.50 | 2.470 | 591.3 | 2542.1 |
| 3 | 0.80 | 1.145 | 868.0 | 2476.8 |
| 4 | 3.40 | 3.357 | 615.8 | 2368.3 |
| 5 | 1.90 | 1.727 | 585.7 | 2386.2 |

The mean fitted thickness is 2.049 m against a true mean of 1.960 m. The
distribution of errors is the informative part: **the thin layer is the badly
resolved one.** At site 3 a 0.80 m layer is recovered as 1.145 m — a 43% error —
with $v_1$ pushed up to 868 m/s. This is the classical velocity–thickness
trade-off of refraction: a faster top layer produces a longer intercept for the
same thickness, so the inversion can trade one against the other. The fitted
$v_1$ ranges over 586–868 m/s about a true 600 m/s.

### 4.3 The sensitivity of the depth to the assumed velocity

The trade-off has a closed form once $v_2$ is known from the slope. For site 2:

$$\sin i_c = \frac{600}{2500} = 0.240, \quad i_c = 13.887^\circ, \quad
\cos i_c = 0.9708,$$

$$t_i = \frac{2(2.50)(0.9708)}{600} = 8.090\ \mathrm{ms},
\qquad \frac{1}{v_2} = 0.4000\ \mathrm{ms\,m^{-1}},$$

$$x_c = 2(2.50)\sqrt{\frac{2500+600}{2500-600}} = 6.387\ \mathrm{m}.$$

Inverting the same intercept with $v_1 = 630$ m/s:

$$h = \frac{t_i v_1}{2\cos i_c}
= \frac{(8.090\times10^{-3})(630)}{2\cos(14.598^\circ)} = 2.6333\ \mathrm{m},$$

an error of $+5.33\%$ for a $+5\%$ error in $v_1$. **The velocity enters
twice** — directly in the factor $v_1$ and through $\cos i_c$ — so the depth
error slightly exceeds the velocity error, and neither is small compared with
the 4.8% picking-limited uncertainty below.

### 4.4 The uncertainty actually achieved

Repeating the whole five-site inversion 200 times with fresh picking noise gives
a mean catchment thickness of 2.017 m with a standard deviation of 0.097 m,
i.e. the mean is good to 4.8%. Two caveats must accompany that number:

- It is the **picking** contribution only. The dominant real uncertainty is
  *spatial*: five soundings in a 12 km² catchment sample nothing, and the
  thickness varies laterally in a way none of these numbers represents. The
  demonstration's five sites have true thicknesses from 0.8 to 3.4 m; the
  catchment mean of *those five* is not the catchment mean of the catchment.
- It assumes a two-layer model. A velocity gradient in the regolith (weathered
  rock is normally graded) makes the "refractor" a deeper apparent interface,
  biasing the thickness upward.

## 5. Catchment storage, and which uncertainty dominates

With the regolith thickness $h$, the area $A$ and the specific yield $S_y$, the
drainable storage is

$$V = A\,h\,S_y .$$

For the demonstration: $A = 12.0\ \mathrm{km^2}$, $h = 2.049$ m (the fitted mean),
$S_y = 0.10$ (a chosen teaching value):

$$V = 12.0\times10^{6} \times 2.049 \times 0.10 = 2.459\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m^3}
= 2.459\ \mathrm{Mm^3}.$$

Propagating the two uncertainties:

| source | sd | contribution to $V$ | as % of $V$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| regolith thickness | 0.097 m | 0.1163 Mm³ | 4.73% |
| specific yield | 0.05 | 1.2296 Mm³ | 50.00% |

The specific yield contributes **10.6 times** more uncertainty than the
geophysics, and this asymmetry is the honest punchline of the whole module. The
seismic survey measured the thickness it was asked to measure, and measured it
well. The number the water-resource question wants is a *volume*, and the volume
is dominated by a parameter that the seismic survey cannot see at all.

The remedies are known and each has a cost: pumping tests and long-term
water-level records to estimate $S_y$ at the scale that matters; a tracer test
for the mobile porosity; a neutron or NMR measurement for the total and the
mobile water; and, above all, an honest statement of the range. What does *not*
help is adding more refraction sites: the error is not in the geometry.

## 6. Equifinality: the recession does not determine the storage

A recession curve is the most information-dense part of a hydrograph, and it is
routinely used to fit a reservoir model of the form

$$S = k\,Q^{m}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}t} = -Q
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}t} = -a\,Q^{b}, \quad b = 2-m, \quad
a = \frac{1}{mk}.$$

A linear reservoir is the case $b = 1$ (exponential recession); $b > 1$ is the
usual finding in nature. With $S = kQ^{m}$ and $m = 2-b$,

$$S = \frac{Q^{\,2-b}}{a\,(2-b)},$$

which is the quantity a storage estimate needs. The demonstration fits the whole
one-parameter family to *one* 90-day recession (from day 485, $Q$ falling from
3.468 to 0.190 mm/d):

| $b$ | fitted $Q_0$ (mm/d) | fitted $a$ | rms (mm/d) | implied $S$ (mm) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | 1.050 | $1.9598\times10^{-2}$ | 0.25866 | 53.56 |
| 1.20 | 1.351 | $2.9916\times10^{-2}$ | 0.24285 | 53.14 |
| 1.50 | 1.496 | $3.7305\times10^{-2}$ | 0.23362 | 65.56 |
| 1.80 | 1.705 | $4.6664\times10^{-2}$ | 0.22248 | 119.21 |
| 1.95 | 1.854 | $5.2528\times10^{-2}$ | 0.21553 | 392.68 |

Every member of the family reproduces the recession to within **7.5% of the
initial flow** — well inside what a typical gauged record could distinguish — and
the implied storage grows from 54 mm to 393 mm, a factor 7.3, with the growth
continuing without bound as $b \to 2$ because then $2-b \to 0$ in the
denominator. **A recession alone cannot bound the storage of a nonlinear
reservoir at all.** The quantity is identifiable only when $b$ is fixed by
something else — a long multi-year recession, a simultaneous water-level record
(the $S$ in a water balance), or an independent storage estimate.

This is the second face of the same lesson as Section 5: an integrated
characterisation is limited by its least-identified link, and the reported number
must carry that link's uncertainty rather than its own precision.

## 7. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **A single gauged outlet and a closed topographic divide.** The demonstration
   has one outlet by construction; real catchments leak across divides,
   especially in karst and in flat terrain.
2. **Rainfall is uniform over the catchment.** The demonstration's rain is a
   single series, which is the same assumption; real storms have gradients, and
   the error appears directly in the water balance.
3. **The conceptual model structure is right.** Three stores, one partition
   fraction, two time constants: chosen. Section 6 is the demonstration that a
   different structure fits the same data.
4. **The filter parameter is appropriate for the hydrogeology.** It is not, in
   general, transferable between catchments.
5. **The refraction model is two-layered, flat and laterally homogeneous.** See
   Section 4.4.
6. **The specific yield is a constant.** It varies with depth and with the
   drainage timescale; a value appropriate to a drought recession is not the
   value appropriate to a storm flood.
7. **The record is long enough to contain the slowest store at least once.** Two
   years is not; the demonstration's slow time constant is 55 d, which two years
   resolves reasonably, but a 3-year store would not be seen.

## 8. Worked examples

### Example 1 — the water balance, and the size of an ET error

Over one water year the demonstration's totals are $P = 807.6$ mm, $Q = 159.2$
mm (half of the 318.4 mm two-year total), and $\Delta S = 19.6$ mm (half of
39.18 mm). The balance gives

$$\mathrm{ET} = P - Q - \Delta S = 807.6 - 159.2 - 19.6 = 628.8\ \mathrm{mm},$$

and $\mathrm{ET}/P = 0.779$, matching the two-year ratio. Now suppose ET is
overestimated by 10%: $\mathrm{ET}' = 691.7$ mm, and the imbalance is
$807.6 - 159.2 - 691.7 = -43.3$ mm, which the analyst must attribute to storage.
The implied $\Delta S$ changes from $+19.6$ to $-43.3$ mm — a swing of 62.9 mm in
a quantity smaller than 20 mm. **The balance has no way to tell this from a real
storage change**, which is why $\Delta S$ over a single year is essentially
unmeasurable from a water balance alone.

### Example 2 — a refraction sounding by hand

A head-wave branch with slope $0.4000\ \mathrm{ms\,m^{-1}}$ gives
$v_2 = 1/0.4000\ \mathrm{ms\,m^{-1}} = 2500\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Its intercept on
the time axis is $t_i = 8.090$ ms. With $v_1 = 600$ m/s (assumed from the direct
branch, which requires offsets short enough to record it — here down to 2 m, so
the direct branch is measured over 2–6.387 m):

$$\sin i_c = 0.240 \Rightarrow i_c = 13.887^\circ,\quad \cos i_c = 0.9708,$$

$$h = \frac{(8.090\times10^{-3})(600)}{2(0.9708)}
= \frac{4.854}{1.9416} = 2.500\ \mathrm{m}.$$

Now assume the direct branch was mis-read and $v_1 = 700$ m/s. Then
$\sin i_c = 0.280$, $i_c = 16.260^\circ$, $\cos i_c = 0.9600$ and

$$h' = \frac{(8.090\times10^{-3})(700)}{2(0.9600)}
= \frac{5.663}{1.9200} = 2.949\ \mathrm{m},$$

an error of $+18.0\%$ from a $+16.7\%$ velocity error. The velocity-thickness
trade-off is close to one-for-one, and it is the reason refraction depths are
reported *with* the velocity model that produced them, and why a short direct
branch (small near-offsets) is not an optional extra.

### Example 3 — the storage error budget

With $V = A h S_y$ and independent uncertainties, the fractional variance is

$$\left(\frac{\sigma_V}{V}\right)^2
= \left(\frac{\sigma_h}{h}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\sigma_{S_y}}{S_y}\right)^2
= \left(\frac{0.097}{2.049}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{0.05}{0.10}\right)^2
= 0.00224 + 0.25000,$$

so $\sigma_V/V = \sqrt{0.25224} = 0.5022$, i.e. $V = 2.459 \pm 1.235\ \mathrm{Mm^3}$
at one standard deviation — a 50% uncertainty on a number that would otherwise
be quoted to three figures. The thickness term contributes 0.9% of the variance
and the specific yield 99.1%.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Presenting a closed water balance as a validation.** The residual is small
   because the stores were carried through the arithmetic.
2. **Reporting a baseflow index without the filter parameter.** A factor-1.42
   range at fixed data.
3. **Quoting a refraction depth without its velocity model.** The trade-off is
   nearly one-for-one.
4. **Treating the picking uncertainty as the thickness uncertainty.** Spatial
   sampling dominates it and is not in the number.
5. **Quoting a storage volume to three figures.** The specific yield dominates
   the error by an order of magnitude.
6. **Reading a recession as a storage measurement.** The implied storage is
   unbounded as $b \to 2$: the recession identifies a product, not a storage.
7. **Confusing an integrated characterisation with a calibrated model.** They
   are different artefacts, and only the second is testable against unseen data.
