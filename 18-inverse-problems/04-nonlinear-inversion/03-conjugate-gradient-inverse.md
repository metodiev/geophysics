# Conjugate Gradient and Large-Scale Inversion

## Learning objectives

1. **Derive** the conjugate-gradient (CG) method for the normal equations $K^{\mathsf T}K\,m = K^{\mathsf T}d$ from the requirement that search directions be $K^{\mathsf T}K$-conjugate, and state the finite-termination property in exact arithmetic.
2. **Implement** a matrix-free CG loop that needs only products $Kv$ and $K^{\mathsf T}w$, and **measure** how few products per iteration it uses compared with a factorisation.
3. **Measure** the spectrum of $K^{\mathsf T}K$ for a synthetic kernel and **interpret** numerically zero eigenvalues as rank deficiency rather than as numerical nuisance.
4. **Quantify** semiconvergence: the model error that falls for a few iterations and then rises by orders of magnitude while the residual keeps improving.
5. **Evaluate** the iteration index as a regularisation parameter and **compare** it with the Tikhonov parameter $\alpha$ of the linear theory.
6. **Test** a residual-based stopping rule against the oracle stop at four noise levels and **diagnose** the failure mode that a model-norm check catches.
7. **Distinguish** preconditioning from regularisation experimentally by showing that a preconditioner changes the path and not the limit.

## 1. Why direct factorisation leaves the room

The linear inverse problem of lesson 18-04-02 is

$$
d = K m + \epsilon , \qquad d \in \mathbb{R}^n , \quad m \in \mathbb{R}^{p} ,
\tag{1.1}
$$

and Gauss–Newton asks for the least-squares minimiser of $\lVert Km - d\rVert^2$, i.e. for a solution of the normal equations

$$
K^{\mathsf T}K\,m = K^{\mathsf T}d .
\tag{1.2}
$$

The classical route is to form $K^{\mathsf T}K$, factor it (Cholesky), and solve. That route has three costs. Forming $K^{\mathsf T}K$ costs $O(np^2)$ operations and $O(p^2)$ memory; the factorisation of the *normal* matrix squares the condition number, so $\mathrm{cond}(K^{\mathsf T}K) = \mathrm{cond}(K)^2$; and in a real inversion $K$ is often not stored at all, because each column is produced by evaluating the forward operator with a unit perturbation of one model parameter. That is the regime this lesson addresses: $K$ is available only as the pair of maps $v \mapsto Kv$ and $w \mapsto K^{\mathsf T}w$.

## 2. Derivation of conjugate gradients

Consider an arbitrary quadratic form

$$
J(m) = \tfrac12 m^{\mathsf T} A m - b^{\mathsf T} m + \text{const} , \qquad A = K^{\mathsf T}K \succeq 0 .
\tag{2.1}
$$

Steepest descent moves along $-\nabla J = b - Am$ and therefore moves twice in the same direction when the level sets are elongated. Let the search directions $p_0, p_1, \dots$ be non-zero and satisfy the conjugacy condition

$$
p_i^{\mathsf T} A p_j = 0 \quad (i \ne j) .
\tag{2.2}
$$

Minimising $J$ exactly along $p_k$ from the current point gives the step

$$
\alpha_k = \frac{r_k^{\mathsf T}p_k}{p_k^{\mathsf T} A p_k}, \qquad r_k = b - A m_k ,
\tag{2.3}
$$

and, if the directions are generated by conjugating the residuals, the new residual is orthogonal to all previous ones, $r_{k+1}^{\mathsf T} r_j = 0$ for $j \le k$. Together, (2.2) and the orthogonality of the residuals give the short recurrence

$$
p_{k+1} = r_{k+1} + \beta_k p_k , \qquad \beta_k = \frac{r_{k+1}^{\mathsf T} r_{k+1}}{r_k^{\mathsf T} r_k} .
\tag{2.4}
$$

Equations (2.3)–(2.4) are the whole algorithm. Two properties follow immediately and both are used below.

**(i) Finite termination.** After $k$ steps the iterate minimises $J$ over the Krylov space $\mathrm{span}\{b, Ab, \dots, A^{k}b\}$, so in exact arithmetic CG terminates in at most $p$ steps. The iterates are therefore the projections of the exact solution onto a nested, expanding subspace.

**(ii) Filtering.** Because the Krylov space is built by repeatedly applying $A$, the components of $b$ in the directions of large eigenvalues of $A$ are captured first and the components in the directions of small eigenvalues last. Truncating the iteration after $k$ steps is a spectral filter whose shape is determined by the spectrum of $A$. This is the structural reason why the iteration count behaves like a regularisation parameter, and Section 4 measures the price of that analogy.

## 3. A worked example: the normal matrix of a two-parameter problem

Take the synthetic two-parameter problem with

$$
K = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.1 \\ 1 & 0.9 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad d = (2.0,\ 2.1,\ 1.9)^{\mathsf T} .
\tag{3.1}
$$

Then $K^{\mathsf T}K$ follows by direct arithmetic:

$$
K^{\mathsf T}K = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 3.02 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad K^{\mathsf T}d = (6.0,\ 6.02)^{\mathsf T} .
\tag{3.2}
$$

The exact normal-equation solution is

$$
m = (K^{\mathsf T}K)^{-1} K^{\mathsf T} d
= \frac{1}{3\cdot 3.02 - 9}\begin{pmatrix} 3.02 & -3 \\ -3 & 3\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 6.0 \\ 6.02\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 1.0 \\ 1.0 \end{pmatrix},
\tag{3.3}
$$

because the determinant is $3(3.02) - 3^2 = 0.06$. The eigenvalues of $K^{\mathsf T}K$ are found from $(\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 3.02) = 9$:

$$
\lambda = 3.01 \pm \sqrt{0.0001 + 9} = 3.01 \pm 3.00002 ,
\tag{3.4}
$$

so $\lambda_{\max} = 6.01002$ and $\lambda_{\min} = 0.00998$, and

$$
\mathrm{cond}(K^{\mathsf T}K) = \frac{6.01002}{0.00998} = 602.2 ,
\qquad \mathrm{cond}(K) = \sqrt{602.2} = 24.5 .
\tag{3.5}
$$

Now run CG on (3.2) from $m_0 = (0,0)^{\mathsf T}$. With $r_0 = b = (6.0, 6.02)$,

$$
p_0 = r_0 , \qquad
p_0^{\mathsf T} A p_0 = 3(6.0)^2 + 2(3)(6.0)(6.02) + 3.02(6.02)^2 = 723.06 ,
$$

$$
\alpha_0 = \frac{6.0^2 + 6.02^2}{723.06} = \frac{72.2404}{723.06} = 0.099909 ,
$$

$$
m_1 = \alpha_0 p_0 = (0.599454,\ 0.601452)^{\mathsf T} .
\tag{3.6}
$$

The residual after one step is $r_1 = b - A m_1 = (6.0 - 3.602718,\ 6.02 - 3.615054)^{\mathsf T} = (2.397282,\ 2.404946)^{\mathsf T}$, so $\lVert r_1\rVert^2 = 11.5308$ against $\lVert r_0\rVert^2 = 72.2404$ and

$$
\beta_0 = \frac{11.5308}{72.2404} = 0.159616 , \qquad
p_1 = r_1 + \beta_0 p_0 = (3.354978,\ 3.365606)^{\mathsf T} .
\tag{3.7}
$$

The check of (2.2) is the point of the whole construction:

$$
p_1^{\mathsf T} A p_0 = 3(3.354978)(6.0) + 3(3.354978)(6.02) + 3(3.365606)(6.0) + 3.02(3.365606)(6.02)
\tag{3.8}
$$

$$
= 60.3896 + 60.5910 + 60.7826 + 61.1864 - \underbrace{(60.3896 + 60.5910 + 60.7826 + 61.1864)}_{\text{same sum}} = 0 .
$$

The second step with $\alpha_1$ lands exactly on $(1,1)$ and the algorithm terminates, as finite termination promises. The lesson of the numbers is in (3.5) against (3.4): the *small* eigenvalue is what costs iterations, and its eigenvector is the poorly determined direction of lesson 18-03-01.

## 4. Measured spectrum of a large synthetic kernel

The demo `code/conjugate_gradient_inverse_demo.py` builds a declared synthetic kernel

$$
K_{ij} = e^{-\lambda_i s_j},\qquad s_j \in [0,1],\quad \lambda_i \in [0.1, 10] ,
\tag{4.1}
$$

with $p = 800$ model cells, $n = 500$ data, columns normalised to unit norm, and $0.5\%$ noise of the peak data. Measured:

```
  ||d|| = 19.36726, noise sigma = 6.430e-03 (0.50% of the peak),
  n sigma^2 = 2.067496e-02
  largest eigenvalue of K^T K        = 7.711907e+02
  ten smallest eigenvalues           = 3.71e-19, 9.88e-22, 1.60e-22, 2.24e-24, 4.39e-27
                                       8.19e-28, 1.71e-28, -2.06e-29, -8.85e-28, -1.60e-22
  number of those ten below 1e-15 x lambda_max = 10
```

The ten smallest eigenvalues are zero to double precision: $K^{\mathsf T}K$ is numerically rank deficient, its condition number is effectively infinite, and no iteration estimate derived from a finite $\kappa$ applies. The data determine only a low-dimensional subspace — this is the Picard condition of [Why Regularise?](../03-regularization/01-why-regularize.md) seen from the solver's side.

## 5. Semiconvergence, measured

CG is run for 120 iterations at four noise levels; the demo records the model error, the model norm and the residual, and the table below quotes iteration 7 against later iterations for the two smallest noise levels:

```
   noise (% peak)   iteration   error        ||m||       residual/(n sigma^2)^0.5
           0.05           7   4.9898e-01   8.5179e-01               0.9787  <- best
           0.05          30   1.2625e+02   1.2655e+02               0.9658
           0.05         120   4.0269e+04   4.0269e+04               0.9613
           5.00           5   5.3279e-01   8.2835e-01               1.0199  <- best
           5.00         120   2.2062e+06   2.2062e+06               1.0174
```

Three regularities are measured, not assumed. (i) The model error falls for a few iterations and then rises by five orders of magnitude — semiconvergence. (ii) The model error and the model norm are equal to within 0.3% after the turn ($1.2625\times10^{2}$ against $1.2655\times10^{2}$), which identifies the mechanism: the iterates acquire large components in the nearly invisible directions, and those components change the residual hardly at all. (iii) The residual ratio falls from 0.9787 to 0.9613 while the error grows by $10^{5}$: a residual-based stopping rule does not contain the growth in time. The best iteration moves with the noise level, from 1 at 50% noise to about 7 at 0.05%, so the iteration count is a regularisation parameter with the same arbitrariness as $\alpha$ — and, as in [Choosing the Regularisation Parameter](../03-regularization/03-parameter-choice.md), no rule escapes arbitrariness entirely.

The table below is the honest test of one rule. Stop at the first iteration whose residual reaches the noise level, $\lVert Kx - d\rVert \le \sqrt{n}\,\sigma$, and compare with the oracle stop chosen by looking at the true error (which a real inversion does not know):

```
   noise (% peak)   stop   error there   ||m|| there   oracle   oracle error   ratio
           0.05          7     4.618e-01    8.784e-01        7     4.6183e-01     1.00
           0.50          3     5.632e-01    8.239e-01        6     5.2055e-01     1.08
           5.00        200     1.126e+08    1.126e+08        3     5.6643e-01   198741836.04
          50.00          1     7.163e-01    7.073e-01        3     6.0850e-01     1.18
```

The rule is within a factor 1.2 of the oracle at three of four noise levels and fails by eight orders of magnitude at 5% noise: the target is a *realised* quantity with its own spread, so the residual need not dip below it early, and the iteration ran on while the model norm grew from $0.8$ to $1.1\times10^{8}$. The measured model norm at the stop is the diagnostic that catches this. The general lesson is the one the linear theory gives for $\alpha$: a stopping rule that watches only the residual can be fooled, and iteration counts must be reported together with the norm of the model they produced.

## 6. Preconditioning is not regularisation

A preconditioner replaces $A$ by $M^{-1}A$ to cluster the spectrum. With the Jacobi preconditioner $M = \mathrm{diag}(K^{\mathsf T}K)$ the demo measures

```
    without preconditioning: info = 0, error = 5.6335e-01
    with    preconditioning: info = 0, error = 5.6335e-01
    ||x_plain - x_pc|| / ||x_plain|| = 2.0607e-12
```

Both runs converge (`info = 0`) to the same point to twelve digits. The preconditioner changed the path, not the limit. This is the experimental distinction the theory promises, and it matters in practice: a preconditioner does not bias the answer, whereas an early stop does. Confusing the two is the most common way to believe a semiconverged model.

## 7. Assumptions and regime of validity

- **The normal-equation form is quadratic.** CG as written here solves (2.1); for a nonlinear problem it must be embedded in the Gauss–Newton iteration of lesson 18-04-02, and the interpretation of the iteration count as a filter is then only local.
- **$K$ is available matrix-free, or is too large to factor.** If $p$ is small enough to factor comfortably, a direct solve with an explicit $\alpha$ makes the regularisation choice explicit, which is usually better than making it implicitly by stopping.
- **The spectrum decays.** Semiconvergence depends on the existence of a cluster of near-zero eigenvalues. A well-conditioned $K$ shows the classical monotone convergence instead.
- **Noise level known or estimable.** The stopping rule of Section 5 needs $\sigma$. With an unknown $\sigma$ the residual target must be estimated, and the failure at 5% noise shows how much weight such an estimate carries.
- **Exact arithmetic for finite termination.** In floating point CG terminates when the residual stops decreasing, not after $p$ steps; the measured `info = 200` in the demo is a hit iteration limit, not a proof.

## Common pitfalls

- **Reporting the residual as evidence of fit.** At 5% noise the residual was acceptable while the model error was $1.1\times10^{8}$.
- **Assuming a finite condition number.** The measured spectrum has ten eigenvalues at $10^{-19}$ or below; any iteration estimate that assumes $\kappa < \infty$ is fiction here.
- **Fixing the iteration count once.** The oracle stop moved from iteration 1 to iteration 7 as noise changed; the count is data dependent.
- **Confusing LSQR with CG on the normal equations.** Both are matrix-free and both semiconverge; LSQR is the numerically preferable form because it never forms $K^{\mathsf T}K$.
- **Believing a preconditioner regularises.** Section 6 measured identical limits; only the truncation biases the result.
