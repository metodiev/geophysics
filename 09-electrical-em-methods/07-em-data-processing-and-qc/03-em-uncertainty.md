# Quantifying Uncertainty in EM Interpretation

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Separate** the three sources of uncertainty in an EM interpretation —
   measurement noise, model error and prior choice — and **state** which of them
   each standard formula actually describes.
2. **Derive** the linearised parameter covariance from the sensitivity matrix and
   the data weights, and **interpret** its correlation matrix in geological terms.
3. **Measure** the coverage of a nominal 95% interval by Monte Carlo, and
   **decide** from that measurement whether the interval may be quoted.
4. **Construct** the profile (admissible-set) of a parameter with the others free,
   and **report** the interval rather than a single number.
5. **Quantify** the bias produced by a systematic error in gain or geometry and
   **express** it in units of the nominal standard error, showing that the
   interval cannot detect it.
6. **Explain** how a bounded parameter search turns a prior into an answer, and
   **demonstrate** that the admissible set, not the optimum, is the result.
7. **Write** a defensible uncertainty budget that assigns a term to each source
   and names the experiment that would test the largest one.

## 1. Intuition: three uncertainties, three tools

An interpreted EM result — "the second layer is at 8 m", "the plume has a
conductivity of 0.4 S/m" — carries an uncertainty that has at least three
independent parts, and they are not interchangeable.

- **Noise uncertainty.** Given the model and the data errors, how well is a
  parameter determined? This is what a covariance matrix reports. It shrinks as
  $\sqrt{N}$, and it is the only part that gets smaller if you measure longer.
- **Model uncertainty.** The data were generated by an Earth that is not in your
  parameterisation: three layers instead of two, a lateral gradient, a drift that
  was not linear. Fitting the wrong model produces a *bias* that the covariance
  does not know about. Only the comparison of an independent data set, or a
  synthetic study, can estimate it.
- **Prior and choice uncertainty.** Which parameter bounds, which regularisation,
  which equivalence class you report. This is not a nuisance; it is a real
  degree of freedom, and it is often larger than both of the others.

The lesson's claim, and the reason it is the last in the module, is this:
**a formally correct covariance matrix is a lower bound on the uncertainty, and
the size of the gap between that bound and the truth is measurable — but only
with a synthetic experiment in which the truth is known.**

All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**. The earth, the noise level,
the gain errors and the parameter bounds are chosen values.

## 2. The data and the sensitivity matrix

The synthetic earth is the module's two-layer model,
$\rho_1 = 25\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$, $\rho_2 = 500\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$,
$H = 8.0$ m, sounded with a Wenner array at twelve spacings from 1 m to 100 m
(module [09-07-01](01-em-noise-and-coupling.md) gives the exact forward model).
Each datum has a $3\%$ relative error:

$$\sigma_i = 0.03\,\rho_a(a_i) .$$

So the sounding is $\rho_a = 25.038 \pm 0.751\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$ at 1 m and
$246.42 \pm 7.39\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$ at 100 m. One realisation of the noise gives
the observed sounding

$$24.7,\ 25.5,\ 24.8,\ 27.9,\ 28.0,\ 35.9,\ 51.2,\ 74.3,\ 101.4,\ 146.2,\
182.2,\ 231.8\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}.$$

The sensitivity matrix, computed by central differences at the true model, is the
quantitative statement of what a sounding can do:

| $a$ (m) | $\partial\rho_a/\partial\rho_1$ | $\partial\rho_a/\partial\rho_2$ | $\partial\rho_a/\partial H$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | $+1.0013$ | $+0.0000$ | $-0.01423$ |
| 3.51 | $+1.0504$ | $+0.0004$ | $-0.49326$ |
| 12.33 | $+1.8305$ | $+0.0077$ | $-5.14554$ |
| 43.29 | $+4.2741$ | $+0.0670$ | $-13.26105$ |
| 100.00 | $+5.7254$ | $+0.2066$ | $-17.81101$ |

The 1 m spacing is a $\rho_1$ measurement and nothing else; the 100 m spacing
sees $\rho_2$ but its $H$ sensitivity is only $9\%$ larger than at 43 m. **The
information about $H$ and $\rho_2$ lives in the middle of the spacing range**,
which is why a sounding restricted to short spacings cannot measure $\rho_2$
(§6) and a sounding restricted to long ones cannot resolve $H$.

## 3. The linearised covariance

### 3.1 Derivation

Let $\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{f}(\mathbf{p}) + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ with
$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_i^{2}))$
and $\mathbf{J} = \partial\mathbf{f}/\partial\mathbf{p}$. Linearising about the
estimate $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ and weighting by $\mathbf{W} =
\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_i^{-2})$ gives the least-squares estimate

$$\hat{\mathbf{p}} = \mathbf{p}_0 +
(\mathbf{J}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{J})^{-1}
\mathbf{J}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}\,(\mathbf{d}-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{p}_0)),$$

and, because the estimator is linear in the data, its covariance is

$$\boxed{\;\mathbf{C} = (\mathbf{J}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{J})^{-1}\;},
\qquad \sigma_{p_j} = \sqrt{C_{jj}} .$$

Two assumptions are smuggled into that box and they are the whole subject of this
lesson: the model is **linear** over the region the data support, and the error
model $\mathbf{W}$ is **correct**. If either fails, $\mathbf{C}$ is a
lower bound of unknown tightness.

### 3.2 What the demonstration measures

Fitting the grid library and refining continuously gives
$\rho_1 = 24.812$, $\rho_2 = 424.85\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$, $H = 7.5439$ m with
$\chi^{2} = 12.54$ for 12 data and 3 parameters (expected $\approx9$). The
covariance at the true model gives

| parameter | value | $\sigma$ | 68% interval | 95% interval |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\rho_1$ ($\Omega$ m) | 25.000 | 0.3841 | [24.616, 25.384] | [24.247, 25.753] |
| $\rho_2$ ($\Omega$ m) | 500.000 | 39.5205 | [460.48, 539.52] | [422.54, 577.46] |
| $H$ (m) | 8.000 | 0.2582 | [7.742, 8.258] | [7.494, 8.506] |

and the correlation matrix

$$\mathrm{corr} = \begin{pmatrix}
1.000 & +0.169 & +0.663\\
+0.169 & 1.000 & +0.692\\
+0.663 & +0.692 & 1.000
\end{pmatrix}$$

with rows and columns in the order $(\rho_1, \rho_2, H)$.

**The correlations are the geological content of the covariance.** $\rho_2$ and
$H$ are correlated at $+0.692$: raising the half-space resistivity and thickening
the top layer remove each other's effect on the sounding, because the two
together control the depth at which the current starts to sense the half-space.
$\rho_1$ and $H$ are correlated at $+0.663$ for the same reason at the shallow
end. Reporting $H = 7.54 \pm 0.26$ m with no mention of the fact that a
$\rho_2$ two standard errors higher would move $H$ by a comparable amount is
therefore an incomplete statement — and the correlation, not the standard error,
is the part a reader needs.

### 3.3 The misfit valley, directly

Computing the best $H$ and the misfit for a sequence of fixed $\rho_2$:

| $\rho_2$ ($\Omega$ m) | best $H$ (m) | $\chi^{2}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 400.0 | 7.713 | 11.538 |
| 450.0 | 7.713 | 2.440 |
| 500.0 | 8.051 | 0.182 |
| 550.0 | 8.051 | 2.624 |
| 625.0 | 8.404 | 6.877 |

Both the value and the misfit move: $\rho_2$ from 400 to 625 changes the best
$H$ by 0.7 m and the misfit by 11.4, i.e. from "clearly unacceptable" to
"perfectly acceptable" and back. The valley is not a line but a shallow trough,
and its width is what an interval should report.

## 4. Monte Carlo and measured coverage

The grid search plus refinement is repeated for 200 noise realisations. The
empirical distributions of the estimates give

| parameter | linearised $2\times1.96\sigma$ | Monte Carlo 2.5–97.5% | skewness |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\rho_1$ | 1.506 | [24.172, 25.626] | $-0.400$ |
| $\rho_2$ | 154.920 | [435.964, 587.696] | $+0.386$ |
| $H$ | 1.012 | [7.528, 8.496] | $+0.189$ |

The empirical interval widths agree with the linearised ones to within about
$10\%$ — the problem is nearly linear in these parameters over the region the
data support — and the empirical $\rho_2$–$H$ correlation is $+0.7405$ against
the linearised $+0.6920$, a $7\%$ discrepancy in the same direction as the
skewness.

Now the measurement that justifies the whole section. For each realisation, check
whether the **true** value lies inside the linearised 95% interval:

| parameter | measured coverage | nominal |
|---|---|---|
| $\rho_1$ | 0.945 | 0.950 |
| $\rho_2$ | 0.945 | 0.950 |
| $H$ | 0.970 | 0.950 |

With 200 realisations the binomial standard error on a coverage estimate is
$\sqrt{0.95\cdot0.05/200} = 1.5\%$, so $0.945$ and $0.970$ are both within about
$1.3$ standard errors of nominal. The interval is **calibrated for this problem**,
and — this is the operative point — that is a *measured* statement, not an
assumption. An earlier version of the same experiment, with a coarse parameter
grid and no continuous refinement, gave coverages *well below* nominal — a
deficit of five to fifteen percentage points on the same three parameters. The
apparent "non-linearity" was the grid quantisation, which exceeded $\sigma_H$ and
therefore quantised the estimator more coarsely than the interval it was being
tested against. A coverage test is only as good as the estimator it tests.

## 5. Systematic error: the interval cannot see it

Add a systematic error to the data — a gain error, in which the whole sounding is
multiplied by $(1+g)$, and a geometry error, in which the true spacing is
$(1+e)$ times the value used in the inversion. Both use the *same* noise
realisation, so the trend with $g$ and $e$ is not confounded with scatter. The
measured biases, in units of the nominal $\sigma$:

| systematic | value | $\rho_1$ bias$/\sigma$ | $\rho_2$ bias$/\sigma$ | $H$ bias$/\sigma$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| gain | 0.000 | $+0.90$ | $+0.07$ | $+0.23$ |
| gain | 0.010 | $+1.55$ | $+0.20$ | $+0.22$ |
| gain | 0.020 | $+2.20$ | $+0.33$ | $+0.22$ |
| gain | 0.030 | $+2.85$ | $+0.45$ | $+0.22$ |
| gain | 0.050 | $+4.16$ | $+0.70$ | $+0.22$ |
| spacing | 0.010 | $+0.90$ | $+0.07$ | $-0.08$ |
| spacing | 0.030 | $+0.91$ | $+0.07$ | $-0.68$ |
| spacing | 0.050 | $+0.91$ | $+0.07$ | $-1.25$ |

A $3\%$ gain error puts $\rho_1$ $2.85\sigma$ from its true value: the nominally
95% interval ($\pm1.96\sigma$) **excludes** the truth, and it does so for a
perfectly measured, perfectly inverted sounding. A $5\%$ spacing error does the
same to $H$ at $-1.25\sigma$ — not yet excluding, but of the same order as the
noise interval.

Two properties make this failure dangerous rather than merely annoying:

- **It is invisible in any single sounding.** The misfit of the biased fit is
  fine; there is no residual pattern to see, because a systematic error is not a
  random one.
- **It does not shrink.** More repeats, longer stacking or a better receiver
  reduces $\sigma$ and leaves the bias unchanged, so *the better your data, the
  more significant the systematic error becomes.*

The only defences are independent: measure the gain by injecting a known current,
measure the spacings with a tape, and — best — fit the bias itself by comparing
results from two geometries.

## 6. The admissible set, and the prior that is always there

Now restrict the observation to $a\le20$ m: a shallow sounding with 8 data. The
longest spacing no longer approaches $\rho_2$, and the sensitivity matrix of §2
says exactly what that costs. Profiling the misfit — for each $\rho_2$, minimise
over $\rho_1$ and $H$ — gives

| $\rho_2$ ($\Omega$ m) | best $H$ (m) | $\chi^{2}$ | $\Delta\chi^{2}$ | admitted at 95%? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 151.7 | 6.223 | 17.88 | 9.26 | no |
| 186.8 | 6.496 | 14.29 | 5.66 | yes |
| 246.6 | 7.389 | 11.15 | 2.52 | yes |
| 429.8 | 8.051 | 8.99 | 0.36 | yes |
| 698.8 | 8.404 | 9.30 | 0.68 | yes |
| 1217.9 | 8.773 | 9.35 | 0.72 | yes |

The set admitted at 95% ($\Delta\chi^{2}<5.99$ for one parameter) runs from
$186.8$ to $1500\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$ — **a factor of 8 in $\rho_2$**, over which
the best-fit $H$ slides from 6.5 m to 8.8 m. Every one of those models is a
defensible answer to this data set.

This is where the prior enters, whether or not the analyst admits it. Any
inversion has a search range; the range is a prior. If the range is narrower than
the admissible set, the reported value is where the prior stops the search rather
than where the data stop it. The honest report is the interval — and, when the
interval spans a factor of 8, the honest conclusion is that the survey did not
measure $\rho_2$ at all, whatever the optimum says.

The same argument is why the demonstration resists reporting "$H = 7.54$ m": the
number is one member of an elongated ridge of acceptable models, and the ridge is
the result.

## 7. An uncertainty budget

Collecting the terms measured in this lesson for the $\rho_2$ and $H$ of the
full 1–100 m sounding:

| source | magnitude | how it was obtained | does more data help? |
|---|---|---|---|
| noise on $\rho_2$ | $\pm39.5\ \Omega$ m (7.9%) | covariance, coverage-checked at 0.945 | yes, as $1/\sqrt{N}$ |
| noise on $H$ | $\pm0.258$ m (3.2%) | covariance, coverage 0.970 | yes |
| correlation $\rho_2$–$H$ | $+0.69$ | covariance | no — it is a property of the geometry |
| gain error 3% | $+0.45\sigma$ on $\rho_2$, $+0.22\sigma$ on $H$ | measured, §5 | **no** |
| spacing error 5% | $-1.25\sigma$ on $H$ | measured, §5 | **no** |
| processing bias | up to a few per cent on the earliest gate | measured in module [09-07-02](02-em-processing-workflow.md) | no |
| model error (2 layers for a 3-layer Earth) | large and positive-definite | not measurable from the data alone | no |
| prior / search bounds | dominates when the interval is wide | profile, §6 | no |

The table is the deliverable of this lesson. A reader who receives it can see
which term to attack: if the noise terms dominate, measure more or design a better
array; if a systematic term dominates, calibrate; if the interval is wide because
the geometry is wrong, change the survey, because no amount of processing will
help.

## 8. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Gaussian, independent data errors.** The covariance formula assumes they
   are; if the errors are correlated (which they are when a common calibration or
   a common drift is present) then $\mathbf{W}$ must be the inverse of the full
   error covariance, and the naive diagonal form understates $\sigma$.
2. **A linear model over the confidence region.** Checked here by coverage; at
   larger contrasts, or with a parameter near a physical bound, it fails and the
   Monte Carlo interval must be used instead.
3. **A correct forward parametrisation.** The two-layer model is exact for the
   synthetic data by construction, so this lesson's *model* error is zero — which
   is precisely why the bootstrap and covariance agree. On real data the largest
   term in the budget is usually the one this lesson deliberately switched off.
4. **Known data errors.** $\sigma = 3\%$ was known. Estimating $\sigma$ from the
   residual of the same fit introduces a factor
   $\sqrt{n_{\text{data}}/(n_{\text{data}}-n_{\text{par}})}$; with 12 data and 3
   parameters that correction is $1.15$, i.e. $15\%$ on every $\sigma$.
5. **Continuous parameters.** The grid was refined; a grid-only estimate
   quantises the interval and destroys the coverage, as measured in §4.
6. **An honest search range.** The profile is only as wide as the range scanned.

## 9. Worked examples

### Example 1 — does the interval contain the truth?

The demonstration's own realisation gives $\rho_1 = 24.812$ against
$\sigma_{\rho_1} = 0.3841$: a deviation of $-0.49\sigma$, comfortably inside.
$H = 7.5439$ against $\sigma_H = 0.2582$ is $-1.77\sigma$ — inside the 95%
interval ($1.96\sigma$) but on its edge. With 12 data and 3 parameters a
$\chi^{2}$ of 12.54 against an expectation of 9 is a mild excess; this particular
realisation is a slightly unlucky one, and the covariances say exactly how
unlucky.

### Example 2 — the cost of estimating the error level

If the $3\%$ error level were estimated from the residual of the fit rather than
known, every $\sigma$ would grow by
$\sqrt{12/(12-3)} = \sqrt{1.333} = 1.155$. The 95% interval on $H$ would widen
from $[7.494, 8.506]$ to $[7.415, 8.585]$. This correction is small here because
$n_{\text{par}}=3$ is small; for a 20-parameter layered inversion with 30 data it
is a factor $\sqrt{30/10} = 1.73$, and forgetting it is one of the commonest
errors in published intervals.

### Example 3 — how much does the prior matter?

Take the shallow sounding of §6, whose admissible $\rho_2$ range is
$186.8$–$1500\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$ (a factor 8.0). If the report quotes
$\rho_2 = 430\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$ with the linearised $\sigma$, that $\sigma$ was
computed at a model whose misfit is 0.36 above the best — and the same $\sigma$
attaches to $\rho_2 = 187$ and to $\rho_2 = 1218$, because the likelihood is flat
over the ridge. The number and the error bar are both properties of the chosen
member, not of the data. The profile is the only report that does not depend on
the choice.

## 12. Common mistakes

1. **Quoting a covariance as "the uncertainty".** It is the noise term only.
2. **Reporting an optimum without the admissible set.** The optimum is one member
   of a ridge.
3. **Assuming an interval is calibrated because the formula is standard.** Measure
   the coverage on a synthetic.
4. **Forgetting the correlation.** A parameter at $1\sigma$ with a strongly
   correlated partner is not "known to $1\sigma$".
5. **Letting the search bounds decide the answer silently.** State the range.
6. **Believing a small residual.** It is a statement about the noise model, not
   about the model.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Systematic errors trade for significance.** Better data make a bias more
   significant, not less.
2. **Symmetrising a skewed interval.** $\rho_2$'s Monte Carlo skewness here is
   $+0.386$; the linearised interval is honest only because the problem is
   nearly linear.
3. **Ignoring the parameters you fixed.** Fixing $\rho_1$ would shrink
   $\sigma_H$; the shrinkage is a prior, not information.
4. **Treating model error as noise.** It does not average down and it has a sign.
5. **Publishing a number without the range searched.** The range is part of the
   result.
