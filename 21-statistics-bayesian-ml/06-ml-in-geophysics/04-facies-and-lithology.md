# Facies and Lithology Prediction from Well Logs

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **State** what the label of a facies classifier is, why it is categorical
   rather than measured, and what happens when the description scheme changes
   between wells.
2. **Construct** a nearest-centroid classifier as a Gaussian maximum-likelihood
   rule and **explain** what its pooled covariance assumption means physically.
3. **Distinguish** a shuffled sample split, a bed-held-out split and a
   depth split, and **state** the question each one answers.
4. **Compute** overall accuracy, per-class recall and the confusion that hides
   behind a high accuracy on an unbalanced section.
5. **Diagnose** a distribution shift in the log response from a per-class recall
   table rather than from an aggregate score.
6. **Interpret** a model that scores 0.95 overall and 0.60 on the class that
   matters, and **explain** why the two numbers are both correct.
7. **Specify** the reporting requirements for a facies model: log suite and
   units, depth interval, description scheme, split definition, confusion
   matrix, and the depth range over which the model was fitted.

All logs, beds, facies and accuracies below are **SYNTHETIC**, generated by
[`code/facies_and_lithology_demo.py`](./code/facies_and_lithology_demo.py). No
well, core description or published log response is read or quoted.

## 1. Intuition: the label is a description, not a measurement

Gamma-ray, density and neutron logs are measurements with units: API, g/cm³ and
v/v. Facies are not. A facies label is a **human description** of a rock volume,
made from core, cuttings or a mud log, and it belongs to one of a scheme's
classes. Two consequences follow, and they shape everything else:

- The classifier is solving a **mapping to a scheme**, not recovering a physical
  property. If the scheme changes (a "shaly sand" class appears, or the
  carbonate classes are merged), the labels change and the model must be
  retrained, even though the rocks did not.
- The labels are uncertain in a way the logs are not. Core-to-log depth
  mismatches of a few decimetres are routine, and a bed thinner than the logging
  tool's vertical resolution cannot be labelled at all.

Logs also drift. The demonstration makes that concrete: over 300 m of section the
sand's gamma ray rises from 70 to 90 API while the shale's rises from 95 to
105 API, and the density and neutron responses migrate with it. A model fitted
in the shallow interval is then applied, as it must be, to a section whose
statistics differ.

## 2. The synthetic well

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| samples | 3 000, spacing 0.1 m, total 300 m |
| beds after median filtering | 254 |
| GR noise (1 s.d.) | 10.0 API |
| RHOB noise (1 s.d.) | 0.050 g/cm³ |
| NPHI noise (1 s.d.) | 0.040 v/v |
| facies proportions, shallow third | sand 0.369, shale 0.531, carbonate 0.100 |
| facies proportions, deep third | sand 0.047, shale 0.950, carbonate 0.003 |

Downhole the sand becomes shaly — its response migrates towards the shallow
shale signature (GR 70 → 90 API, RHOB 2.32 → 2.48 g/cm³, NPHI 0.24 → 0.32 v/v)
— and shale takes over the section. This is the drift that makes a depth split
different from a shuffled split, and it is a caricature of a real shaly-sand
problem rather than an invention: the class means themselves move.

## 3. Three splits, three questions

| Split | Training | Test | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| shuffled samples | 2 100 samples | 900 samples | can the model interpolate within the same interval? |
| whole beds held out | 2 128 samples | 872 samples | does it generalise to beds it has never seen? |
| depth split | 900 samples above 90 m | 2 100 samples below | does it work where the rocks and proportions changed? |

Measured overall accuracy:

| Model | shuffled | whole beds | depth split |
|---|---|---|---|
| nearest centroid (Gaussian ML) | 0.959 | 0.961 | 0.961 |
| decision tree (depth 4) | 0.950 | 0.950 | 0.951 |

All three splits give the same answer to two decimal places. That is itself a
result: with 254 beds of a few decimetres, the within-bed correlation that a
sample-level split leaks is not large enough to move a 900-sample score. The
honest reading is that the leak is real but here it is smaller than the sampling
error of the metric — not that a shuffled split is safe.

The reason the metric does not move is the one that matters, and it is in the
next section.

## 4. Why overall accuracy is the wrong summary here

Per-class recall on the deep test interval (share of the interval in brackets):

| Class | share of test interval | nearest centroid recall | tree recall |
|---|---|---|---|
| sand | 0.089 | 0.608 | 0.597 |
| shale | 0.892 | 0.995 | 0.986 |
| carbonate | 0.019 | 1.000 | 0.975 |

Accuracy is a *weighted* average of recall with the class proportions as
weights. When 89.2% of the interval is shale and shale recall is 0.99, the
overall number is dominated by the majority class: a model that never found a
sand body would still score about 0.94 here. The sand recall of 0.60 is the
result, and it is invisible in every headline number the demonstration prints in
§3.

Two lessons follow, and both generalise far beyond well logs:

1. **Report the confusion matrix, not the accuracy,** whenever the class
   proportions are unbalanced — which is always in stratigraphy.
2. **Accuracy in a shifted interval is a misleadingly stable metric** because the
   shift itself changes the weights. The deep interval is 89% shale, so a model
   that has become worse at sand automatically looks better; the two effects
   partly cancel and the aggregate barely moves.

## 5. What the drift is doing to the model

The nearest centroid rule assigns a sample to the class whose standardised
distance is smallest. Downhole the sand response moves *towards* the shallow
shale centroid, so deep sand samples land closer to the (shallow) shale centroid
than to the sand centroid — a misclassification that is deterministic, not
scatter. The tree has the same problem in a different form: its splits were
placed at shallow thresholds, and deep sand crosses them, so it is partitioned
into the shale leaf. Both models therefore have a shale-favouring bias in the
deep interval, and both look fine overall because shale is the dominant class.

The remedy is not a larger model. It is one of:

1. **A depth-aware feature** — include depth (or a trend) so the model can move
   its decision boundary downhole; this is often the single highest-value change.
2. **A normalisation that removes the drift** — for example use ratios or
   differences between logs rather than absolute values, if the drift is common
   to all of them.
3. **Per-zone models** — fit and report separately for intervals whose
   statistics differ, and state how the zones were chosen (a fixed criterion
   decided before looking at the score).

## 6. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **The description scheme is fixed and consistent.** Merging or splitting
   classes between wells makes the label definition well-dependent.
2. **Depth is correct.** Core-to-log shifts of a few samples are routine and
   inflate the apparent error of any model scored against core.
3. **The tool response is stable.** Tool drift, hole conditions and invasion all
   change the measured logs without changing the rock.
4. **Beds are thicker than the tool resolution.** A model cannot label what the
   tool could not resolve; the demonstration's beds are 0.5 m and thicker.
5. **The training interval contains every class.** A class absent from training
   will be predicted as something else, and its recall will be zero — which is
   the extreme form of the imbalance problem in §4.
6. **The proportion in the training interval matches the deployment interval.**
   It does not here, and that is the subject of §4.

## The honest summary

On a synthetic 300 m well whose sand becomes progressively shalier downhole, a
nearest-centroid classifier and a depth-4 decision tree both score 0.95–0.96
overall accuracy whether the split is by sample, by bed or by depth — and the
same models recover only 0.60 of the deep sand, while shale recall is 0.99 on an
interval that is 89% shale. The aggregate metric is stable because the class
weights absorb the failure; the per-class recall is where the drift shows. The
transferable conclusions are: report the confusion matrix, choose the split that
matches the deployment geometry, expect the log response itself to move with
depth, and treat a class-mean shift as a modelling problem to be stated rather
than a data problem to be averaged away.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Reporting accuracy on an unbalanced section.** Shale alone scores about
   0.89 on the deep interval.
2. **Shuffling samples from the same bed.** The leak exists; here it is smaller
   than the metric's sampling error, which is not a reason to keep doing it.
3. **Treating a class-mean drift as noise.** It is deterministic, it moves the
   decision boundary, and it is invisible in aggregate scores.
4. **Comparing models on overall accuracy only.** Both models here are within
   0.001 overall and differ in which class they lose.
5. **Assuming the training proportions hold at the well.** A model carries its
   training prior; state it.
6. **Scoring against core without a depth-matching check.** A one-sample shift
   can dominate the error budget of a good model.
