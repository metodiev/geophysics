# Pitfalls: Spurious Correlations, Non-Stationarity, Extrapolation

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Quantify** the spurious correlation of two independent random walks as a
   function of record length, and **state** the transformation that removes it.
2. **Distinguish** a distributional shift from a structural one, and **predict**
   which models survive each.
3. **Measure** the RMSE inflation caused by a non-stationary mean shift, and
   **explain** why no validation scheme inside the training period can detect it.
4. **Demonstrate** that a model can be excellent in-sample and useless out of range,
   using a polynomial that fits a saturating function.
5. **Compute** the inflation of cross-validated performance caused by performing
   feature selection on the full data set, and **state** which preprocessing steps
   must move inside the fold.
6. **Design** a validation protocol that would have caught each of the four
   pitfalls, and **state** the cost of the discipline.
7. **Identify** the geophysical situations where each pitfall is most likely:
   trending time series, changing instrumentation, sparse target classes and
   high-dimensional evidence layers.

Every series, matrix and error below is **SYNTHETIC**, generated by
[`code/ml_pitfalls_geophysics_demo.py`](./code/ml_pitfalls_geophysics_demo.py). No
measured data set is read or quoted anywhere.

## 1. Intuition: four ways to be wrong while every diagnostic looks fine

The four pitfalls in this lesson share a structure. Each one produces a number
that is *correct for the computation performed* and wrong for the question asked,
and each one is invisible in the metric that a hurried paper reports:

| Pitfall | Metric that looks fine | Question it fails to answer |
|---|---|---|
| spurious correlation | $r$, $R^2$, a $p$-value | is the relation physical or a shared trend? |
| non-stationarity | validation inside the training period | will it work after the regime changes? |
| extrapolation | in-range hold-out RMSE | what happens outside the range? |
| selection leakage | cross-validated score | how would it perform on data never seen? |

The remedy for all four is a **split that matches the deployment geometry**, and
the cost of that discipline is that the honest number is always worse. That is the
trade this lesson asks you to accept with your eyes open.

## 2. Spurious correlation between trending series

Two independent random walks of 100 samples, over 1 000 replications:

| statistic | levels | first differences | reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| mean $\lvert r\rvert$ | $0.420$ | $0.082$ | $1/\sqrt{n} = 0.100$ |
| fraction with $\lvert r\rvert > 0.5$ | $0.383$ | $0.000$ | — |
| fraction with $\lvert r\rvert > 0.8$ | $0.090$ | $0.000$ | — |

The mechanism is not subtle: a random walk is a sum of random increments, so its
variance grows with time and its low-frequency content dominates the squared
correlation. Two independent walks therefore *look* related because both wander.
Nearly 40% of independent pairs reach $\lvert r\rvert > 0.5$, and 9% reach 0.8 —
which is to say that a correlation of 0.8 between two trending geophysical series
is not evidence of anything.

**What removes it.** Difference the series: on increments the mean $\lvert r\rvert$
is $0.082$, below the $1/\sqrt{n} = 0.100$ that independent data give (the
difference operator induces a small negative correlation). Alternatively detrend
both series, use a cointegration test rather than a correlation, or model the
trend explicitly with the trend's uncertainty reported. The choice must be stated,
because the two analyses give different answers about the same data: one finds a
strong relation, the other finds none.

## 3. Non-stationarity: the failure no internal validation can see

Train a predictor on the first part of a series and test it on the next, when the
mean has shifted:

| process | measured RMSE | bias | expectation |
|---|---|---|---|
| stationary | $1.073$ K | $+0.048$ K | $1.000$ K |
| mean shifted by 2 K | $2.227$ K | $+2.015$ K | $2.236$ K |

The inflation factor is $2.1$, and it is entirely a bias: the model learned the
training mean, which is now wrong. Note that the *scatter* is unchanged — the
model is not worse at predicting fluctuations, it is predicting a level that no
longer exists.

Two consequences:

1. **Cross-validation inside the training period cannot detect this.** Any
   random fold, and even a temporal fold, as long as it stays inside a period
   whose mean is stable, returns the stationary answer. Only a split that spans
   the change does, and the split must therefore be chosen from the physics of the
   system (a policy change, an instrument change, a seasonal regime) rather than
   from the row order.
2. **The diagnostic is a residual-versus-time plot with a fitted level**, not a
   summary statistic. The measured $+2.015$ K bias is a horizontal line in that
   plot; no RMSE alone reveals it.

## 4. Extrapolation: fitted where the data were, applied where they were not

Truth $y = \tanh(2x)$ with 0.02 of noise, fitted on $x \in [0,1]$ and evaluated
on $x \in [1, 1.5]$:

| polynomial degree | RMSE inside $[0,1]$ | RMSE outside $[1,1.5]$ | max $\lvert$error$\rvert$ outside |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $0.0910$ | $0.4000$ | $0.603$ |
| 3 | $0.0206$ | $0.0639$ | $0.140$ |
| 5 | $0.0202$ | $0.2995$ | $0.789$ |

Three readings, all useful.

- **Every fit is validated inside the interval where it was fitted.** The
  in-sample RMSE says nothing about the answer to the question asked, which is
  what happens outside.
- **More capacity is not monotonically better outside.** Degree 5 is no better
  than degree 3 inside ($0.0202$ versus $0.0206$) and five times worse outside
  ($0.2995$ versus $0.0639$): it spends its extra flexibility on curvature the
  data cannot constrain, and that curvature is unbounded beyond the range.
- **The failure is a shape error, not noise.** The maximum error outside is
  $0.789$ for degree 5 against a noise level of $0.02$ — forty times the noise.

The geophysical version is a model trained on one range of a controlling variable
and applied to another: magnitude, depth, epicentral distance, temperature,
salinity, sea level. The rule is to **state the range and refuse to extrapolate
without a stated reason to believe the functional form**, and to prefer forms
whose behaviour outside the range is physically bounded (saturation, monotone
limits) over polynomials.

## 5. Selection leakage: choosing features before splitting

Fifty samples, 200 pure-noise features, a target independent of all of them:

| procedure | cross-validated $R^2$ |
|---|---|
| select the 10 best features on all data, then cross-validate | $+0.602$ |
| select the 10 best features inside each fold, then cross-validate | $-0.271$ |

There is no signal. The leaky procedure nonetheless reports $R^2 = +0.602$,
because the selection step looked at the test fold: features were chosen partly
because they correlate with the target *in the test data*, and the subsequent
cross-validation measures how well that accidental correlation is remembered. The
honest value is $-0.271$, which is what $R^2$ looks like when a model fitted on
noise is applied to fresh noise (it is worse than predicting the mean, so $R^2$ is
negative).

The leak is worth $0.873$ in $R^2$ — on a problem with no signal at all. In a real
prospectivity or seismic-attribute study with hundreds of candidate layers and
tens of labels, the same mechanism is what produces the impressive numbers.

**Everything that touches the labels or the data distribution belongs inside the
fold:**

- feature selection and feature construction (ratios, principal components);
- standardisation, centring and normalisation;
- imputation of missing values;
- outlier removal and quality-control thresholds;
- hyper-parameter choice and model selection;
- any transformation fitted on the pooled data, including a variogram or a
  covariance model used in a subsequent inversion.

The cost is honest: with a small data set, in-fold selection is noisy, so the
reported score is lower *and* more variable. That variability is the true
uncertainty of a small-data study, and hiding it is the whole point of the leak.

## 6. How each pitfall is caught

| Pitfall | Test that catches it | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| spurious correlation | difference or detrend; use a cointegration test | the relation usually disappears, and the paper with it |
| non-stationarity | temporal split spanning a known regime change | fewer training data, and a worse headline number |
| extrapolation | test at the edge of the range and beyond it | the model fails, visibly, and the range must be stated |
| selection leakage | nested cross-validation with all preprocessing inside the fold | a lower and noisier reported score |

## 7. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **The generator stands in for a real series.** A random walk and an AR(1)
   process are caricatures; real geophysical series have seasonality, coloured
   noise and instrument changes.
2. **The leaks are additive.** In practice a study may have all four at once, and
   their contributions cannot be separated from a single reported number.
3. **The metrics are the standard ones.** Every measurement here uses RMSE,
   $R^2$ or Pearson correlation; the corresponding pitfalls for ranking metrics
   (AUC) and for classification appear in
   [21-06-04](./04-facies-and-lithology.md) and
   [21-06-06](./06-mineral-prospectivity-ml.md).
4. **The regimes are known.** The non-stationarity experiment assumes the shift is
   *retrospectively* identifiable; in a live study, the whole difficulty is that
   the regime change is not yet visible.
5. **Uncertainty is reported with the number.** A leak-inflated $R^2$ of $0.602$
   and an honest $-0.271$ differ far more than any confidence interval at these
   sample sizes.

## The honest summary

Four synthetic experiments, one lesson each. Two independent random walks reach
$\lvert r\rvert > 0.5$ in $38.3\%$ of 100-sample realisations and $\lvert r\rvert
> 0.8$ in $9.0\%$, while their increments correlate at $0.082$. A 2 K mean shift
inflates prediction RMSE from $1.073$ K to $2.227$ K through a bias of $+2.015$ K
that no in-period validation can see. A degree-5 polynomial that fits
$\tanh(2x)$ to $0.0202$ RMSE inside its range errs by up to $0.789$ outside it,
forty times the noise level. And selecting features on the full data set turns an
$R^2$ of $-0.271$ into $+0.602$ on data with no signal whatsoever. The transferable
conclusions are not the numbers but the tests: difference or detrend trending
series; split across the regime change, not across rows; state and respect the
fitted range; and put every data-dependent step inside the fold.

Without notes:

1. Explain why two independent random walks correlate, state the measured
   fractions, and name the transformation that removes the effect.
2. A model has an in-period cross-validated RMSE of 1.07 K and a post-shift RMSE
   of 2.23 K with a bias of +2.02 K. State the diagnosis and the split that would
   have exposed it.
3. Degree 3 and degree 5 fit the training range equally well and differ by a
   factor of 4.7 outside it. State the principle this illustrates and the
   polynomial-free alternative.
4. Your colleague selects the best 5 of 300 attribute volumes by their correlation
   with production, then cross-validates the resulting model and reports
   $R^2 = 0.5$. State the diagnosis, the corrected procedure, and the direction in
   which the honest number will move.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Correlating two trending series.** Nearly 40% of independent pairs exceed
   $\lvert r\rvert = 0.5$.
2. **Validating a non-stationary process inside one regime.** The measured
   inflation here is $2.1\times$ and none of it appears in an in-period score.
3. **Reporting in-range hold-out error as evidence of generalisation.** Out of
   range, the degree-5 fit is wrong by 40 times the noise.
4. **Choosing features before splitting.** $+0.873$ of $R^2$ manufactured from
   nothing.
5. **Standardising, imputing or outlier-filtering on the pooled data.** Each is
   the same leak in a different costume.
6. **Reading a negative cross-validated $R^2$ as a bug.** $-0.271$ is the correct
   answer when there is no signal.

## Where this is used

- **Time-series forecasting of geodetic, hydrological and climate records**, where
  stationarity is the central assumption
  ([20-08-03](../../20-signal-processing/08-timeseries-analysis-geophysics/03-geodetic-time-series.md)).
- **Attribute-to-property regression in reservoir characterisation**, where
  hundreds of candidate attributes invite selection leakage.
- **Hazard and susceptibility mapping**, where labels are few and evidence layers
  numerous.
- **Instrument-network change studies**, where the regime change is an
  instrument upgrade and the shift is a calibration offset.
- **Subsurface property prediction from remote sensing**, where train-here,
  predict-there is the normal mode of use.
