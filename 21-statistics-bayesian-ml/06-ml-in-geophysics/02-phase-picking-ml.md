# Deep-Learning Phase Picking: SeisBench and Beyond

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Define** a phase pick as a time estimate with a stated tolerance, and
   distinguish it from the window-level *detection* label of
   [21-06-01](./01-seismic-ml.md).
2. **Construct** three label types for the same record — binary window, per
   sample, and offset regression — and **predict** the bias each one induces.
3. **Measure** the pick error of a threshold-crossing picker and of a learned
   window classifier on the same synthetic records, reporting bias, mean
   absolute error and the fraction of picks inside a stated tolerance.
4. **Explain** why the learned window classifier in this experiment is *worse*
   than the classical picker, and identify the label definition as the cause
   rather than the model.
5. **Compare** pick-error statistics at two signal-to-noise ratios and state how
   the ranking of the two pickers changes.
6. **State** what a pretrained-model toolbox such as SeisBench provides and what
   it does not: generalisation evidence, a fixed label convention, and the
   transfer problem of applying it to a new network.
7. **Specify** the reporting requirements for a published picker: tolerance,
   residual distribution, signal-to-noise definition and the reference labels.

Every record and pick statistic below is **SYNTHETIC**, generated by
[`code/phase_picking_ml_demo.py`](./code/phase_picking_ml_demo.py). No waveform
archive, no pick catalogue and no published pick-error distribution is used.

## 1. Intuition: a pick is a number with a tolerance

Detection asks a yes/no question. Picking asks a *where* question: at what time
does the P (or S) arrival start? A pick is therefore useful only together with
two numbers that are almost always omitted from abstracts:

- the **tolerance** at which it counts as correct (for example $\pm0.05\
  \mathrm{s}$);
- the **error statistic** at that tolerance (the fraction inside it, and the
  distribution outside it).

The demonstration makes this concrete by scoring the *same* picks at three
tolerances. A method described as "0.90 accurate" may be exactly the same method
described as "0.35 accurate", with only the tolerance changed. Neither number is
wrong; the one without the other is.

A second intuition matters as much: picking is a **regression** problem in
disguise, and the label convention decides the bias. If training windows are
labelled "contains the onset", the model has no incentive to be precise inside
the window, and it will fire on the earliest part of the labelled span.
Section 4 measures that bias: $-0.180\ \mathrm{s}$ at a comfortable
signal-to-noise ratio, larger than any tolerance the downstream network would
accept.

## 2. The synthetic records and the two pickers

Each record is 300 samples at 100 Hz (3.0 s) of Gaussian noise plus a single
8 Hz Ricker wavelet whose peak occurs at sample 150 (1.50 s). The wavelet peak
amplitude is set so that peak-over-RMS equals $3.16$ (10 dB) or $1.58$ (4 dB).

Two pickers are compared:

1. **STA/LTA first crossing.** Compute the short-term (20 sample) over long-term
   (160 sample) mean-square ratio and take the first sample above 3.0; if the
   ratio never crosses, take its maximum. This is the classical picker that a
   field analyst would use as a reference.
2. **Learned window classifier.** Slide a 20-sample window with stride 1, label
   it 1 if it *contains* the onset sample and 0 otherwise, extract four features
   (window mean square, maximum STA/LTA inside the window, a peakedness
   statistic, and the 4–12 Hz to 0.5–2 Hz energy ratio), fit a logistic
   regression on 60 records, and pick the first window whose probability is at
   least 0.5.

Both pickers are trained or calibrated on 60 records and scored on 200
independent records at each signal-to-noise ratio. The labelled windows are
$7.1\%$ positive: 1 200 of 16 860.

## 3. Measured pick error at 10 dB

Peak-over-RMS $3.16$. The true pick is sample 150, so every error below is in
seconds relative to 1.50 s.

| Statistic | STA/LTA crossing | learned window classifier |
|---|---|---|
| bias (mean signed error) | $+0.002\ \mathrm{s}$ | $-0.180\ \mathrm{s}$ |
| mean absolute error | $0.342\ \mathrm{s}$ | $0.568\ \mathrm{s}$ |
| scatter (1 s.d.) | $0.635\ \mathrm{s}$ | $0.777\ \mathrm{s}$ |
| within $\pm0.02\ \mathrm{s}$ | $0.150$ | $0.005$ |
| within $\pm0.05\ \mathrm{s}$ | $0.475$ | $0.090$ |
| within $\pm0.10\ \mathrm{s}$ | $0.740$ | $0.345$ |

Two things are worth stating plainly.

First, **the learned classifier is worse**, on every statistic, and the reason is
the label, not the model. Its label says "the onset is somewhere in this 0.20 s
window"; nothing in it rewards a pick at the wavelet peak. It therefore fires as
soon as the leading edge enters the window, which is the measured
$-0.180\ \mathrm{s}$ bias — about the width of the label window minus the true
onset's offset inside it.

Second, **the scatter is large for both**. A 0.635 s standard deviation at 10 dB
is not a usable P pick for relative location; the record is short, the event is
one dominant cycle, and the only information about the arrival is a
low-frequency waveform. The demonstration deliberately uses a hard regime: the
result to take away is the *reporting structure*, not these particular numbers.

## 4. Measured pick error at 4 dB

Peak-over-RMS $1.58$, a factor of two weaker.

| Statistic | STA/LTA crossing | learned window classifier |
|---|---|---|
| bias | $+0.061\ \mathrm{s}$ | $+0.029\ \mathrm{s}$ |
| mean absolute error | $0.927\ \mathrm{s}$ | $0.917\ \mathrm{s}$ |
| scatter (1 s.d.) | $1.062\ \mathrm{s}$ | $1.040\ \mathrm{s}$ |
| within $\pm0.02\ \mathrm{s}$ | $0.015$ | $0.005$ |
| within $\pm0.05\ \mathrm{s}$ | $0.060$ | $0.045$ |
| within $\pm0.10\ \mathrm{s}$ | $0.180$ | $0.135$ |

At 4 dB the two pickers converge: MAE $0.927$ versus $0.917\ \mathrm{s}$, within
$1\%$ of each other. The regime, not the method, dominates. A method comparison
run at a single signal-to-noise ratio is therefore a statement about that regime;
the honest figure is a pair of curves (error versus peak-over-RMS), which is also
the reason benchmarks report error as a function of magnitude and distance
rather than a single number.

Note the bias reversal: the STA/LTA picker is *late* at 4 dB ($+0.061\
\mathrm{s}$) because the ratio needs to build up before crossing, and the learned
classifier's early bias shrinks to $+0.029\ \mathrm{s}$ because at low
signal-to-noise the noise, not the labelled window, decides where it fires first.
Both biases are properties of the decision rule, and both are smaller than the
scatter — which is exactly why a bias-only report is not enough.

## 5. Three label conventions and the bias each one induces

| Training label | What it teaches | Induced bias |
|---|---|---|
| binary, window contains onset | where the arrival *is*, to one window | $-\tfrac{1}{2}$ window, measured $-0.180\ \mathrm{s}$ here |
| binary, per sample (onset or not) | a probability trace with sample resolution | a latch on the leading edge, unless the label is a delta |
| continuous offset (regression) | the signed distance to the onset | unbiased if the target is the onset and the loss is symmetric |

The lesson is not that window labels are wrong — they are cheap and they detect
well (see [21-06-01](./01-seismic-ml.md), where the same feature family reaches
0.891 AUC for detection). It is that a detector's label and a picker's label are
different objects, and a report that gives a pick error without naming the label
convention has hidden half of its error budget.

## 6. What a pretrained-picker toolbox provides

Toolboxes such as **SeisBench** exist to make pretrained seismic phase pickers
and their benchmarks comparable: a common waveform format, a model zoo, and
published performance measured on public data sets. In practice this buys three
things and leaves three things undone.

Bought:

1. **A trained model** without a labelled local data set — the largest single
   barrier for a new network.
2. **A label convention fixed in the model**, so "the pick" means something
   specific and documented.
3. **A benchmark**, so a new model has a reference point other than the author's
   own test set.

Not bought:

1. **Transfer evidence for your network.** A picker trained on regional
   broadband data has no claim on a 4.5 Hz geophone in a noisy urban site.
2. **Your tolerance.** The published residual distribution is a statement about
   the benchmark's tolerance and its signal-to-noise distribution.
3. **Calibrated uncertainty.** A probability trace is not a posterior over the
   onset time; turning it into an error bar requires a calibration step on
   labelled local data.

Because these toolboxes are actively developed, treat any specific API or
benchmark number as version-dependent and check the current documentation;
this repository deliberately quotes no external performance figure.

## 7. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **One phase, one wavelet, known dominant frequency.** Real records contain
   P, S, coda, converted phases and instrument response.
2. **White, stationary noise.** Real noise is coloured and non-stationary, and
   the STA/LTA ratio's variance depends on both.
3. **A single, exactly known reference pick.** Reference labels in real data
   come from analysts or other algorithms and carry their own scatter; the
   measured bias of a model is then only a bias *relative to the reference*.
4. **Picks are independent across records.** In an array, picks share a common
   noise field and a common event, so their errors are correlated.
5. **The tolerance is fixed before the error is measured.** Choosing it after
   seeing the residuals is a multiple-comparison decision dressed as a metric.

## The honest summary

On synthetic records with white noise and one 8 Hz wavelet, a classical STA/LTA
crossing picker has a mean absolute error of $0.342\ \mathrm{s}$ at 10 dB and
$0.927\ \mathrm{s}$ at 4 dB, and a learned window classifier trained with
"window contains the onset" labels is worse at 10 dB ($0.568\ \mathrm{s}$) and
identical at 4 dB ($0.917\ \mathrm{s}$). The learned picker's
$-0.180\ \mathrm{s}$ bias at 10 dB is caused by the label convention and is
larger than any tolerance such a network would accept. The transferable
conclusions are (i) the label convention sets the achievable precision, (ii) a
method comparison at one signal-to-noise ratio decides nothing, and (iii) every
pick statistic is a statement about a tolerance. The numbers themselves belong
to this generator and to no real network.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Quoting a pick accuracy without a tolerance.** The same picks here score
   0.740 and 0.345 at two tolerances.
2. **Training a picker with detection labels.** The measured
   $-0.180\ \mathrm{s}$ bias is a label artefact.
3. **Comparing methods at one signal-to-noise ratio.** At 4 dB the two pickers
   are within $1\%$; at 10 dB they differ by $66\%$.
4. **Treating a probability trace as an error bar.** Calibration on labelled
   local data is a separate step.
5. **Using a pretrained model without a local test set.** Transfer is an
   empirical question about your network.
6. **Reporting a bias as if it were the error.** Here the bias is smaller than
   the scatter at both levels; the scatter dominates.

## Where this is used

- **Earthquake location.** Pick precision propagates into hypocentre uncertainty
  ([06-04-01](../../06-seismology/04-earthquake-location-and-magnitude/01-hypocentre-location.md))
  and, for relative methods, into the double-difference residual
  ([06-04-03](../../06-seismology/04-earthquake-location-and-magnitude/03-relative-location.md)).
- **Early warning.** A warning system consumes the *first* P pick, so a picker's
  early bias is a systematic error in the origin-time estimate, while its
  scatter is what sets the false-alarm rate.
- **Induced-seismicity monitoring.** Large pick volumes at low signal-to-noise
  make automatic picking unavoidable; the tolerance then determines the smallest
  event the catalogue can resolve.
- **Ambient-noise and receiver-function workflows**, where arrival-time offsets
  are used as differential measurements and a constant bias partly cancels —
  which is why the *scatter*, not the bias, is the quantity to minimise.
