# Spatial and Directional Statistics in Earth Science

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Explain** why the independence assumption fails for gridded and mapped data
   and **state** the consequences for classical standard errors.
2. **Compute** Moran's $I$ for a gridded field with a stated contiguity rule and
   **compare** it with its null expectation $-1/(N-1)$.
3. **Compute** the experimental semivariance and **relate** it to the
   autocovariance by $\gamma(h) = C(0) - C(h)$.
4. **Distinguish** second-order stationarity from the intrinsic hypothesis and
   **state** which one each estimator needs.
5. **Compute** the circular mean, the resultant length and the Rayleigh test for
   directional data, and **explain** what each constrains.
6. **Diagnose** the cases in which a spatial summary is a single number hiding a
   non-stationary or anisotropic map.

All fields and bearings in this lesson are **SYNTHETIC**: Gaussian random fields
generated by smoothing white noise, and bearings drawn from a von Mises law. No
measured value is used, and no number constrains a real map.

## 1. Intuition: neighbouring values are not independent

Every classical standard error in this curriculum is built on $n$ independent
observations. A map violates that by construction: values one kilometre apart
resemble each other more than values fifty kilometres apart, so the map carries
far less information than its pixel count suggests. The effective sample size of
a gridded field is not the number of cells but the number of times the field
could be shifted before it becomes unrecognisable, roughly the area divided by
the square of the correlation length.

Spatial statistics therefore asks two questions that ordinary statistics does
not: **how much** are neighbouring values alike, and **over what distance** does
that resemblance die? The first is answered by an index such as Moran's $I$, the
second by the semivariance as a function of lag. The pair is the entry point to
module [21-02 Geostatistics](../02-geostatistics/README.md), where the second
question becomes the variogram and the kriging system.

## 2. Spatial autocorrelation: Moran's I

Let $z_i = x_i - \bar x$ be the centred values on a lattice of $N$ sites, and let
$w_{ij}$ be a spatial-weights matrix with $w_{ii} = 0$ and total
$W = \sum_{i,j} w_{ij}$. Moran's $I$ is a spatial analogue of a correlation
coefficient:

$$I = \frac{N}{W}\cdot\frac{\sum_i \sum_j w_{ij}\,
z_i z_j}{\sum_i z_i^{2}} .$$

With rook contiguity and row-standardised weights the expression simplifies, and
under the null of no spatial autocorrelation

$$\mathbb E[I] = -\frac{1}{N-1} .$$

The expectation is *negative*, not zero, because the weights exclude the site
itself: a site's own value is perfectly correlated with itself, and removing that
contribution leaves the remaining pairs slightly anti-correlated on average. The
demonstration's $30\times30$ grid therefore has $\mathbb E[I] = -0.0011$, and
judging a measured $I$ against zero rather than against $-1/(N-1)$ is a common
error.

## 3. The semivariance

For a lag vector $h$, the experimental semivariance is

$$\hat\gamma(h) = \frac{1}{2N(h)}\sum_{i=1}^{N(h)}
\big(z(x_i) - z(x_i + h)\big)^{2},$$

averaged over the $N(h)$ pairs separated by $h$. It is the natural estimator
under the **intrinsic hypothesis**, which requires only that the increments
$Z(x+h) - Z(x)$ be second-order stationary — a weaker condition than
second-order stationarity of $Z$ itself. When the covariance exists,

$$\gamma(h) = C(0) - C(h), \qquad C(h) = \operatorname{Cov}\big(Z(x), Z(x+h)\big),$$

so the semivariance rises from $0$ at $h = 0$ to the **sill** $C(0)$ at the
**range**, the lag beyond which values are effectively uncorrelated. A non-zero
intercept at $h \to 0$ is the **nugget**, which combines measurement error and
variability at scales shorter than the sampling interval.

## 4. Directional data

Directions do not live on a line, so the arithmetic mean of angles is wrong: the
mean of $350^{\circ}$ and $10^{\circ}$ is $0^{\circ}$, not $180^{\circ}$. Treat
each bearing $\theta_i$ as a unit vector, sum the components, and measure the
**resultant length**

$$R = \Big\|\frac1n\sum_i (\cos\theta_i,\ \sin\theta_i)\Big\|,
\qquad \bar\theta = \operatorname{atan2}\!\Big(\tfrac1n\sum \sin\theta_i,\
\tfrac1n\sum\cos\theta_i\Big).$$

$R$ is $0$ for a uniform direction distribution and approaches $1$ for a tight
cluster. The **von Mises** distribution plays the role of the Gaussian on the
circle, with concentration $\kappa$;
$R \approx I_1(\kappa)/I_0(\kappa)$, the ratio of modified Bessel functions. The
**Rayleigh test** of uniformity uses

$$Z = n R^{2}, \qquad p \approx e^{-Z},$$

and rejects the uniform null when $R$ is large. A significant Rayleigh test says
that a preferred direction exists; it does not say the distribution is von Mises,
nor does it estimate the mean direction's uncertainty, for which the
bootstrap is required.

## 5. Worked example

The demonstration builds $30\times30$ fields, $N = 900$ cells.

**Moran's I.** White noise gives $I = -0.0346$ against $\mathbb E[I] = -0.0011$;
a Gaussian-smoothed field with correlation length $0.5$ cells gives $+0.2877$, one
with $2.0$ cells gives $+0.9348$, and one with $5.0$ cells gives $+0.9471$. Two
things are worth reading from this. First, even mild smoothing moves $I$ far from
its null; a "significant" $I$ is not by itself evidence of structure worth
modelling. Second, $I$ **saturates**: going from $2.0$ to $5.0$ cells barely
changes it, because the index has no lag scale. It answers "is there spatial
structure?" and not "at what distance?".

**Semivariance.** On the length-$2.0$ field with sample variance $0.019$:

| lag (cells) | $\hat\gamma$ | fraction of sill |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.0015 | 0.077 |
| 2 | 0.0054 | 0.281 |
| 3 | 0.0103 | 0.536 |
| 5 | 0.0167 | 0.869 |
| 8 | 0.0177 | 0.921 |
| 12 | 0.0188 | 0.978 |
| 15 | 0.0196 | 1.020 |

The rise from $0.077$ to $1.020$ of the sill over fifteen cells is the range
signal. The last value slightly exceeding the sill is the noise of a single
realisation — the sill is estimated from the same finite field — and it is the
reason a variogram is fitted with a model rather than read off point by point.

**Directions.** Sixty bearings drawn uniformly give a circular mean of
$70.85^{\circ}$ with $R = 0.1037$ and Rayleigh $Z = 0.645$, $p = 0.525$: no
evidence of a preferred direction. Sixty bearings drawn from a von Mises law
concentrated at $45^{\circ}$ with $\kappa = 3$ give a mean of $43.30^{\circ}$,
$R = 0.7682$ and $Z = 35.404$, $p = 4.2\times10^{-16}$. The mean direction is
recovered to within $1.7^{\circ}$, and $R$ is close to the von Mises prediction
$I_1(3)/I_0(3) = 0.810$ (the small-$n$ bias-corrected value is
$R(1+1/(2n)) = 0.775$); the sampling scatter of $R$ at $n = 60$ is of order
$0.05$.

## Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Stationarity of the weights' interpretation.** Moran's $I$ is a
   global average; on a map with a trend it measures the trend, not the local
   texture.
2. **A stated contiguity rule.** Rook, queen and distance-band weights give
   different $I$ on the same map; the rule belongs in the report.
3. **The intrinsic hypothesis for the semivariance.** Only *increments* need be
   stationary, which is why the semivariogram survives mild trends that break
   the covariance.
4. **Isotropy for a one-dimensional variogram.** If the range differs by
   direction, the lag must be a vector and the field is anisotropic.
5. **The Rayleigh test is omnibus.** Rejection can come from a bimodal or
   skewed direction distribution, not only from a single preferred direction.
6. **$R$ depends on $n$.** $R$ is a biased estimate of $I_1(\kappa)/I_0(\kappa)$
   at small $n$; the correction is $R\,(1 + 1/(2n))$ approximately.

## The honest summary

Spatial data break the independence that makes classical standard errors work,
and they break it in two independent ways: the amount of correlation and the
distance over which it extends. Moran's $I$ answers only the first and saturates
at $+0.95$ whether the correlation length is two cells or five; the semivariogram
answers the second and is the object that geostatistics fits. The demonstrations
show the gap plainly: an $I$ of $0.93$ and a range of about five cells are two
different facts about the same field. What none of these numbers establishes is
that the field is stationary or isotropic — those are claims about the geology
which the map alone can suggest but not prove.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Comparing $I$ with zero.** The null expectation is $-1/(N-1)$, not $0$.
2. **Ignoring the weights definition.** Different contiguity rules give different
   $I$ values on the same data.
3. **Reading a range off a raw semivariogram.** A single realisation's
   semivariogram is noisy; it is fitted with a model.
4. **Treating the nugget as measurement error only.** It also absorbs
   variability below the sampling interval, which no instrument error explains.
5. **Averaging directions arithmetically.** It gives the answer diametrically
   opposite the truth for tightly clustered data.
6. **Assuming isotropy.** A range that depends on azimuth means the one-lag
   variogram is averaging two different fields.

## Where this is used

- **Geochemical and soil surveys**: spatial autocorrelation of element
  concentrations before interpolation.
- **Potential-field and seismic attribute mapping**: effective sample size for
  any test run on a map.
- **Structural geology**: directional statistics of fault strikes and fracture
  orientations.
- **Paleomagnetism and rock magnetism**: circular means and concentration of
  directions, where the Rayleigh test is the routine first question.
- **Remote sensing**: spatial autocorrelation as a measure of texture and as the
  leakage structure that
  [21-04-03 model assessment](../04-machine-learning-fundamentals/03-model-assessment.md)
  must respect.
