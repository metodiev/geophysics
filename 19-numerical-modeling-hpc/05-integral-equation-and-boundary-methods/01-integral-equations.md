# Volume and Surface Integral Equations

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Derive** the volume-integral representation of a potential field from the
   Poisson equation and a Green's function, and state the sign convention it
   carries.
2. **Reduce** an area integral over a source body to a line integral over its
   boundary with the divergence theorem, and explain what that buys and what it
   costs.
3. **Distinguish** a Fredholm equation of the first kind from one of the second
   kind, and predict which one is ill-posed before you discretise it.
4. **Measure** the order of a Nyström discretisation and the order of an area
   quadrature from a refinement table.
5. **Quantify** the growth of the condition number of a first-kind system with
   the number of unknowns, and explain semi-convergence of the naive solution.
6. **State** the assumptions under which a potential-field integral equation is
   equivalent to the differential equation it replaces.

## 1. Why integral equations appear in geophysics

A differential formulation asks for the field **everywhere**: discretise the
volume, impose the equation at every node, and solve. An integral formulation
asks for the **sources** on a surface — an equivalent-source layer, a boundary
density, a scattering strength — and then obtains the field anywhere by
integration. The two are equivalent mathematics; they differ in what has to be
discretised.

That difference matters most where a differential method is awkward:

- **Unbounded domains.** The Laplace equation outside a body needs no artificial
  truncation boundary if the representation itself has the right behaviour at
  infinity. Section 6 of the demonstration measures what a truncated grid costs.
- **Thin or localised targets.** A sheet-like body, or a body embedded in a
  layered medium, is represented by sources on a surface, and the field in a
  layered medium is built from a layered Green's function rather than a large
  grid.
- **One-dimensional reduction.** In 2-D a body's field can be computed from an
  integral over its boundary, so a 2-D quadrature becomes a 1-D quadrature.

The price is that the resulting linear system is usually **dense** and often
**ill-conditioned**, and the conditioning is not an implementation detail: it is
a property of the equation, not of the discretisation.

## 2. The volume integral representation, derived

Take the 2-D Poisson equation for a potential $\Phi$ generated by a density
$\rho(\mathbf{r})$:

$$\nabla^{2}\Phi(\mathbf{r}) = 4\pi G\rho(\mathbf{r}),$$

with $G = 6.67430\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m^{3}\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$ the standard
gravitational constant. Let the kernel be the 2-D logarithmic Green's function

$$G_{2D}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}') = \frac{1}{2\pi}\ln|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|,
\qquad \nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{r}}G_{2D} = \delta(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'),$$

where the Laplacian is with respect to the **field** point $\mathbf{r}$ and the
right-hand side is the 2-D Dirac delta. Because $\nabla^{2}$ is symmetric under
the exchange $\mathbf{r}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{r}'$, the delta picks out the field
point, and the represented solution is

$$
\boxed{\;\Phi(\mathbf{r}) = 2G\rho\int_{A}\ln|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|\,\mathrm{d}A'\;}
$$

for a uniform density. The factor $2$ comes from the $1/2\pi$ in the chosen
kernel and the $4\pi$ in the equation: it is a **convention** that must be
carried consistently, and the numerical check below is what makes the convention
visible rather than invisible.

### 2.1 The Poisson equation as a check, not a decoration

A representation is only useful if it satisfies the equation that motivated it.
Applying the Laplacian to the representation gives $\nabla^{2}\Phi = 4\pi G\rho$
inside the body and $0$ outside — and the demonstration measures exactly that,
by taking a numerical divergence of the field it computed:

| point | central-difference step $h$ | measured divergence ($\mathrm{s^{-2}}$) | ratio to $4\pi G\rho$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| inside the body | 400 m | $+2.181981\times10^{-6}$ | $+1.00060$ |
| inside the body | 100 m | $+2.180747\times10^{-6}$ | $+1.00004$ |
| outside the body | 400 m | $-3.660640\times10^{-10}$ | $-0.00017$ |
| outside the body | 100 m | $-2.287897\times10^{-11}$ | $-0.00001$ |

with $4\pi G\rho = 2.180665\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$. Two things are worth
noticing. First, the inside ratio approaches **one** and the outside ratio
approaches **zero**, which is the Poisson equation. Second, the residual falls
by $4$ for each halving of $h$ (measured order $2.00$ in both cases), which
identifies it as the truncation error of the **central difference**, not of the
integral. A check whose error does not behave like the error of the thing you
measured is not a check.

## 3. From an area integral to a boundary integral

The field is $\nabla\Phi$, and the divergence theorem converts each component.
For the $x$ component, note that $\partial\ln|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|/\partial x'
= (x'-x)/|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|^{2}$, so

$$\int_{A}\frac{x'-x}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}A'
= \oint_{\partial A}\ln|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|\,n_{x}\,\mathrm{d}l',$$

because $\int_{A}\partial f/\partial x'\,\mathrm{d}A' = \oint f n_{x}\,\mathrm{d}l'$
for any smooth $f$. Hence

$$
\boxed{\;\nabla\Phi(\mathbf{r})
= -2G\rho\oint_{\partial A}\ln|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|\,\mathbf{n}'\,\mathrm{d}l',}
$$

with $\mathbf{n}'$ the **outward** normal of the body. The minus sign is the one
to check: for an observation point far to the $+x$ side of a body whose normal
points away, the integral is positive and the field must point away from the
body in this sign convention.

### 3.1 What the reduction buys, measured

For a rectangle the boundary is four straight edges. The demonstration uses $48$
Gauss-Legendre nodes per edge — $192$ one-dimensional nodes in total — and
compares the resulting field with an area quadrature using $1200\times1200 =
1\,440\,000$ cells:

| observation $x$ | area route ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$) | boundary route ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$) | relative difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 km | $0$ | $+3.469\times10^{-18}$ | not defined (exact zero by symmetry) |
| 3 km | $+1.04562042\times10^{-3}$ | $+1.04562238\times10^{-3}$ | $1.875\times10^{-6}$ |
| 6 km | $+1.78038925\times10^{-3}$ | $+1.78039460\times10^{-3}$ | $3.005\times10^{-6}$ |
| 10 km | $+1.82530142\times10^{-3}$ | $+1.82530236\times10^{-3}$ | $5.147\times10^{-7}$ |

The two agree to about $10^{-6}$, and the agreement is limited by the **area**
route: a second-order rule with a finite-difference derivative. The comparison is
not "the boundary method is exact"; it is "the boundary method reaches the
accuracy of a million-cell area quadrature with $192$ nodes, and the residual is
the reference's error".

That is the recurring trade of integral methods: **accuracy per unknown is high,
and the operator is dense.** Section 4 of [02 Boundary Element Methods](02-boundary-element.md)
measures the same trade against a volume grid and reports the case where the
volume grid wins.

### 3.2 Quadrature order, and what Richardson adds

The area rule is a midpoint rule on a rectangle, so its error should fall as
$h^{2}$. Measured error at the profile point $x = 6$ km against a
Richardson-extrapolated reference:

| $n$ per side | error ($\mathrm{m^{2}\,s^{-2}}$) | observed order |
|---|---|---|
| 12 | $4.321914\times10^{-3}$ | — |
| 24 | $1.080545\times10^{-3}$ | 2.00 |
| 96 | $6.753538\times10^{-5}$ | 2.00 |
| 384 | $4.220966\times10^{-6}$ | 2.00 |

Two Richardson extrapolations, from $n = 96,192$ and from $n = 192,384$, agree
with the reference value $226.133735211\ \mathrm{m^{2}\,s^{-2}}$ to
$2.16\times10^{-11}$ and $1.25\times10^{-12}$ respectively, and with each other
to $2.04\times10^{-11}$. An extrapolation that is checked **against a second,
independent extrapolation** is the cheapest available evidence that a
single-power error law is being respected.

## 4. Fredholm equations: first kind and second kind

Both classes appear in potential-field work, and they behave differently.

**Second kind.** With a smooth kernel $K$ and a parameter $\lambda$,

$$\psi(s) = f(s) + \lambda\int_{0}^{1}K(s,s')\psi(s')\,\mathrm{d}s'
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (I - \lambda\mathcal{K})\psi = f .$$

If $\|\lambda\mathcal{K}\| < 1$ the operator is a contraction, the Neumann series
$\psi = \sum_{m}(\lambda\mathcal{K})^{m}f$ converges, and the discretised system
stays well conditioned at every resolution. This is the form a boundary integral
equation takes.

**First kind.** With the same kernel,

$$g(s) = \int_{0}^{1}K(s,s')\rho(s')\,\mathrm{d}s' .$$

The operator smooths: a smooth kernel has singular values that decay
geometrically, so inverting it amplifies noise without bound. This is the form
the equivalent-source method takes, and its difficulty is the same difficulty as
any potential-field inverse problem.

## 5. Discretisation, measured behaviour, and ill-posedness

The Nyström method represents $\psi$ by its values at midpoint nodes $s_j =
(j+\tfrac12)h$ and the integral by the midpoint rule. For the demonstration's
Gaussian kernel ($\sigma = 0.10$, $\lambda = 0.80$) the measured error against a
$4096$-panel reference is:

| $n$ | $\max|\psi-\psi_{\text{ref}}|$ | observed order | $\mathrm{cond}(I-\lambda K)$ | contraction $\|\lambda hK\|_{\infty}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | $1.335913\times10^{-1}$ | — | 3.9950 | 0.8000 |
| 32 | $7.467216\times10^{-3}$ | 2.04 | 4.3300 | 0.8000 |
| 128 | $4.683841\times10^{-4}$ | 2.00 | 4.3284 | 0.8000 |
| 256 | $1.172648\times10^{-4}$ | 2.00 | 4.3283 | 0.8000 |

Three measured facts, all of which the lesson will use again:

- the error is **second order**, as a midpoint rule requires;
- the condition number is bounded at every $n$ and close to the predicted
  $1/(1-\lambda) = 5$;
- the contraction factor is $0.8000$ at every resolution, and the measured
  number of Neumann iterations to drive the residual below $10^{-12}$ is
  **106**, against $\ln(10^{-12})/\ln(0.8) = 124$ predicted from the contraction
  alone. The gap is the price of a pointwise rather than a normwise criterion.

The same kernel in a **first-kind** equation behaves very differently. With
right-hand side from a smooth model $\rho(s) = 1 + 0.5\cos(2\pi s)$ and additive
noise of $10^{-6}$:

| $n$ | $\mathrm{cond}(A)$ | $\log_{10}$ | relative solution error |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | $9.287\times10^{0}$ | 0.97 | $8.229\times10^{-6}$ |
| 16 | $4.045\times10^{4}$ | 4.61 | $4.594\times10^{-3}$ |
| 32 | $6.542\times10^{16}$ | 16.82 | $2.117\times10^{10}$ |
| 64 | $2.510\times10^{19}$ | 19.40 | $5.042\times10^{12}$ |
| 128 | $2.545\times10^{19}$ | 19.41 | $2.615\times10^{12}$ |

The condition number **grows with resolution** until it hits the floor set by
double precision ($\epsilon \approx 2.2\times10^{-16}$, so
$\mathrm{cond} \approx 1/\epsilon$ is the ceiling). The solution error grows with
it, spectacularly. This is **semi-convergence**: refining the discretisation of a
first-kind equation makes the answer worse, not better, once the noise floor is
reached. The conditioning is a property of the *continuous* equation; a finer
grid merely lets you see it.

## 6. Assumptions and regime of validity

Every result above holds under conditions that must be stated with it.

1. **Constant density within each body.** A variable density turns the volume
   integral into a weighted one but leaves the structure intact; a discontinuous
   density across a boundary requires the boundary term to be handled explicitly.
2. **Free space, no other bodies.** The representation uses the free-space
   Green's function. Topography, a water layer or a layered subsurface require a
   different kernel (Section 3 of [03 Numerical Green's Functions](03-greens-function-numerics.md)).
3. **Observation points off the source.** The integrals are finite for
   $\mathbf{r}\notin\partial A$; on the boundary the kernel is logarithmically
   singular and integrable, and the jump (free) term of
   [02 Boundary Element Methods](02-boundary-element.md) appears.
4. **Two dimensions.** The log kernel, its $2\pi$ and the factor $2$ in
   $\Phi = 2G\rho\int\ln\cdot\,\mathrm{d}A$ are all 2-D statements. In 3-D the
   kernel is $1/(4\pi|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|)$ and the reduction to the boundary
   is a surface integral. The demonstration's arithmetic does not transfer.
5. **Smoothness for the order statements.** The measured order $2.00$ assumes a
   smooth integrand. A corner in the boundary, or an observation point near the
   boundary, reduces the order and must be measured again.
6. **Quadrature accuracy, not modelling accuracy.** Everything measured here
   concerns the discretisation of a chosen model. It says nothing about whether
   the model describes the Earth; that question belongs to
   [19-07 Verification, Validation and Numerical Uncertainty](../07-verification-validation-uncertainty/README.md).

## 7. A fully worked example

Take the demonstration's body: a rectangle $x\in[-6000, 6000]$ m,
$z\in[3000, 9000]$ m, uniform $\rho = 2600\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. Its area is
$A = 1.2\times10^{4} \times 6.0\times10^{3} = 7.2\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m^{2}}$
($72.0\ \mathrm{km^{2}}$), its centroid is at $z = 6000$ m, and

$$2G\rho A = 2(6.67430\times10^{-11})(2600)(7.2\times10^{7})
= 2.498858\times10^{1}\ \mathrm{m^{2}\,s^{-2}},$$

which the demonstration prints as the monopole strength. Far away, the field
should be that of a line mass concentrated at the centroid,
$g_{R} \approx 2G\rho A/R$. At $R = 30$ km the boundary quadrature gives
$g_{x} = 8.413406\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ against
$2G\rho A/R = 8.329526\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, a ratio of $1.010070$.

Two conclusions follow, and both are used later in the module.

- The leading term is right to $1\%$ at $30$ km, so a point-mass approximation is
  a controlled first approximation, not a guess.
- The remaining $1\%$ is **structure**, and its size is computable. Measuring the
  residual $|R g_{R} - 2G\rho A|$ at $R = 30, 60, 120, 240$ km gives
  $2.516389\times10^{-1}$, $6.258479\times10^{-2}$, $1.562500\times10^{-2}$,
  $3.904913\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^{2}\,s^{-2}}$, a fitted slope of
  $\log(\text{residual})$ against $\log R$ of $-2.003$. The first correction to a
  monopole is a quadrupole decaying as $R^{-2}$ in two dimensions — exactly the
  hierarchy that [04 Fast Multipole and Hierarchical Acceleration](04-fast-multipole.md)
  exploits.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Fixing a convention twice.** The $2\pi$ in the kernel, the $4\pi$ in the
   Poisson equation and the factor $2$ in $\Phi$ form one consistent set. Change
   one and the field is wrong by a constant factor that no convergence study
   will reveal.
2. **Reporting an agreement without saying what limited it.** "The two methods
   agree to $3\times10^{-6}$" is only meaningful with "and the reference's own
   error is of the same order".
3. **Judging a first-kind discretisation by its data residual.** The residual can
   be driven to zero while the solution error grows; only the solution error
   against a known model, or the singular-value spectrum, shows the problem.
4. **Assuming a finer grid improves an ill-posed inverse problem.** It makes the
   conditioning worse, because a better-resolved operator has smaller singular
   values still to be inverted.
5. **Treating a boundary reduction as free.** It converts a dense area sum into a
   dense boundary sum; the operator is still dense and still needs a solver or an
   acceleration hierarchy.
6. **Forgetting that the boundary route needs the observation point off the
   boundary.** On the boundary the free term appears and, if it is left out, the
   answer is off by a factor of order two.
