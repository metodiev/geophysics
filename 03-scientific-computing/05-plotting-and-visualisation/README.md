# Module 03-05 — Plotting, Visualisation and Scientific Figures

**Stages:** beginner → advanced · **Prerequisites:**
[03-01-04 Functions, Scope, Modules and Packages](../01-python-fundamentals/04-functions-modules.md)
for building a figure inside a function,
[03-02-01 ndarray Creation, dtypes, Shapes and Strides](../02-numpy/01-ndarray-basics.md)
and [03-02-03 Broadcasting Rules and Vectorised Thinking](../02-numpy/03-broadcasting.md)
for the arrays every plot consumes, and
[02-01-02 Measurement Uncertainty, Significant Figures and Error Bars](../../02-physics/01-measurement-and-physical-reasoning/02-measurement-uncertainty.md)
for what an error bar is allowed to mean.

A figure is a scientific argument, not decoration. That single principle organises
this module. Every display decision — the axes limits, the mark, the clip level, the
palette, the physical size of the page — is either part of the argument or an
unstated claim that the reader will supply for you. The module's eight lessons take
each decision in turn and, wherever possible, **replace the folk rule with a
measurement**: how many pixels a million points can occupy, how many times a
rainbow palette reverses its lightness, how much a degree of longitude is worth at
60° north, how many samples a clip level saturates.

Three features distinguish this module from a general plotting course:

- **Every demonstration measures.** The scripts compute the quantities that decide
  each display choice and print them. Where a claim is made in a lesson — that
  `plot_surface` silently discards three quarters of a fine grid, that a percentile
  clip is asymmetric by 17.65 %, that a rainbow palette maps two very different
  values to the same grayscale — the number comes from a script in
  [`code/`](./code) and is quoted as it was printed.
- **Every figure is reproducible.** The demonstrations write images to a temporary
  directory and print its path; nothing is committed. The point is that the figure
  is generated by code that is committed, so the figure can be regenerated,
  reviewed and corrected.
- **Synthetic data are labelled.** Wherever a demonstration generates data, the
  lesson says SYNTHETIC and describes the generating model, so the effect of each
  display choice can be checked against ground truth rather than against folklore.

The stack used is Matplotlib for the static figures, Cartopy for maps, `pyproj` for
geodesy, and NumPy for the arithmetic. Every demonstration runs headless
(`matplotlib.use("Agg")`), saves with `savefig`, and exits without opening a window.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

1. Work in the Matplotlib **Figure/Axes object model**, label every part of a plot
   with a quantity and a unit, and compute the pixel dimensions a given physical
   size and dpi produce.
2. Plot data **with uncertainty**, state the coverage of every error bar, fit a line
   by weighted least squares, report the parameter errors, and test the fit with
   $\chi^2/\nu$.
3. **Quantify** the distortion of plotting longitude and latitude as if they were
   Cartesian, choose a projection from the property the reader must judge, and
   compute and either state or remove a cross-section's vertical exaggeration.
4. **Assess** what a 3-D surface or voxel rendering preserves and what it destroys,
   and demonstrate the occlusion ambiguity that no camera can resolve.
5. **Display** seismic data with a stated gain, a symmetric clip level and a
   declared polarity, and measure what each clip level throws away.
6. **Audit** a colormap in CIE $L^*$: count its lightness reversals, measure its
   steepest lightness step, and decide between a sequential, diverging and cyclic
   palette from the structure of the data.
7. **Prepare** a figure for publication at a stated physical width, with embedded
   fonts, a legibility-checked type size, and at least two encoding channels for
   every categorical distinction.
8. **Decide** when an interactive display is warranted and what state must be
   written down when its view is exported as a static figure.

## Suggested path

**If you are working through the scientific-computing folder in order:** all eight
lessons in numeric order. Lessons 01–03 are the spine: the object model, the
encoding of uncertainty, and the coordinate systems. Lesson 05 is the one that is
used most often in practice and must be worked, not read.

**If you are here to make figures for a paper:** read [01](./01-matplotlib-basics.md)
in full (the object model is not optional), [02](./02-line-scatter-plots.md) §1–§4,
[06](./06-colormaps-perception.md) in full, and
[07](./07-publication-figures.md) in full. Then measure your own deliverable against
the checks in lesson 07 §1–§5 before submitting.

**If you are here for seismic interpretation:** read
[05](./05-seismic-displays.md) in full, with [06](./06-colormaps-perception.md)
§2–§5 as its palette foundation, and [03](./03-maps-and-cross-sections.md) §4 for
the time-versus-depth axis. The exercise sets for 05 and 06 are the ones that
matter.

**If you are here for potential fields or geodesy:** read
[03](./03-maps-and-cross-sections.md) in full, then
[06](./06-colormaps-perception.md) §6 (centring a diverging palette on the physical
zero), then the gravity anomalies you intend to display can be compared across
figures.

**If you are here for 3-D models or tomography:** read
[04](./04-3d-and-surfaces.md) in full and
[06](./06-colormaps-perception.md) §2–§3. The claim in 04 §6 — that a section panel
wins whenever a number is the message — is the one to argue with.

**If you already have plotting experience:** attempt ★★★9 of
[01](./01-matplotlib-basics.md), ★★★9 of [04](./04-3d-and-surfaces.md), ★★★9 of
[06](./06-colormaps-perception.md) and ★★★9 of
[08](./08-interactive-viz.md) first. If those close cleanly, use lessons 02, 03, 05
and 07 as reference.

## Mastery checkpoint

Complete this without notes, with a calculator and no code.

> A single-column figure is to be placed in an 85 mm column at 300 dpi.
> **(a)** Compute the maximum pixel width of an acceptable raster file, and state
> why quoting "300 dpi" alone is not enough.
> **(b)** The figure was authored 174 mm wide with 8 pt axis labels. Compute the
> effective label size after placement and state whether it meets a 6 pt floor.
> **(c)** The figure contains a Bouguer anomaly panel, a residual anomaly panel and
> a wrapped-phase panel. Assign a palette class to each and state, for each, the
> value that must receive the neutral colour or state that there is none.
> **(d)** A cross-section panel is 85 mm wide and 55 mm tall and covers 400 km
> horizontally and 40 km in depth. Compute the vertical exaggeration and the
> apparent dip of a true 5° reflector.
> **(e)** State the one check you would run on the delivered PDF to confirm that its
> text will not be substituted by the publisher's system.

Parts (a)–(b) are lesson [07](./07-publication-figures.md); part (c) is lesson
[06](./06-colormaps-perception.md); part (d) is lesson
[03](./03-maps-and-cross-sections.md) §4; part (e) is lesson
[07](./07-publication-figures.md) §5.

**Worked answer for (b) and (d):**

Placement ratio $= 85/174 = 0.4885$, so an 8 pt label becomes
$8 \times 0.4885 = 3.91$ pt — below the floor. The fix is to author at 85 mm with
7 pt labels, not to enlarge the fonts and scale down.

For (d): horizontal scale $= 400/85 = 4.706$ km mm⁻¹; vertical scale
$= 40/55 = 0.7273$ km mm⁻¹; vertical exaggeration
$= 4.706/0.7273 = 6.47$. A true 5° dip then appears at
$\arctan(6.47 \times \tan 5^\circ) = \arctan(0.5661) = 29.5^\circ$ — nearly six
times the true dip, which is why the exaggeration must be printed on the figure.

## Common pitfalls across this module

1. **Drawing an error bar with no coverage.** "$\pm 0.3$" could mean one standard
   deviation, one standard error of the mean, or a 95 % interval.
2. **Unweighted least squares on heteroscedastic data.** The demonstration's
   weights span a factor of 33.5, so the noisiest points decide the slope.
3. **Plotting longitude against latitude as if it were a Cartesian grid.** The
   shear is a factor of $1/\cos\varphi$: a factor of 2.00 at 60° north.
4. **An unstated vertical exaggeration.** At VE = 6.47 a true 5° dip renders at
   29.5°, and the error is not a constant offset because the relation is
   $\tan\theta_{\text{app}} = \mathrm{VE}\tan\theta$.
5. **Trusting `plot_surface`.** The default cap reduces a 360 × 360 grid to the same
   2025 quads as a 90 × 90 grid, discarding 98.43 % of the data without a warning.
6. **Measuring angles or distances off a 3-D figure.** The page metric is the
   camera: the same data maximum moves tens of pixels between view angles.
7. **An unstated seismic clip level.** The maximum clip sits 6.90 dB above the 99th
   percentile, so an unquoted clip changes every apparent amplitude ratio.
8. **An asymmetric clip under a diverging palette.** The percentile clip on the
   demonstration's section is asymmetric by 17.65 %, which moves the neutral colour
   off the physical zero.
9. **A rainbow palette for continuous data.** `jet` reverses its lightness five
   times and maps data values 93.3 % of the range apart to the same grayscale.
10. **Authoring a figure at the wrong physical size.** An 8 pt label at 174 mm
    becomes 3.91 pt in an 85 mm column, below the legibility floor.
