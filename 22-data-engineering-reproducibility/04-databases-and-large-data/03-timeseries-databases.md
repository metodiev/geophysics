# Time-Series Databases for Sensor Networks

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Compute** the storage of a sensor archive, and **state** why the write pattern
   (append-only, time-ordered) drives the schema.
2. **Measure** the compression a delta or second-difference scheme achieves on your own
   series, and **state** the error it introduces.
3. **Estimate** the byte reduction of a downsampled query, and **explain** why
   aggregation belongs in the engine.
4. **Choose** a retention policy from the measured cost of raw and aggregate tiers.
5. **Design** a schema that handles gaps, late-arriving data, backfill, and clock
   problems without corrupting the record.
6. **Diagnose** the four operational failures of a sensor archive: a clock shift, a
   duplicate backfill, a silently truncated channel, and a retention policy that deleted
   what an analysis needed.

Numbers in this lesson are **SYNTHETIC**: the series is generated by the demonstration
and the compression figures come from a stated bit-packing model; no database was
queried and no published compression result is quoted.

## 1. Intuition: an append-only stream, queried by time

A sensor archive is different from an event catalogue in three ways that decide
everything about its design. Writes are **appends in time order**, at a rate set by the
instrument; the table grows without bound; and almost every query is a **time window
with an aggregation** ("the daily mean for station 12 in March"). The consequences:

- the primary key is `(channel, time)`, and the physical order should follow it, so an
  append is a sequential write and a window query is a range scan;
- **retention** is a first-class design decision rather than an afterthought, because the
  archive grows monotonically for as long as the instrument runs;
- **aggregates are the query interface**: a plot of five years of 1 Hz data is not a
  plot of five years of 1 Hz data, it is a decimated summary, and the engine should
  produce it.

## 2. Storage: what a sample costs

For $N$ samples at one value per timestamp, the naive cost is
$N \times (\text{value bytes} + \text{timestamp bytes})$. The demonstration's
**SYNTHETIC** series is $N = 10^{6}$ samples at 1 Hz — 11.6 days:

$$S_{\text{naive}} = 10^{6} \times 8\ \text{B} = 8.0\ \text{MB}, \qquad
S_{\text{with stamps}} = 10^{6} \times (8 + 4)\ \text{B} = 12.0\ \text{MB},$$

and one station channel at that rate needs about **32×** the 11.6-day volume per year.

Compression exploits the fact that neighbouring samples are similar. Taking the second
difference $d_2 = x_{i+1} - 2x_i + x_{i-1}$ removes a linear trend, and packing each
value in the bits its magnitude needs gives, for a quantisation step of
$10^{-3}$ data units:

| Series | s.d. of $d_2$ | Mean bits per value | Storage ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| noise-dominated ($\sigma = 1$) | $2.45 \times 10^{0}$ | 12.8 | 4.98× |
| smooth ($\sigma = 0.01$) | $2.45 \times 10^{-2}$ | 6.3 | 10.14× |

The quantisation error bound is $5.0 \times 10^{-4}$ data units — far below the
$\sigma = 1$ signal but **not** negligible against a $\sigma = 0.01$ one, which is the
honest caveat: a compression scheme is a representation choice with an error, and the
error must be compared with the measurement uncertainty, not with the signal's amplitude.
Two ratios from the same code on the same shape (4.98× and 10.14×) make the general
point that a compression figure is a property of the **data**.

## 3. Queries: aggregate in the engine

A plot of one day of 1 Hz data displays at most a few thousand pixels, so the client
needs a decimated series, not a million samples. Reading raw and decimating in the client
transfers the full volume:

| Query interval | Raw bytes | 1-minute means | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 h | 28.8 kB | 0.5 kB | 60× |
| 24 h | 691.2 kB | 11.5 kB | 60× |
| 168 h | 4 838.4 kB | 80.6 kB | 60× |

The 60× is the ratio of the sampling rate to the aggregate's rate, and it holds at every
interval. The engineering answer is a **precomputed aggregate tier** (minute means and
hourly means, refreshed as data arrive), so the common query reads the tier rather than
the raw table. The aggregate is derived data: it can be regenerated, so it is cheap to
keep and safe to rebuild, while the raw data cannot be regenerated and belong in the
retention plan
([22-02-05](../02-reproducible-workflows/05-data-versioning.md) §7).

## 4. Retention: the arithmetic of keeping raw

Per channel per year at 1 Hz with 8-byte values:

| Tier | Volume per channel-year |
|---|---|
| raw samples | 0.25 GB |
| 1-minute means | 0.004 GB |
| hourly means | 0.00007 GB |

A 100-channel network stores **25 GB/year** of raw data, and keeping both aggregate tiers
adds **0.7 GB/year**, i.e. **1.69 %** overhead. The decision is therefore not a storage
decision at all: aggregates are essentially free, and the question is whether the raw
data may ever be needed again. If it may — for a reprocessing, a calibration change, a
question about a moment tensor — then raw must be kept and backed up; if it certainly may
not, then the raw archive is the only thing that can prove the aggregate was computed
correctly.

The design that generalises: **keep raw, keep the aggregates, and make the aggregate
tier disposable**. Name the tiers by resolution, record which one a figure used, and let
the retention policy act on the aggregates first.

## 5. Schema and operations

1. **Key and order.** Primary key `(channel, time)`; physical order matching it, so
   writes append and window queries range-scan.
2. **Partition by time.** Monthly or annual partitions keep each index small enough to
   cache and make retention a partition drop rather than a mass delete — the difference
   between an instant and an hours-long vacuum.
3. **Store flags beside values.** The QC flags of
   [22-03-05](../03-data-formats-and-engineering/05-data-quality-control.md) are part of
   the record; a flagged sample is still a sample.
4. **Represent gaps explicitly.** Missing samples are absent rows (or a declared fill
   value), never zeros; the convention is metadata, and the estimators state how they
   treat it.
5. **Handle late data with an upsert.** A backfill re-delivers old timestamps: an
   upsert on `(channel, time)` makes the operation idempotent, which is the same
   write-then-verify discipline as in
   [22-03-03](../03-data-formats-and-engineering/03-data-pipelines.md).
6. **Watch the clock.** Instrument clocks drift and can step; record both the instrument
   time and the receipt time, apply documented corrections, and keep the raw time so a
   correction can be revised.
7. **Test the archive against the instrument.** A per-channel row count per day compared
   with the expected sample rate is the cheapest detector of a truncated channel.

## 6. Worked example: an archive that cannot be reprocessed

A network's five-year archive is 125 GB of hourly means. A calibration change means every
value must be recomputed, and the raw samples were deleted after a year to save space.

1. **Can the reprocessing be done?** No: the hourly means cannot be un-averaged, and the
   calibration change is not a linear scale factor that could be applied afterwards (the
   sensor's response change is nonlinear at low amplitude).
2. **What would the storage have been?** Keeping the raw archive: five years × 25 GB/year
   = 125 GB, against the aggregate tier which costs 3.5 GB for the same span — under 3 %
   extra. The saving was real but small, and it cost the ability to reprocess.
3. **What can be recovered?** Only the data that was kept raw; the rest must be
   re-processed at the source if the instruments still hold it, or accepted as
   uncalibrated for the affected span.
4. **The policy that prevents it.** Raw is retained for at least one full reprocessing
   cycle (specified in the data management plan), aggregates are rebuilt automatically
   when raw changes, and the calibration version is stored with each value.

## Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **A chosen bit-packing model.** The 12.8 and 6.3 mean-bit figures come from a
   quantisation step of $10^{-3}$ and a magnitude-based width; other schemes and settings
   differ, and the durable result is the contrast between the two series.
2. **A fixed sampling rate.** Real networks have multiple rates and irregular sampling;
   the storage arithmetic is then per channel per rate.
3. **A single value per sample.** Vector-valued channels (three-component sensors) scale
   the volume, and their components may compress differently.
4. **Chosen aggregate tiers.** The 1-minute and hourly tiers are conventional, not
   universal; the ratio (the sampling rate divided by the aggregate rate) is what
   transfers.
5. **No engine was benchmarked.** The lesson describes the design pattern; a specific
   engine's compression codecs, partitioning and continuous-aggregate machinery differ.

## The honest summary

A sensor archive is an append-only, time-ordered stream whose cost is dominated by two
decisions: how each sample is represented and how long the raw tier is kept. A
second-difference scheme with a quantisation step of $10^{-3}$ stores the same synthetic
series at 4.98× (noise-dominated) or 10.14× (smooth) the float64 size, with a
quantisation error of $5.0 \times 10^{-4}$ that must be compared with the measurement
uncertainty. Queries want aggregates — a 60× byte reduction at every interval — so the
aggregate tier is the query interface, and it costs 1.69 % of the raw tier. The raw data
are the only irreplaceable tier, and deleting them to save that percentage is the
decision that turns a calibration change into an unrecoverable loss.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Deleting raw data to save space.** Aggregates cost 1.69 % of raw; the space saving
   is small and the loss is permanent.
2. **Decimating in the client.** The 60× saving is lost, and every plot re-reads the raw
   tier.
3. **Treating a compression ratio as a property of the format.** The same scheme gives
   4.98× or 10.14× depending on the series.
4. **Ignoring the quantisation error.** $5.0 \times 10^{-4}$ against a $\sigma = 0.01$
   signal is a 5 % distortion of the noise, not a rounding detail.
5. **Mass-deleting old partitions.** Retention should drop partitions, not rows; the
   difference is measured in hours.
6. **Assuming the clock is right.** Instrument time and receipt time are different
   facts, and only one of them is under the network's control.

## Where this is used

- **Seismic and geodetic networks** — decades of continuous channels with per-channel
  rates and quality flags.
- **Hydrological and meteorological monitoring** — the same append-only pattern at
  lower rates, with the same retention question.
- **Laboratory and instrument arrays** — high-rate channels where the raw tier's volume
  is the whole problem.
- **Cloud-native sensor archives** — chunked, content-addressed layout with an
  aggregate tier: see
