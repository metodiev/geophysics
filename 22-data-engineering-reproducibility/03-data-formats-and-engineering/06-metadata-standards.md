# Metadata Standards and Controlled Vocabularies

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Explain** why metadata is the interface of a data set, and **state** what a
   reader can and cannot recover without it.
2. **Compute** record-level completeness from per-field omission rates, and **state**
   why per-field statistics understate the problem.
3. **Design** and use a controlled vocabulary, and **measure** the label reduction a
   mapping achieves and the records it fails to map.
4. **Convert** declared units to a canonical unit, and **state** the two checks that
   catch a wrong unit declaration.
5. **Parse** ISO 8601 durations and time stamps correctly, and **explain** the
   ambiguity that makes hand-written parsing unsafe.
6. **Specify** a validation gate at the point of entry, and **state** what it must
   reject, normalise and record.

The catalogue, vocabularies and unit tables in this lesson are **SYNTHETIC** teaching
models generated by the demonstration; no real catalogue was read, no standard's text
is reproduced, and the unit table is a small subset containing only exact conversions.

## 1. Intuition: metadata is the interface

Two files can contain the same numbers and describe different worlds. Whether a
station's coordinate is `52.1` in degrees north or in some national grid; whether an
acceleration is in $\mathrm{m/s^{2}}$ or in gal; whether a time stamp is UTC or local
standard time; whether `-9999` is a measurement or a sentinel — every one of these
decisions changes the meaning of the numbers and none of them is visible in the bytes.
Metadata is the part of the data set that carries those decisions, and a data set
without it is an interchange format with no semantics.

The practical framing used throughout this lesson: **metadata is an interface, and an
interface must be machine-checkable**. A unit written in a comment is a hope; a unit
in an attribute that a validator reads is an interface. Everything here is about moving
decisions from prose into structures that software can check.

## 2. Required fields and record-level completeness

The demonstration's **SYNTHETIC** catalogue has 200 station records and eight required
fields with chosen per-field omission rates:

| Field | Missing | Missing (%) |
|---|---|---|
| network | 3 | 1.5 |
| station | 0 | 0.0 |
| channel | 3 | 1.5 |
| latitude | 12 | 6.0 |
| longitude | 1 | 0.5 |
| elevation_m | 19 | 9.5 |
| unit | 13 | 6.5 |
| start_time | 3 | 1.5 |

No field is missing in more than a tenth of the records, yet only **152 of 200** records
(76.0 %) have every required field present: **48 records (24.0 %)** are unusable as
they stand. The arithmetic is the point — the probability that a record is complete is
the product of the per-field probabilities only when omissions are independent,

$$P(\text{complete}) = \prod_{f} (1 - p_f),$$

which here is $0.985 \times 1.0 \times 0.985 \times 0.94 \times 0.995 \times 0.905
\times 0.935 \times 0.985 = 0.717$, in the right range of the measured 0.76 (the fields
are deliberately generated with independent draws in the demonstration's model). The
lesson to carry: **report completeness per record**, because per-field rates of a few
per cent compound into a quarter of the catalogue, and the compound figure is what
decides how much work the analysis needs.

## 3. Controlled vocabularies

A **controlled vocabulary** is a closed list of permitted values, with a mapping from
the free text that people actually write. The demonstration models nine free-text
sensor descriptions that should map to three canonical classes:

| Quantity | Measured |
|---|---|
| distinct free-text terms | 9 |
| canonical classes | 3 |
| label reduction | 3.0× |
| records whose term does not map | 30 (15.0 %) |

Two consequences. First, the **reduction is what makes a query meaningful**: without
the mapping, "count records per sensor type" returns nine groups instead of three, and
the long tail of spelling variants is invisible in every summary. Second, the
**unmapped residue is the governance problem**: 15 % of records whose term is not in
the vocabulary must be either mapped by a person, rejected at entry, or carried
explicitly as `unknown` — and the choice must be recorded, because "unknown" inflates
and silently changes a distribution.

The practice that keeps a vocabulary useful:

1. **Version it.** Vocabularies gain terms; a mapping applied in 2024 and again in 2026
   must be reproducible, so the vocabulary and its mapping table are versioned
   artefacts, not a dictionary in someone's code.
2. **Validate at entry.** The validator rejects a term that is not in the vocabulary
   (or maps it and records the mapping), so the residue cannot grow.
3. **Publish the dictionary.** A reader needs the definitions, not only the labels —
   what exactly distinguishes `broadband` from a specific sensor model.
4. **Keep the original term.** Store both the canonical value and the raw string, so a
   later revision of the vocabulary can be re-applied without re-reading the sources.

## 4. Units: declared, canonical, and checked

Unit handling is where the silent errors live, because a wrong unit produces a
plausible number. The demonstration's catalogue declares five acceleration units and
maps them to a canonical $\mathrm{m/s^{2}}$ with exact factors:

| Declared unit | Records | Factor to $\mathrm{m/s^{2}}$ |
|---|---|---|
| m/s^2 | 48 | $1.00 \times 10^{0}$ |
| m/s2 | 31 | $1.00 \times 10^{0}$ |
| gal | 45 | $1.00 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| cm/s^2 | 37 | $1.00 \times 10^{-2}$ |
| nm/s^2 | 39 | $1.00 \times 10^{-9}$ |

Only **48 records (24.0 %)** declare the canonical spelling; the remaining 76 % need a
conversion, and the conversions differ by up to nine orders of magnitude:
$9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}} = 981\ \mathrm{gal} = 9.81 \times 10^{9}\ \mathrm{nm/s^{2}}$.
Two unit facts to keep straight: `m/s2` and `m/s^2` are the same unit written two ways
(a normalisation, not a conversion), while `gal` is the CGS unit of acceleration used
in gravity and geodesy, equal to $0.01\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}}$.

Two checks catch the failures that the conversion table itself cannot:

1. **Dimensional and magnitude plausibility.** Convert to canonical, then test the
   range: a strong-motion channel's samples are of order $10^{-6}$ to
   $10\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}}$, so a value of $981$ in canonical units is a factor-of-100
   error, not a large earthquake. The bound comes from the channel type and the
   instrument documentation, not from the data.
2. **Cross-source agreement.** If two data sets should agree at a station-coincident
   location, compare them: a unit error appears as a constant ratio, which is far
   easier to see across sources than within one.

## 5. Time: the other silent axis

Time stamps and durations are parsed, not read, and they carry three classes of trap:

- **Format.** ISO 8601 duration strings (`P1Y2M3DT4H5M6S`) are compact and precise, and
  they are ambiguous to a naive string split: `M` means **months** in the date part and
  **minutes** in the time part, so a hand-written parser silently returns the wrong
  value. The demonstration parses them with a validating parser:

| Value | Seconds |
|---|---|
| P1Y | 31 557 600.0 |
| P2M | 5 260 032.0 |
| P1Y2M3DT4H5M6S | 37 091 538.0 |
| PT30M | 1 800.0 |
| P1DT12H | 129 600.0 |
| P0.5D | 43 200.0 |
| `foo` | rejected |

The parser **rejects** malformed input rather than guessing, which is the property to
require: a validator that coerces `foo` to zero is worse than one that refuses it,
because the refusal is visible.

- **Zone and epoch.** A time stamp without a zone is ambiguous by up to a day, and a
  numeric time without a stated epoch is meaningless. Store UTC with an explicit
  designator, and record the epoch for numeric times.
- **Conventions.** Seconds in a minute, leap seconds and the treatment of a day's end
  are exactly the details that hand-written arithmetic gets wrong; use a library, and
  record which one ([22-07-03](../07-software-testing-quality/03-regression-testing-data.md)
  suggests the reference tests).

## 6. Practice: a validation gate that earns its place

1. **Schema first.** Every field has a type, a unit, a cardinality and a required
   flag; the schema is a versioned artefact.
2. **Validate at entry, not at analysis.** The gate rejects a record with a missing
   required field, an unknown vocabulary term, an unconvertible unit or an unparsable
   time — and records the rejection with a reason.
3. **Normalise and keep the original.** Convert to canonical units and vocabulary, and
   store the raw values alongside, so the transformation is reversible and auditable.
4. **Publish the dictionary.** Field definitions, units, vocabularies, ranges and the
   provenance fields of
   [22-02-05](../02-reproducible-workflows/05-data-versioning.md) §5.
5. **Check the physics, not only the schema.** Amplitude bounds, coordinate ranges and
   time-span plausibility catch what type checking cannot.
6. **Report completeness with every release.** Per record and per field, so a consumer
   can see what fraction of the archive is usable for their query.

## 7. Worked example: a station list that changes the result

A group's analysis uses 200 station records; when the catalogue is refreshed, the
result changes by 6 %.

1. **Check completeness first.** In the demonstration's model, 24 % of records are
   incomplete. If the refresh added records with missing elevation, the analysis's
   station-specific corrections change for reasons unrelated to the science.
2. **Check the vocabulary.** A new sensor term that does not map (15 % of records in
   the model) can silently reclassify instruments, changing any per-instrument
   grouping.
3. **Check the units.** A new provider declaring `gal` instead of `m/s^2` introduces a
   factor of 100; the magnitude check catches it, a schema check does not.
4. **Check the time axis.** A provider that supplies local time without a zone shifts
   every record, which changes travel-time residuals systematically.
5. **What to report.** The three checks that passed, the record counts per stage, and
   the two fields whose coverage changed between the catalogue versions — that is a
   result a reader can assess; "the catalogue was updated" is not.

## Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Independence of field omissions.** The product formula and the demonstration's
   76 % assume omissions are independent; real catalogues have correlated omissions
   (a provider that omits elevation also omits the datum), which makes completeness
   worse than the product suggests.
2. **A small, exact unit table.** Only exactly convertible units are included; units
   that depend on a datum (for example elevations in a national vertical datum), on
   gravity, or on a time scale need a transformation, not a factor.
3. **A teaching vocabulary.** The nine terms and three classes are a model of the
   governance problem, not a list from any standard; real vocabularies are orders of
   magnitude larger and are maintained by communities.
4. **Durations with nominal month and year lengths.** The parser uses nominal
   conversions (365.25 days per year, 30.44 days per month), so a duration in months is
   not a fixed number of seconds between two dates — a distinction that matters for
   window arithmetic.
5. **No standard is quoted.** The lesson describes the *shape* of metadata practice;
   it does not reproduce or interpret any standards body's specification, and where a
   standard exists it should be read directly.

## The honest summary

Metadata is the interface of a data set, and the measurements here show what its
absence costs: eight fields with per-field omission rates under 10 % leave 24 % of a
catalogue unusable as a whole; nine free-text sensor terms collapse to three classes
(3.0×), with 15 % of records unmappable without governance; and 76 % of declared
acceleration units need a conversion whose factors span nine orders of magnitude.
Time adds a third axis, where an ISO 8601 duration is ambiguous to hand-written parsing
(`PT30M` = 1800 s, and `M` means months in the date part) and a missing time zone is a
silent day-long shift. The response is a versioned schema, a validation gate at the
point of entry that rejects rather than coerces, normalisation that keeps the original
values, a published dictionary, and physics checks that catch the errors a type system
cannot see.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Reporting per-field completeness.** Per-field rates under 10 % compound to 24 % of
   records unusable.
2. **Free text where a vocabulary belongs.** Nine terms for three classes makes a
   group-by meaningless and hides the long tail.
3. **Assuming the declared unit.** 76 % of the modelled records need a conversion, and
   the factors span nine orders of magnitude.
4. **Hand-written duration arithmetic.** `M` is months in the date part and minutes in
   the time part; `PT30M` is 1800 seconds.
5. **A validator that coerces.** Turning `foo` into zero hides the error; rejection
   makes it visible.
6. **Discarding the raw term or value.** Without it, a later vocabulary revision cannot
   be re-applied.
