# Automated Quality Control and Flagging

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Define** a quality flag as a decision with two error rates, and **compute** the
   false-positive budget of a threshold on Gaussian noise.
2. **Evaluate** a flagging rule with a confusion matrix, and **report** precision and
   recall rather than a flag count.
3. **Measure** masking: how the contamination changes a naive threshold but leaves a
   robust one stable, and **state** the consequence for recall.
4. **Choose** a robust scale (median and MAD) or a naive one (mean and standard
   deviation) for a stated detection goal, with the measured trade-off.
5. **Handle** gaps explicitly, **state** why a zero fill is a false measurement, and
   **quantify** its effect on the mean and the standard deviation.
6. **Design** a two-pass QC procedure with documented rules, retained raw values and a
   record of which rule version flagged what.

The record, spikes and gaps in this lesson are **SYNTHETIC** and generated by the
demonstration, so the true labels are known exactly. Every confusion matrix is
measured against that truth; no real record is used and no published QC threshold is
quoted as a standard.

## 1. Intuition: a flag is a decision, and decisions have two error rates

Automated quality control assigns a **flag** — "suspect", "out of range", "spike" — to
each sample or record. A flag is not a fact about the data; it is the output of a rule,
and every rule has two ways to be wrong:

- a **false positive**: a good sample flagged, which costs data, sometimes expensive
  data;
- a **false negative**: a bad sample passed, which costs correctness, and is the error
  nobody notices.

The practical consequence is that a QC design is an error-cost decision, and the
useful questions are: what does the rule detect, what does it miss, and what fraction
of the record does it consume? Those are measurable, and this lesson measures them.

## 2. The false-positive budget of a threshold

A rule of the form "flag anything beyond $k$ standard deviations" has a known
false-positive rate on Gaussian noise. For $k = 3$, two-sided:

$$P(|z| > 3) = 2\,\Phi(-3) = 0.0027 = 0.27\ \% .$$

On the demonstration's **SYNTHETIC** record of $N = 2000$ samples that budget is
**5.4 samples** — the rule *must* flag about five good samples even when the data are
perfectly clean. Tighter thresholds trade data for sensitivity: $2\sigma$ flags 4.6 %
(92 samples), $4\sigma$ flags 0.006 % (0.1 samples). Any rule quoted without its
false-positive budget is incomplete, because the budget is what the analyst is
spending in exchange for the detection.

## 3. Two contamination scenarios, three rules

The demonstration generates two records and applies three rules:

- **naive**: $|\hat{x} - \text{mean}| > 3\,\text{sd}$;
- **robust**: $|\hat{x} - \text{median}| > 3 \times 1.4826\,\text{MAD}$ (the $1.4826$
  factor makes the MAD-based scale comparable to a standard deviation for Gaussian
  data);
- **rate of change**: $|\Delta x| > 8\sigma$.

| Scenario | Rule | TP | FP | FN | Precision | Recall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 spikes at $12\sigma$ | naive | 12 | 0 | 0 | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| | robust | 12 | 3 | 0 | 0.800 | 1.000 |
| | rate of change | 12 | 12 | 0 | 0.500 | 1.000 |
| 100 spikes at $4\sigma$ | naive | 47 | 0 | 53 | 1.000 | **0.470** |
| | robust | 74 | 3 | 26 | 0.961 | **0.740** |
| | rate of change | 0 | 0 | 100 | 0.000 | **0.000** |

Three readings matter:

1. **With obvious outliers the naive rule looks excellent** — and that is an artefact
   of the outliers themselves inflating its scale, discussed in §4.
2. **With moderate outliers the naive rule misses more than half of them** (recall
   0.470) while the robust rule catches three quarters (0.740). The naive rule's
   precision of 1.000 is not a virtue: it flags nothing it is not sure about, and the
   price is paid in missed detections.
3. **The rate-of-change rule fails entirely on small spikes**: a $4\sigma$ excursion
   does not exceed a jump threshold of $8\sigma$, so recall is zero. A rule calibrated
   for large transients is not a rule for small ones, and the confusion matrix says so
   in one number.

## 4. Threshold stability: masking

The reason the naive rule behaves so differently between the two scenarios is that its
scale is estimated from the contaminated data:

| Contamination | Naive $3\sigma$ threshold | Robust $3 \times 1.4826$ MAD | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| clean | 2.933 | 2.924 | 1.00 |
| 12 spikes at $12\sigma$ | 4.094 | 2.927 | 1.40 |
| 100 spikes at $4\sigma$ | 4.041 | 3.222 | 1.25 |

The naive threshold rises by **40 %** when a handful of extreme values are present,
because the standard deviation is quadratic in the deviations: one $12\sigma$ value in
$2000$ samples adds $144/2000 = 0.072$ to the variance. The robust threshold moves by
at most 10 %, because the median and the MAD are order statistics determined by the
bulk of the distribution.

This is **masking**, and it has a precise consequence: a rule whose threshold depends
on the contamination sees a *wider* threshold exactly when the data are dirtier, so its
recall falls precisely where detection matters most (measured: 0.470 against 0.740 in
§3). It also means the threshold cannot be quoted as a fixed property of the rule:
"we flag at $3\sigma$" describes a number that is 2.93 or 4.09 depending on what is
in the window.

## 5. Choosing between naive and robust scales, honestly

Neither is universally better, and the measurements show why:

- **Robust scales are the right default** for flagging, because the threshold must not
  depend on the very outliers being detected (§4).
- **Naive scales have their place** where the contamination is known to be bounded: if
  a stage upstream has already removed gross outliers, the standard deviation is a
  reasonable estimator and, being computed from all the data, has lower variance than
  the MAD at small $N$.
- **Both are one-dimensional.** A spike in a quiet record and a genuine large
  transient are indistinguishable to a single-amplitude rule; distinguishing them
  needs context (neighbouring channels, coherence, physical bounds), which is why
  real QC is a *sequence* of rules with different evidence.

The design rule: state the detection goal, choose the scale that meets it, and report
the confusion matrix measured on data with known labels — which for a real record
means you must create labels (inject known spikes into a copy of the data, as the
demonstration does) rather than assume a rule works.

## 6. Gaps: missing is not zero

Missing samples are the second half of QC, and the failure is representational rather
than statistical. The demonstration measures the effect of two fills on a clean record:

| Missing | Fill | Mean | Standard deviation | Fake zeros presented as data |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 % | NaN (skipped) | $-0.00006$ | 0.9935 | 0 |
| 5 % | zero | $-0.00006$ | 0.9683 | 100 |
| 30 % | NaN (skipped) | $+0.02781$ | 1.0069 | 0 |
| 30 % | zero | $+0.02065$ | 0.8677 | 515 |

The zero fill **shrinks the standard deviation** — 1.0069 to 0.8677 at 30 % missing —
because zeros sit closer to the mean than the data they replaced, and it does so
silently: each fake zero carries no flag, no NaN and no marker, so every later
statistic treats it as a measurement. The mean moves too, in the direction of zero
($+0.02781 \to +0.02065$), which is a systematic bias proportional to the missing
fraction.

The practice that follows: **NaN (or a declared fill value that is never a valid
measurement) is the only honest placeholder**; the missing-value convention belongs in
the file's metadata; and every estimator that touches the series must state how it
handles missing values — dropping them, interpolating them, or treating the gap
explicitly in the time axis. A gap is information (the instrument was down), and a zero
destroys that information while inventing a measurement.

## 7. Practice: QC that can be audited

1. **Flag, do not delete.** Keep the raw values with their flags, so a later analyst can
   revisit the decision; deletion is irreversible and hides the evidence.
2. **Two passes.** A conservative first pass for gross errors (range, physical bounds,
   flat-line detection), then a statistical pass on the cleaned data for subtler
   features.
3. **Version the rules.** A QC flag is an output of a rule; record which rule version
   produced which flag, or the archive becomes unexplainable after the rule changes.
4. **Report both error rates.** Precision and recall on labelled data, plus the
   false-positive budget of each threshold.
5. **Make gaps explicit.** A declared fill value, a documented convention, and a
   per-estimator statement about missing values.
6. **Check the physics.** Amplitude bounds, cross-channel consistency and spectral
   shape catch what no single-channel threshold can.
7. **Re-calibrate on the data.** Thresholds derived on one station's noise do not
   transfer to another's; estimate the scale per channel and record it.

## 8. Worked example: a QC rule that was quietly destroying data

A station's processed series has 12 % fewer samples than the raw archive. The QC rule
is "flag beyond $2\sigma$ of the running mean".

1. **Compute the budget.** A $2\sigma$ two-sided rule flags 4.6 % of clean Gaussian
   data. Over a multi-year series that is $4.6\ \%$ of the record — not 12 %, but the
   first term.
2. **Add the gap handling.** If gaps were filled with zeros before the rule ran, the
   fill creates 2 spurious exceedances per gap edge, and if a running mean was computed
   across gaps, the statistic itself is biased near them.
3. **Add the masking direction.** With the spike contamination in the record, the
   running mean and sd are inflated by the very spikes being tested, so the effective
   threshold is wider than $2\sigma$ and the rule *misses* the large spikes while
   spending its budget on the tails.
4. **Diagnose the shortfall.** The missing 12 % is the sum of the budget (4.6 %), the
   gap-related flags, and the flags from the true outliers — and the fix is to replace
   the rule with a robust threshold on the corrected scale, to flag rather than delete,
   and to record the rule version with the flags.

The lesson: a QC rule with a plausible constant in it can remove a tenth of a data set
without any error appearing anywhere.

## Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Gaussian noise for the budget.** The $0.27\ \%$ figure is exact for Gaussian noise;
   real noise is often heavier-tailed, which raises the false-positive rate at the same
   threshold, so the budget must be measured on the data rather than assumed.
2. **Known labels.** Every confusion matrix here is measured against injected spikes.
   On a real record the labels do not exist, which is why rule evaluation uses
   injections, held-out known events or expert assessment, not a claimed truth.
3. **Independent samples.** The MAD and the standard deviation behave as quoted for
   independent samples; for correlated records the effective sample size is smaller
   ([20-05-01](../../20-signal-processing/05-noise-and-random-signals/01-stochastic-processes.md)),
   which widens the sampling error of any threshold estimate.
4. **One channel at a time.** All rules here are single-channel; the multichannel
   evidence that separates a spike from a genuine transient is not modelled.
5. **A chosen contamination.** The spike counts, sizes and missing fractions are
   teaching values chosen to make the trade-off visible; they are not a claim about
   any instrument's error rate.

## The honest summary

Quality control is decision-making under uncertainty, and the useful questions are
measurable: a $3\sigma$ rule spends a $0.27\ \%$ false-positive budget (5.4 samples of
2000), a naive threshold drifts by 25–40 % depending on the contamination while a
robust one stays within 10 %, and the difference shows up as recall — 0.470 against
0.740 on 100 moderate spikes — rather than as a flag count. Gaps are a representational
problem with a measurable cost: zero-filling 30 % of a record shrinks its standard
deviation from 1.0069 to 0.8677 while presenting 515 fake zeros as measurements. The
procedure that survives audit is to flag rather than delete, to run conservative then
statistical passes, to version the rules, to report both error rates, and to state
explicitly how each estimator treats missing values.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Quoting a threshold without its budget.** $3\sigma$ flags 0.27 % of clean data —
   5.4 samples of 2000, on every clean record.
2. **Using the mean and standard deviation to detect the outliers that inflate them.**
   Measured masking: the naive threshold moves 40 % and recall falls to 0.470.
3. **Judging a rule by its precision or its flag count.** Recall is the number that
   matters for correctness, and it is the one a flag count hides.
4. **Filling gaps with zeros.** At 30 % missing the standard deviation falls from
   1.0069 to 0.8677 and 515 fake measurements enter the analysis.
5. **Deleting flagged data.** It destroys the evidence and makes the decision
   unauditable.
6. **Calibrating a rule on one station.** Noise scales differ by channel, so a
   threshold estimated elsewhere is an assumption, not a result.
