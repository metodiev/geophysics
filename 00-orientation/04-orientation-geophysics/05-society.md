# Geophysics in Society: Hazards, Resources, Environment, Climate

## Learning objectives

- Describe the main societal domains in which geophysics provides evidence, and
  name the measurement behind each.
- Explain why hazard products are probabilistic, and how to read a probability
  statement correctly.
- Distinguish what geophysics can establish directly from what it can only
  constrain, in each societal domain.
- Identify the ethical questions that arise when geophysical evidence is applied
  to public decisions.

## Prerequisites

[Careers, Institutions and Professional Bodies](./04-careers.md) for who produces
this work, and [The Subdisciplines and What Each One Measures](./02-subdisciplines.md)
for what each method can measure.

A note on numbers. This lesson contains **no statistics about losses, deaths,
reserves or market sizes**, because those figures are produced by named agencies
whose published reports should be cited directly and checked for the year in
question. Where a quantity is quoted elsewhere in the curriculum, the source
should be traceable; if it is not, treat it as unverified.

## 1. The five societal domains

Geophysics enters public life through five doors, and they are not independent:

| Domain | Question society asks | Geophysical evidence that helps |
|---|---|---|
| Hazards | How strong will the shaking be, and where? | Seismicity catalogues, ground-motion modelling, site characterisation, geodesy |
| Resources | Where is water, heat, minerals or storage capacity? | Gravity, magnetics, EM, seismic, heat flow, geochemistry-informed rock physics |
| Environment | What is in the ground, and where is it moving? | Resistivity and EM imaging, GPR, hydrogeophysical monitoring, time-lapse surveys |
| Climate | How is the Earth system changing? | Satellite gravimetry, radar altimetry, ice-penetrating radar, GNSS, InSAR |
| Infrastructure | Is it safe to build or operate here? | Site investigation, cavity and fault detection, subsidence and deformation monitoring |

Alongside these sits **verification and security**: the use of seismic and other
monitoring to detect and characterise events of concern, including the
verification regime associated with the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty,
which relies on an international network of monitoring stations. That is a
straightforward application of the seismology in
[06-seismology](../../06-seismology/README.md) to a policy question, and it is one
of the reasons national monitoring capability is maintained.

## 2. Hazards

### Earthquakes

Earthquake hazard work combines three elements: the rate and size distribution of
events on each fault or in each zone, the ground motion a given event produces at
a site, and the response of the built environment. Geophysics supplies the first
two — seismicity catalogues and rates from instrumental monitoring, and
ground-motion prediction informed by wave propagation and site conditions.

Ground motion depends strongly on the shallow subsurface: soft sediments amplify
shaking relative to rock. That is why **microzonation** — mapping the shallow
velocity and resonance structure of a city — is a standard geophysical product,
using methods from
[06-seismology/10-refraction-and-near-surface-seismology](../../06-seismology/10-refraction-and-near-surface-seismology/README.md)
and ambient-noise techniques.

Public earthquake information is an operational geophysical service. The USGS
programme at [https://earthquake.usgs.gov](https://earthquake.usgs.gov) publishes
event locations, magnitudes and associated products as part of a global network,
and regional networks do the same at national scale. These services are also a
practical illustration of the uncertainty discussion in section 7: an early,
automatically determined magnitude is revised as more data arrive.

### Volcanoes

Volcanic monitoring is multi-parameter by nature: seismicity (swarms, tremor),
ground deformation (GNSS and InSAR), gas emissions, gravity changes and thermal
anomalies. Each parameter is a different physical measurement, and no single one
is sufficient, because the underlying process — magma moving and degassing — is
not directly observable. Forecasts are probabilistic statements about the
likelihood of an eruption within a window, and their credibility rests on how
many independent signals agree.

### Tsunamis, landslides and subsidence

Tsunami hazard requires characterising the source (seafloor displacement) and the
propagation, which is why marine bathymetry and seismic source modelling matter;
global relief and bathymetry compilations, such as the NOAA relief grids described
at [https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/mgg/global/](https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/mgg/global/),
underpin both. Landslide and subsidence hazards are often mapped by repeat
topographic measurement and by InSAR, which detects millimetre-scale ground
motion over wide areas.

### Induced seismicity

Injection or extraction of fluids can change stresses enough to trigger
earthquakes — a well-documented phenomenon that turned operational geophysics
into a regulatory subject. The technical content is seismological (detection,
location, magnitude, discrimination from natural events) and hydrological (pore
pressure diffusion). The societal content is a risk-benefit judgement that
geophysics informs but does not settle. See
[06-seismology/12-induced-and-triggered-seismicity](../../06-seismology/12-induced-and-triggered-seismicity/README.md).

## 3. Resources

- **Groundwater.** Resistivity, EM and GPR map aquifer geometry and the
  clay-rich units that confine them; see
  [14-hydrology-hydrogeophysics](../../14-hydrology-hydrogeophysics/README.md).
  Groundwater is the resource on which the largest number of people depend, and it
  is also the one most easily over-exploited, which is why monitoring and
  sustainable-yield questions are as important as exploration.
- **Minerals.** Gravity, magnetics and EM detect ore-related contrasts (density,
  magnetite content, conductivity) and are used alongside geochemistry; see
  [11-exploration-geophysics/02-mineral-exploration](../../11-exploration-geophysics/02-mineral-exploration/README.md).
  Demand for minerals used in low-carbon technologies has made this area
  strategically prominent.
- **Geothermal energy.** Heat flow, magnetics, gravity and seismic methods map
  heat sources, permeability and structure for geothermal development — one of the
  clearest cases where geophysics is central rather than supporting.
- **Hydrocarbons.** Reflection seismology and potential fields remain the core
  methods for exploring for and characterising reservoirs, and the same methods
  are now applied to storage. This is also the domain where the ethical tension
  between extraction and climate policy is most visible, and the tension is
  resolved by policy, not by measurement.
- **Subsurface storage.** Depleted reservoirs and deep saline aquifers are
  candidates for carbon dioxide storage; geophysics provides the characterisation
  and, crucially, the *monitoring* — repeated seismic and gravity surveys that
  detect whether the injected fluid stays where intended, together with the
  gravity and geodetic signatures of large-scale mass change.

## 4. Environment

- **Contamination.** Conductivity contrasts make EM and resistivity the workhorses
  of contaminated-site investigation, with GPR for shallow detail and seismic for
  stratigraphic control.
- **Waste and legacy sites.** Landfill characterisation, buried drum detection and
  the integrity of containment all rely on near-surface methods.
- **Radioactive waste.** Siting decisions for long-lived waste depend on
  characterising rock properties and long-term stability, using gravity,
  magnetics, seismic and hydrogeological methods together.
- **Permafrost and the cryosphere.** Ground-penetrating radar and resistivity
  surveys map the active layer and ice content; thaw changes both ground
  stability and the carbon cycle, so the measurements serve engineering and
  climate purposes simultaneously.
- **Monitored natural attenuation.** Time-lapse geophysical surveys, repeated over
  months or years, show whether a plume is changing. Designing such a survey is a
  question about *resolution in time*, not only in space.

## 5. Climate

Geophysics contributes to climate science in ways that are easy to overlook
because they are not "climate modelling":

- **Ice mass.** Satellite gravimetry measures changes in the total mass of ice
  sheets and glaciers, and radar altimetry measures their surface height;
  together these quantify mass loss. NASA's GRACE pages at
  [https://grace.jpl.nasa.gov](https://grace.jpl.nasa.gov) document the mission
  and its data products.
- **Sea level.** Tide gauges and satellite altimetry measure sea level, and
  geodesy separates ocean-volume change from vertical land motion — including the
  ongoing adjustment of the solid Earth to the removal of ice sheets, which
  affects both instruments and coastlines.
- **Ocean heat and circulation.** Oceanographic measurements use acoustic and
  satellite methods among others.
- **Earth-system budgets.** Mass and energy budgets are the accounting framework
  in which ice loss, sea level and ocean heat are reconciled, and they are the
  same budget reasoning used for gravity and heat-flow problems in this
  curriculum.
- **Climate data services.** Public archives such as the Copernicus Climate Data
  Store at [https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu](https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu)
  and the broader Copernicus programme at
  [https://www.copernicus.eu](https://www.copernicus.eu) provide the observational
  records. For specific quantitative statements about the climate system — rates,
  trends, attributions — the assessment reports published by the
  Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change are the appropriate authoritative
  source, and any figure you reuse should be traced to the report, chapter and
  year.

**Two cautions.** First, do not quote climate numbers from memory; go to the
assessment reports. Second, respect the division of labour: geophysics measures
mass, height, deformation and radiation; the interpretation of those measurements
in terms of future climate requires the coupled models of
[16-atmosphere-climate](../../16-atmosphere-climate/README.md).

## 6. Infrastructure and engineering

Before a tunnel, a dam, a pipeline, an offshore platform or a building is built,
someone has to characterise the ground. That work is geophysics, often combined
with drilling:

- **Site investigation.** Seismic refraction and surface-wave methods give
  stiffness and depth to rock; resistivity gives water and clay; GPR gives
  shallow structural detail.
- **Cavities and voids.** Microgravity surveys detect low-density voids;
  GPR and seismic methods image them.
- **Seafloor and offshore.** Acoustic methods map the seabed and shallow
  sub-seabed for cable routes, platforms and wind installations.
- **Monitoring.** Repeat surveys and InSAR detect settlement, slope movement and
  structural deformation, converting one-off characterisation into ongoing
  assurance.
- **Critical infrastructure.** GNSS is not only a science tool; timing and
  positioning from satellite navigation support many civilian systems, so
  geodetic infrastructure has societal dependence implications well beyond
  geophysics.

## 7. Communicating hazard and uncertainty

Hazard products are probabilities, and probabilities are routinely misread. Four
practical rules:

1. **State the period and the area.** "A given probability in the next fifty
   years, for this zone" is a complete statement; "a high risk" is not.
2. **Give the rate rather than only a percentage when you can.** A rate per
   thousand years is often easier to reason about than a percentage.
3. **Separate aleatory from epistemic uncertainty.** The randomness of future
   events is different from our ignorance about the model, and they should not be
   merged into one number.
4. **Say what would change your estimate.** New data, a revised model, a larger
   catalogue; naming this makes the product honest and the scientist accountable.

The same discipline applies in resources and environment: a detection limit is a
probability of detection, and an inversion result is a distribution of plausible
models, not a photograph.

## 8. Ethics

Five questions that arise repeatedly, and which a curriculum can raise but not
answer:

1. **Extraction versus climate policy.** The same methods that find hydrocarbons
   find geothermal heat and storage capacity. Which projects one works on is a
   personal and professional decision, and professional bodies' ethics statements
   are the starting point for the discussion.
2. **Induced seismicity.** Who bears the risk of a project's seismic side effects,
   and how is it communicated and regulated?
3. **Data access and equity.** Data that are open in one country may be restricted
   in another; the capacity to use them is distributed unevenly, and building
   local capability is part of responsible practice.
4. **Dual use.** Monitoring techniques developed for verification and hazard have
   potential military applications; institutional safeguards exist for this
   reason.
5. **Honest uncertainty.** Overstating a result can cause harm — a false
   reassurance about safety, or a wasted investment; understating it can cause
   harm by preventing action. Stating uncertainty clearly is an ethical duty, not
   a hedge.

## Common mistakes

1. **Reading a probability as a prediction.** "Ten per cent in thirty years" is a
   rate statement, not a forecast of a specific event.
2. **Treating a geophysical model as a picture of the subsurface.** It is an
   inference with resolution and uncertainty.
3. **Quoting hazard, reserve or climate statistics from memory.** Cite the
   agency, the report and the year — or say nothing.
4. **Assuming detection implies characterisation.** Finding an anomaly is not
   the same as knowing what caused it.
5. **Ignoring the monitoring requirement.** Many societal applications — storage,
   contamination, subsidence — are about change over time, not a single survey.
6. **Confusing what geophysics measures with the decision it informs.** The
   decision is political, economic and ethical; the measurement is evidence.

## Real-world applications

- **Public warning.** Earthquake, volcano and tsunami warning systems are
  operational products of geophysical networks.
- **Building codes and land-use planning.** Hazard maps and microzonation enter
  regulation directly.
- **Water management.** Aquifer characterisation and monitoring underpin permits
  and allocations.
- **Energy transition.** Geothermal and carbon-storage projects are planned and
  monitored on geophysical evidence.
- **Insurance and risk transfer.** Catastrophe models use the same hazard data,
  and their assumptions are auditable in the way section 7 recommends.
- **International verification.** Monitoring networks for nuclear-test
  verification rely on seismological methods and analysis.
- **Open data and public trust.** Publishing both data and uncertainty, as the
  services linked above do, is what allows independent scrutiny of a claim.
