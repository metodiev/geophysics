# Careers, Institutions and Professional Bodies

## Learning objectives

- Classify the kinds of organisations that employ people with geophysical skills,
  and state what each typically needs from them.
- Describe the common role archetypes — acquisition, processing, interpretation,
  modelling, software, instrumentation, regulation, research — and the skills
  each relies on.
- Explain what professional societies do, and why accreditation and licensure
  arrangements differ between countries and must be checked locally.
- Build an evidence plan for a target role, using artefacts rather than claims.

## Prerequisites

[The Subdisciplines and What Each One Measures](./02-subdisciplines.md). Careers
follow methods: what you can measure determines what you can be hired to do.

A note on what this lesson deliberately omits. It contains **no salary figures, no
employment percentages and no market sizes.** Those numbers depend on country,
sector, commodity cycle and year, they change quickly, and quoting them from
memory would be exactly the kind of fabricated statistic this curriculum forbids.
For current figures, consult a national statistical agency, a professional body's
salary survey, or the employer directly — and cite the source and date when you
reuse the number.

## 1. Categories of employer

Geophysics is applied in more places than most students expect. The categories
below are organised by what the employer's core problem is.

| Category | Examples of organisations | What the work is about |
|---|---|---|
| National geological and geophysical surveys | Government earth-science agencies | Regional mapping, national data archives, hazard and resource assessment |
| Research institutes | Publicly funded geoscience research centres, such as those in the GFZ network of institutions ([https://www.gfz-potsdam.de](https://www.gfz-potsdam.de)) | Long-horizon research, observatory operation, method development |
| Universities | Departments of geophysics, geology, physics, engineering | Teaching, doctoral research, fundamental and applied projects |
| Resource industries | Mineral exploration and mining companies; oil and gas operators and service companies | Finding and characterising deposits and reservoirs |
| Energy transition | Geothermal developers, carbon-storage project operators, critical-minerals companies | Characterising subsurface for heat, storage or new mineral supply |
| Engineering and geotechnical consultancies | Ground investigation firms serving construction and infrastructure | Site characterisation before building: tunnels, foundations, dams, pipelines |
| Environmental and water consultancies | Environmental firms, water agencies, regulators | Aquifer mapping, contamination investigation, monitoring |
| Surveying and geodetic services | National geodetic agencies, mapping organisations, such as the national geodetic infrastructure illustrated by [https://geodesy.noaa.gov](https://geodesy.noaa.gov) | Reference frames, positioning, deformation monitoring |
| Space and Earth observation | Space agencies and Earth-observation programmes, for example [https://www.esa.int](https://www.esa.int) and [https://www.copernicus.eu](https://www.copernicus.eu) | Satellite missions, data products, applied remote sensing |
| Catastrophe risk and insurance | Reinsurance and risk-modelling companies | Quantifying hazard and loss from earthquakes, storms, floods |
| Defence and verification | National laboratories and monitoring organisations | Monitoring for nuclear explosions and other events of concern |
| Data, software and instrument vendors | Acquisition-system manufacturers, software houses, cloud data providers | Building the tools everyone else uses |
| Utilities and infrastructure operators | Power, water, transport, offshore operators | Monitoring assets, managing ground risk |
| Education and communication | Schools, museums, media, outreach programmes | Translating the science for a general audience |

Two observations about this list. First, **the same skill set serves very
different employers**, because the underlying problem — infer something about the
subsurface or the Earth system from measurements — is the same. Second, **the
sector with the most vacancies varies with the commodity and policy cycle**, so a
career plan built on one sector is fragile; a plan built on capabilities is not.

## 2. Role archetypes

Within an organisation, work tends to fall into recognisable archetypes. These
matter more than job titles, which vary widely between employers.

| Role archetype | Core activity | Skills that matter most |
|---|---|---|
| Acquisition / field geophysicist | Designing and running surveys; instrument deployment, quality control in the field | Field safety, instrument behaviour, logistics, decision-making under time pressure |
| Processing geophysicist | Turning raw recordings into usable data: corrections, filtering, velocity analysis, imaging | Signal processing, numeracy, scripting, attention to artefacts |
| Interpretation / integration geophysicist | Turning processed data into geological or engineering conclusions, usually combining methods | Physics, geology, uncertainty reasoning, clear writing |
| Modelling and inversion specialist | Forward modelling, inversion, uncertainty quantification, method development | Mathematics, inverse theory, numerical methods, high-performance computing |
| Geodesist / deformation specialist | Reference frames, GNSS and InSAR processing, time-series analysis | Coordinate systems, statistics, careful error handling |
| Software and data engineer | Building pipelines, tooling, formats, reproducibility infrastructure | Software engineering, data formats, cloud and HPC practice |
| Instrumentation and hardware engineer | Sensor design, electronics, calibration, field ruggedisation | Electronics, physics, practical engineering |
| Project and survey manager | Scoping work, managing contracts, safety and quality assurance | Communication, planning, regulatory knowledge |
| Regulator, policy or assurance specialist | Reviewing submissions, setting standards, assessing risk | Broad technical literacy, ethics, written argument |
| Academic researcher and lecturer | Research, supervision, teaching, peer review | The full research cycle, including writing and reviewing |

Most careers combine several of these at different times. A field geophysicist who
becomes a project manager has not left geophysics; they have moved along a
recognised progression, and the technical judgement is what makes them good at it.

## 3. The skills employers actually test

Across sectors, the same capabilities recur in job descriptions and interviews:

1. **Physics and quantitative reasoning.** Can you estimate the size of an
   effect before computing it, and check a result for plausibility?
2. **Programming and data handling.** Can you process a real dataset, and is your
   analysis reproducible by someone else?
3. **Uncertainty literacy.** Can you state what your result constrains and what it
   does not, without either overclaiming or hiding behind vagueness?
4. **Field competence and safety.** Can you work safely and productively outside,
   often with expensive equipment and other people?
5. **Communication.** Can you explain a caveat to a non-specialist decision-maker
   without losing the technical content?
6. **Domain context.** Do you understand the geological or engineering problem
   the measurement is meant to answer?

Notice that item 3 is the one that candidates most often fail in technical
interviews, and item 6 is the one that separates a competent processor from a
useful interpreter.

## 4. Professional bodies and societies

Professional and learned societies are the institutional backbone of the subject.
The best-known international examples include the American Geophysical Union
(AGU), the European Geosciences Union (EGU), the Society of Exploration
Geophysicists (SEG), the European Association of Geoscientists and Engineers
(EAGE), the International Union of Geodesy and Geophysics (IUGG), the American
Association of Petroleum Geologists (AAPG), the Geological Society of America
(GSA), and the International Association of Hydrogeologists (IAH). National
societies exist for most countries as well.

What they typically provide:

- **Journals and conferences**, which are where results are published and
  scrutinised.
- **Ethics and professional-conduct statements**, which are the reference point
  when a technical decision has a moral dimension — for example reporting an
  inconvenient result, or declining work you are not competent to sign off.
- **Technical standards and best-practice guidance**, often produced jointly with
  regulators.
- **Networks and mentoring**, which in practice account for many job transitions.
- **Continuing professional development** and, in some countries, a route to
  registration.

Check the current policy documents directly rather than relying on a summary:
society policies on ethics, authorship and data availability are revised from
time to time.

## 5. Accreditation and licensure: check locally

Whether you must be registered to practise, and what the protected titles are,
**varies by country and by field of practice**, and in some places varies within a
country (for example, requirements for signing off engineering-geology or
geotechnical work may differ from those for exploration work). Some jurisdictions
regulate geoscientists through a statutory body; others rely on employer
assurance and voluntary registration.

Because the rules change and are jurisdiction-specific, this lesson does not state
them. If you need to know, ask: the relevant national professional body, the
regulator responsible for your sector, and your employer's quality-assurance
function. All three are authoritative in a way that a general curriculum cannot
be.

## 6. Research careers

The academic and public-research route is broadly: an undergraduate degree in a
physical science, an MSc or equivalent specialisation, a doctorate, then
postdoctoral research and, if the opportunity arises, a permanent research or
faculty position. The proportions vary enormously by country and over time, and
there is no guarantee that a doctorate leads to a permanent academic post; many
researchers move into the other categories above, often to their benefit.

The curricula that matter for this route are the same as for industry, with two
additions: [18-inverse-problems](../../18-inverse-problems/README.md) and
[24-research-papers-writing](../../24-research-papers-writing/README.md). Being
able to design a study, write it up and defend it is the actual job; the data
processing is the means.

## 7. Where geophysical skills transfer

The capabilities in section 3 travel well beyond geophysics:

- **Data science and machine learning.** Geophysical data are noisy, spatially
  correlated, physically constrained and often huge; that combination is good
  training for real-world data work.
- **Earth observation and GIS.** Satellite and geospatial employers need exactly
  the coordinate, calibration and uncertainty skills this curriculum teaches.
- **Climate and environmental analysis.** Time-series handling, mass-budget
  reasoning and honest uncertainty are shared with climate science.
- **Energy transition roles.** Geothermal, carbon storage and critical-mineral
  work all require subsurface characterisation, and they are staffed largely by
  people trained in conventional resources.
- **Risk, insurance and civil protection.** Hazard quantification is an applied
  inverse-problem discipline.
- **Software and instrument companies.** Deep domain knowledge is what makes a
  tool usable by domain specialists.

## 8. Building an evidence plan for a target role

Instead of asking "do I have enough experience", produce evidence:

1. **Choose three target roles** from the archetypes in section 2, in different
   employer categories.
2. **List the capabilities each advertises** (read actual postings or programme
   descriptions, and note the repeated verbs).
3. **Map each capability to an artefact you have made or can make.** The
   milestone vocabulary in
   [Research Milestones](../01-study-roadmap/05-milestones.md) is designed for
   this: "processed a real archive end to end with stated provenance" is stronger
   evidence than "familiar with data processing".
4. **Close the largest gap first.** A single missing capability, closed properly,
   changes your candidacy more than incremental improvement in everything.
5. **Write the two-sentence version of each artefact**: what you did, and what it
   showed. Interviews run on those two sentences.

## 9. Professional ethics in practice

Three situations recur across all the employer categories above:

- **Reporting an inconvenient result.** A survey that does not support the
  expected target is a finding. Suppressing it is misconduct, and it is also bad
  business, because the ground does not change to match the report.
- **Competence boundaries.** Working outside your competence without saying so is
  the most common professional failure in technical fields; the honest answer is
  often "I can do A, and for B we need a specialist".
- **Uncertainty in public communication.** Hazard products are probabilistic.
  Presenting a probability as a certainty, in either direction, is a failure of
  the science as well as of the communication.

## Common mistakes

1. **Treating "geophysicist" as one job.** The archetypes differ more than the
   titles suggest.
2. **Assuming the academic route is the default.** Most people with geophysical
   training work outside academia, and many prefer it.
3. **Relying on remembered market statistics.** Rates of employment, salaries and
   demand change; cite current sources or say nothing.
4. **Assuming professional registration rules are universal.** They are not;
   check the jurisdiction you will work in.
5. **Presenting coursework as capability.** Employers test whether you can
   produce and defend an artefact.
6. **Neglecting writing.** Reports, memos and proposals are the deliverable in
   most roles, and the skill is learnable.

## Real-world applications

- **Job searching.** The employer categories in section 1 are the search space;
  the archetypes in section 2 are the search terms.
- **Negotiating scope.** Knowing which archetype a role really is prevents taking
  a job that is entirely fieldwork when you wanted modelling, or the reverse.
- **Team building.** Understanding the archetypes is how a project manager
  assembles coverage of the skills a survey requires.
- **Career pivots.** Section 7 is the map for moving between sectors without
  starting over.

  [https://www.earthscope.org](https://www.earthscope.org). — These are the

  library or a DOI resolver before citing them yourself.
