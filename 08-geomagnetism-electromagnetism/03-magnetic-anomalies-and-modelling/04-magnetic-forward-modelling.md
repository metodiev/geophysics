# Forward Modelling Magnetic Bodies

Everything in this module is built on a forward model: given a body, predict the
anomaly. This lesson develops the two equivalent representations of a uniformly
magnetised body — a **distribution of magnetic surface charge** and a
**distribution of dipoles** — verifies each against an exact solution, and then
measures the error of the approximation everyone actually uses, the single
dipole. The result is a working rule for when the far-field formula may be
trusted and when it must be replaced by an integral.

> All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Derive** the field of a uniformly magnetised body as an integral of magnetic
   surface charge, $\sigma_m = \mathbf{M}\cdot\hat n$.
2. **Derive** the exact axial field of a uniformly charged disk and **use** it to
   validate a numerical integrator.
3. **Implement** a prism anomaly by two independent routes (surface charges and
   a volume sum of dipoles) and **show** that they agree.
4. **Show** that both routes converge to the single-dipole field in the far
   field, and **measure** the rate of convergence.
5. **Measure** the error of the single-dipole approximation as a function of
   distance and **state** the distance, in body widths, beyond which it is
   acceptable.
6. **Compute** how burial depth changes the amplitude and the half-width of an
   anomaly.
7. **Compare** the anomaly shapes of an induced and a remanent body and
   **identify** the features that distinguish them.

## 1. Two equivalent representations

### 1.1 Magnetic surface charge

Outside a body of uniform magnetisation the field may be written as a gradient of
a scalar potential generated by surface charge,

$$\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{P}) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_S \sigma_m(\mathbf{Q})\,
\frac{\hat r}{r^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}S, \qquad
\sigma_m = \mathbf{M}\cdot\hat n, \qquad
\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{P}-\mathbf{Q}, \qquad r = \left|\mathbf{r}\right|,$$

and $\mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \mathbf{H}$ outside. The derivation is the standard
one: inside, $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B}/\mu_0 - \mathbf{M}$, and
$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ gives $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{H} = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{M}$,
so the magnetisation behaves as a volume charge $-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{M}$ plus the
surface term above. For a uniformly magnetised body the volume charge vanishes
and only the surface remains.

For a vertical-sided prism the six faces carry at most three distinct charge
densities: $\pm M_z$ on the top and bottom and $\pm M_x$, $\pm M_y$ on the four
sides. A vertical magnetisation therefore produces only two charged faces.

### 1.2 Volume dipoles

The same field is the sum of the fields of all the dipoles that make up the body:

$$\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{P}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int_V
\frac{3\left(\mathbf{M}\cdot\hat r\right)\hat r-\mathbf{M}}{r^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}V'.$$

The two expressions are mathematically identical outside the body — they are
different rearrangements of the same integral — but they are *numerically*
different, and implementing both is a genuine check on an implementation rather
than a tautology, because the discretisations, the quadratures and the
singularities encountered are different.

## 2. An exact test case: the uniformly charged disk

Before trusting any integrator, test it on something with a closed-form answer.
For a disk of radius $R$ and uniform surface charge $\sigma$, the field on the
axis at distance $h$ from the plane is

$$H_z(h) = \frac{\sigma}{2}\left(1 - \frac{h}{\sqrt{R^{2}+h^{2}}}\right).$$

*Derivation.* Put the disk in $z=0$ and observe at $(0,0,h)$. For a ring element
at radius $\rho$, every contribution has the same axial component
$h/\left(\rho^{2}+h^{2}\right)^{3/2}$, so

$$H_z = \frac{\sigma}{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{R}
\frac{h\,\rho}{\left(\rho^{2}+h^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\,\mathrm{d}\rho\,\mathrm{d}\theta
= \frac{\sigma h}{2}\left[\frac{-1}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+h^{2}}}\right]_{0}^{R}
= \frac{\sigma}{2}\left(1-\frac{h}{\sqrt{R^{2}+h^{2}}}\right).$$

As $R \to \infty$ this gives $H_z \to \sigma/2$, the infinite-sheet result, and
as $h\to\infty$ it gives $\sigma\pi R^{2}/(4\pi h^{2}) = q/(4\pi h^{2})$ with
$q = \sigma\pi R^{2}$ the total charge: the monopole limit.

**Numerical check.** The demonstration integrates the disk in polar coordinates
with Gauss–Legendre quadrature ($80\times80$ nodes) for $R = 3000$ m and
$\sigma = 1$ A m$^{-1}$ and compares with the closed form:

| $h$ (km) | $H_z$ numeric (A m$^{-1}$) | $H_z$ closed form (A m$^{-1}$) | relative error |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 0.417801 | 0.417801 | $1.2\times10^{-15}$ |
| 1.0 | 0.341886 | 0.341886 | $9.7\times10^{-16}$ |
| 2.0 | 0.222650 | 0.222650 | $7.5\times10^{-16}$ |
| 5.0 | 0.071254 | 0.071254 | $1.9\times10^{-16}$ |

The agreement is at double-precision roundoff. The integrator, the prefactor
$1/4\pi$ and the sign convention are all correct.

## 3. A prism by two independent routes

Take a **synthetic** prism $4\times4\times1$ km with its top at $0.5$ km depth,
uniformly magnetised at $M = 5.0$ A m$^{-1}$ and $60^\circ$ inclination
($\mathbf{m} = M V\,\hat m$, $V = 16$ km$^3$, so $m = 8.00\times10^{10}$
A m$^2$). The measurement direction is vertical, so $\Delta T = B_z$.

- Surface charges: six faces, Gauss–Legendre quadrature with $48\times48$ nodes
  per face. Peak anomaly over the profile: **1094.587** nT.
- Volume dipoles: $30\times30\times15$ cells, each carrying
  $\mathbf{M}\,\Delta V$. Peak anomaly: **1094.585** nT.

The two agree to $1.05$ nT, or $0.096\%$ of the peak. That residual is the
*discretisation* of the volume sum, not a disagreement about physics: refining
the cell grid drives it down. Implementing both routes and checking them against
each other is the cheapest available defence against a sign error in a face
normal.

## 4. The far-field limit, and where the dipole approximation dies

### 4.1 Convergence to a dipole

Moving the observation point away along the $x$ axis and comparing the exact
prism field with the field of a single dipole of the same moment at the prism
centre:

| Distance (km) | charge route / dipole | volume-sum route / dipole |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 1.05822 | 1.05816 |
| 20 | 1.01459 | 1.01457 |
| 40 | 1.00360 | 1.00360 |
| 80 | 1.00089 | 1.00089 |

Both routes converge to the dipole, and they converge to the *same* dipole: at
80 km the deviation is $0.09\%$ and falling as $r^{-2}$. The two numerical routes
track each other to five decimal places at every distance, which is a stronger
statement than either agreeing with the dipole.

### 4.2 The error, measured

| $x$ (km) | exact (nT) | single dipole (nT) | error (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 758.172 | $-896.575$ | 218.3 |
| 2.0 | $-95.755$ | $-677.196$ | 607.2 |
| 3.0 | $-323.313$ | $-267.204$ | 17.4 |
| 5.0 | $-76.346$ | $-63.636$ | 16.6 |
| 8.0 | $-16.809$ | $-15.429$ | 8.2 |
| 12.0 | $-4.636$ | $-4.455$ | 3.9 |
| 20.0 | $-0.944$ | $-0.931$ | 1.4 |

Two things are worth stating plainly. The error at 1–2 km is not a few per cent
but a factor, **including a sign change**: at $x = 1$ km the exact anomaly is
positive and the dipole estimate negative. And "far field" means several body
widths, not several tens of metres: at 3 km — three quarters of a body width —
the error is still 17%, and only at 20 km, five body widths, does it fall below
2%.

Directly above the centre the failure is worse than anywhere on the profile:

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Exact anomaly over the centre (nT) | 920.8 |
| Single dipole at the body centre (nT) | 13856.4 |
| Ratio | 15.0 |

A 15-fold error means that any interpretation which fits dipoles to anomalies
measured above shallow bodies is fitting the wrong model. This is the single most
common error in amateur and student magnetic modelling, and it is not subtle.

## 5. Burial depth, amplitude and half-width

Keeping the body and the magnetisation fixed and varying the depth of its top
face ($48\times48$ quadrature, profile sampled every $0.1$ km):

| Top depth (km) | Peak $\Delta T$ (nT) | Half-width at half-maximum (km) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 1168.71 | 1.75 |
| 1.0 | 785.11 | 1.80 |
| 2.0 | 392.16 | 2.00 |
| 4.0 | 115.95 | 2.65 |

The amplitude falls by a factor of 2.5 per kilometre of additional burial, close
to the $z^{-2}$ one expects for a body that is wide compared with its depth (the
far-field $z^{-3}$ sets in only when the depth exceeds the horizontal extent, and
here the body is 4 km wide). The half-width grows slowly — from 1.75 to 2.65 km
— because it is already dominated by the body's own width. **Amplitude tells you
about depth and magnetisation together; width tells you about depth alone.**
That asymmetry is the basis of every depth-estimation method in
[05](05-magnetic-depth-estimation.md).

## 6. Induced and remanent bodies: the same rock, two shapes

The same prism, the same $|\mathbf{M}| = 5.0$ A m$^{-1}$, three directions,
measured with a vertical field:

| Magnetisation | Peak (nT) | Peak position (km) | Minimum (nT) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $I = +60^\circ$ (induced) | 1168.71 | $-1.5$ | $-395.21$ |
| $I = -60^\circ$ (reversed) | 395.21 | $-2.5$ | $-1168.71$ |
| $I = +90^\circ$ | 1069.69 | $+1.0$ | $-157.12$ |

The reversed body is not a mirror image of the induced one: its peak is
$1168.71$ nT against $395.21$ nT, because the induced body's positive lobe is
where its field adds to the ambient field. The vertical body has the largest
negative lobe of the three. Position, amplitude and sign together carry the
direction of $\mathbf{M}$, and none of them carries it alone.

## 7. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Uniform magnetisation.** Exact only for ellipsoids; an excellent
   approximation for weakly magnetic bodies of any shape, because the internal
   field is barely perturbed. For strongly magnetic bodies the demagnetising
   field is shape-dependent and the charge distribution changes; the induced
   moment then saturates, as quantified in
   [07 Applications](07-magnetic-uses.md).
2. **The observation point is outside the body.** The surface-charge integral
   gives $\mathbf{H}$; inside the body $\mathbf{B} = \mu_0(\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{M})$
   must be reassembled explicitly.
3. **Air or vacuum between body and sensor**, i.e. neglect of the magnetic
   properties of the overburden. This is safe: sediments have
   $\chi \lesssim 10^{-3}$.
4. **Quadrature accuracy.** The Gauss–Legendre nodes used here reproduce the
   disk to $10^{-15}$ relative, so the quadrature is not the limiting error; the
   volume-sum discretisation is, at $0.1\%$ for the grid used.
5. **The single-dipole approximation needs several body widths.** Quantified in
   Section 4.2; the failure near a body is qualitative, not marginal.
6. **The field is static.** No induction, no time dependence; the response of a
   conductive body at the frequencies used in magnetic surveys is negligible.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Using the dipole formula near the body.** A 15-fold error and a sign change
   are available at one body width.
2. **Forgetting that only faces with a normal component of $\mathbf{M}$ carry
   charge.** A vertically magnetised prism has two charged faces, not six.
3. **Sign errors in the outward normal.** They are the commonest implementation
   bug; the two-route check of Section 3 exists to catch them.
4. **Confusing the $\mathbf{H}$ of the charge integral with $\mathbf{B}$.** The
   factor $\mu_0$ is not optional, and inside the body the two differ by
   $\mu_0\mathbf{M}$.
5. **Reading a half-width as a depth.** For a wide body the half-width is set by
   the width, not the depth (Section 5).
6. **Treating a strongly magnetic body as uniformly magnetised.** The
   demagnetising field is then large and shape-dependent.
