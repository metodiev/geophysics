# Archaeomagnetic Dating and Secular Variation Curves

Baked clay, a hearth lining, a kiln floor or a lava flow cools through the Curie
temperature of its magnetic minerals and locks in the direction of the ambient
field. If that field direction changes from century to century — it does — then
the direction recorded in the material is a clock. **Archaeomagnetic dating** is
the business of reading that clock: measure a direction, compare it with a
reference curve of the field direction for the region, and report the age
intervals that match.

This lesson is written around the honest structure of that procedure. The
measurement (a direction, with an angular uncertainty) is one input; the
**secular variation curve** is the other; and the date's precision and its
uniqueness are properties of the *pair*. A direction that the curve passes
through twice cannot be dated, however precise the measurement; a curve that
moves slowly gives poor precision, however good the instrument; and a curve with a
systematic offset — from a tectonic rotation, a non-dipole field, or a
mis-calibrated reference site — biases every date calibrated against it. All three
statements are quantified in this lesson with a synthetic curve, because the
synthetic case is the only one in which the true age is known.

All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**. The secular-variation curve
used here is generated by the demonstration from a few oscillators plus a random
walk; it is a teaching curve, not a regional reference curve, and no
archaeological site, sample or published date is described.

   direction, and **state** the conditions under which it does not.
3. **Construct** the direction-to-age map of a secular variation curve and

4. **Quantify** the dating precision as (angular uncertainty)/(rate of change of

7. **State** what an archaeomagnetic date is a statement about, and what it is
   not.

## 1. The field direction as a measurable quantity

The geomagnetic field at a point is a vector, and its direction is described by the
**declination** $D$ (measured from true north toward east) and the **inclination**
$I$ (measured downward from the horizontal). A magnetic measurement of a baked
material gives these two numbers in the *material's* coordinate system, which is
why the field orientation of the sample as found in situ — its azimuth and its
tilt — is part of the measurement, not a post-processing detail.

For a centred dipole, the field direction is a function of latitude alone:

$$B_r=\frac{2\mu_0m\cos\theta}{4\pi r^3},\qquad
B_\theta=\frac{\mu_0m\sin\theta}{4\pi r^3},\qquad
\tan I=\frac{-B_\theta}{B_r}=\frac{2\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}=2\tan\lambda,$$

with $\lambda$ the latitude and $\theta$ the colatitude. This is the **geocentric
axial dipole (GAD)** relation, and it is the reason magnetic directions carry a
latitude signature at all. The demonstration evaluates it, using the exact
constants $\mu_0=4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{H\,m^{-1}}$ and the Earth's mean radius
$6371\ \mathrm{km}$, with a SYNTHETIC dipole moment of $8.0\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{A\,m^2}$
(not a measured value of the geomagnetic moment):

| latitude (°) | GAD inclination (°) | field magnitude ($\mathrm{\mu T}$) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.000 | 30.9 |
| 15 | 28.187 | 33.9 |
| 30 | 49.107 | 40.9 |
| 45 | 63.435 | 48.9 |
| 60 | 73.898 | 55.8 |
| 75 | 82.369 | 60.3 |
| 90 | 90.000 | 61.9 |

Two uses follow. First, a **consistency check**: if a dated archaeological
assemblage at a known latitude has a mean inclination far from the GAD value, the
material, its orientation, or the curve is wrong. Second, a **caution**: the GAD
relation is a time average; the actual field of any century departs from it by the
non-dipole part, which is exactly what a secular variation curve records. The
synthetic site used below is at $45^\circ$, where $\tan I=2\tan45^\circ$ gives
$I=63.435^\circ$.

## 2. How a direction becomes an age

### 2.1 The curve

A **secular variation curve** is a table $[D(t),I(t)]$ for a region, with an
uncertainty envelope for each epoch, built from well-dated archaeological
structures, lavas and sediments. The demonstration generates a SYNTHETIC curve
from three damped oscillators plus a random walk, sampled every $5\ \mathrm{yr}$
from $2000\ \mathrm{yr\,BP}$ to the present:

- declination range $-12.82^\circ$ to $+11.18^\circ$;
- inclination range $+60.50^\circ$ to $+67.33^\circ$;
- median $|\mathrm{d}D/\mathrm{d}t|=5.194^\circ$ per century.

That last number is the one that makes the method work. A direction that moved
only $0.5^\circ$ per century would be useless for dating; the geomagnetic secular
variation in mid-latitudes is fast enough that a $1^\circ$ measurement
discriminates a few decades.

### 2.2 Matching a measurement

Given a measured direction $(D_m,I_m)$ and an angular tolerance $s$, the
admissible ages are

$$\mathcal{A}=\left\{t:\
\arccos\left[\sin I_m\sin I(t)+\cos I_m\cos I(t)\cos\left(D_m-D(t)\right)\right]
<s\right\},$$

the great-circle angular distance between the two directions. The demonstration
takes the SYNTHETIC measurement at $750\ \mathrm{yr\,BP}$ with a $1.0^\circ$ error
in declination and $-0.8^\circ$ in inclination ($D_m=9.60^\circ$,
$I_m=63.32^\circ$) and tabulates the admissible ages by tolerance:

| tolerance (°) | samples admitted | years admitted | intervals |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 13 | 65 | 1 |
| 2.0 | 55 | 275 | 2 |
| 3.0 | 111 | 555 | 3 |
| 5.0 | 186 | 930 | 4 |

At $2.0^\circ$ the admitted ages are $-795$ to $-655\ \mathrm{yr\,BP}$ (width
$140\ \mathrm{yr}$, midpoint $-725$) **and** $-130$ to $-5\ \mathrm{yr\,BP}$
(width $125\ \mathrm{yr}$). The true age, $-750$, is inside the first interval.
The second interval is not an artefact of the measurement: **the curve passes
through the measured direction twice**, so the data admit two dates. This is the
central ambiguity of the method, and it is a property of the curve.

Resolving it needs evidence from outside the magnetic measurement: stratigraphic
order (the dated structure is beneath one that is already dated), a second
independent dating method (radiocarbon, dendrochronology), knowledge that the
material cannot be younger than a historical event, or a second class of material
with a different error budget. Without one of those, the honest report is both
intervals.

## 3. Precision is a property of the curve

A first-order estimate of the age precision follows from the chain rule: if the
direction changes at rate $R$ (degrees per century) and the measurement is
uncertain by $s$ degrees, the age uncertainty is

$$\sigma_t\approx \frac{s}{R}.$$

Measured from the SYNTHETIC curve:

| epoch (yr BP) | $\lvert\mathrm{d}D/\mathrm{d}t\rvert$ (°/century) | $\sigma_t$ for $s=1^\circ$ (yr) | for $s=3^\circ$ (yr) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1800 | 2.004 | 50 | 150 |
| 1200 | 5.174 | 19 | 58 |
| 600 | 7.073 | 14 | 42 |
| 200 | 8.870 | 11 | 34 |

The same measurement, the same instrument, and a fivefold difference in precision
between epochs. Three consequences:

- **A date is not better than its epoch allows.** Quoting "$\pm20$ years" for a
  material dated to an epoch where the curve moves at $2^\circ$ per century is
  arithmetically impossible with a $1^\circ$ measurement.
- **The instrument is rarely the limit.** With modern measurements good to a few
  tenths of a degree, the curve's own uncertainty dominates at most epochs.
- **A still-stand is fatal.** Where the curve turns and the direction is stationary
  in time, $R\to0$ and the age is unbounded: the direction simply does not change,
  so nothing in it records *when*.

## 4. Systematic curve errors

Suppose the local curve differs from the reference curve by a constant declination
offset $\delta$ — what a tectonic rotation of the site, a small unrecognised
non-dipole feature, or a mis-oriented reference data set would produce. Dating the
same SYNTHETIC measurement against the offset curve:

| offset (°) | recovered age (yr BP) | age error (yr) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | $-720$ | $+30$ |
| 0.5 | $-720$ | $+30$ |
| 1.0 | $-715$ | $+35$ |
| 2.0 | $-705$ | $+45$ |
| 4.0 | $-1325$ | $-575$ |

Two regimes, both instructive.

- **Small offsets act through $1/R$.** A one-degree offset moves the date by a few
  decades here, because at this epoch a degree corresponds to a few decades — the
  same arithmetic as Section 3, with the offset playing the role of the
  measurement error.
- **Large offsets change the branch.** At $4^\circ$ the estimator jumps to the
  *other* crossing of the curve — the ambiguity of Section 2.2, triggered by a
  systematic error. A bias that is larger than the local separation between
  branches does not merely shift a date; it relocates it.

This is why secular variation curves are **regional**, why they are published with
their own uncertainty envelopes, and why a date from a site far outside the region
of a curve must say so. The magnetic field's non-dipole part is not small: it is
several degrees.

## 5. What an archaeomagnetic date is a statement about

The defensible form of a date is:

> In this material, the last heating sufficient to reset the thermoremanent
> magnetisation occurred at a time when the field direction, as recorded by this
> material and compared with this reference curve, matched the measured direction,
> within the stated tolerance.

Every clause is doing work, and each corresponds to a failure mode:

1. **"the last heating sufficient to reset"** — if the material was reheated (a
   hearth reused, a kiln relined), the date refers to the last heating, not the
   first use.
2. **"as recorded by this material"** — the material must have acquired and
   retained a stable thermoremanent moment. Partial and viscous remanences,
   chemical alteration, and a weak or unstable magnetic mineralogy all break this,
   and they are diagnosed in the laboratory by stepwise demagnetisation and
   component analysis.
3. **"compared with this reference curve"** — the curve's regional validity, its
   epoch coverage and its own uncertainty envelope all enter the result
   (Section 4).
4. **"within the stated tolerance"** — the tolerance is what generates the
   intervals, and it must be stated with the age, because a larger tolerance can
   turn one interval into three (Section 2.2).
5. **"a time when the field direction ... matched"** — the answer is a set of
   intervals, possibly more than one, and it is not a single date.

A date is a statement about the material's last heating, not about the
construction of a building, unless the heating and the construction are
independently linked — for instance, a kiln in a demolition layer, or a hearth
sealed by a floor that is itself dated.

## 6. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **The material acquired a thermoremanent magnetisation (TRM) that is stable on
   geological timescales.** Requires an adequate concentration of single-domain or
   pseudo-single-domain magnetite (or haematite), and laboratory verification.
   Fails for materials with a large viscous overprint.
2. **The material has not moved since cooling, or its movement is known.** Tilting,
   rotation and slumping are common; a tilted block rotates the recorded direction
   by exactly its tilt, which is indistinguishable from a field anomaly.
3. **The field direction was uniform over the volume sampled.** A structure with
   differential cooling through a varying field (a large kiln over centuries)
   averages the field over time, which broadens the effective age — a
   *convolution* of the material's cooling history with the field's history.
4. **The reference curve is valid for the site and the epoch.** Section 4
   quantifies the cost of being wrong.
5. **The curve's uncertainty is carried through.** Comparing a direction with a
   curve that itself has an envelope requires propagating both; using only the
   measurement error understates the age uncertainty by the ratio of the two.
6. **The direction is a time function, not a monotone one.** Section 2.2 shows why
   the map from direction to age is generally many-to-one.
7. **Secular variation is not the same as palaeosecular variation.** At epochs
   older than a few millennia the curve is built from sediments and lavas with
   their own dating and smoothing, so the precision degrades further.

## 7. Fully worked numeric example

A material from the SYNTHETIC site at $45^\circ$ latitude is measured to have
$D_m=9.60^\circ$, $I_m=63.32^\circ$, with an angular uncertainty of $1.0^\circ$
($1\sigma$) in each component.

1. **GAD consistency.** The expected GAD inclination at $45^\circ$ is
   $\arctan(2\tan45^\circ)=\arctan 2=63.435^\circ$. The measured inclination,
   $63.32^\circ$, is within $0.12^\circ$ of it — consistent with the site's
   latitude and no evidence of a tilt.
2. **Angular tolerance.** A $1\sigma$ uncertainty of $1^\circ$ in each component
   gives an angular uncertainty of about $1.0$–$1.4^\circ$ depending on the
   components' correlation; the demonstration uses the great-circle distance to a
   threshold of $2.0^\circ$ (about $1.5\sigma$) and reports the admitted
   intervals.
3. **The date.** Two intervals: $-795$ to $-655$ and $-130$ to $-5\ \mathrm{yr\,BP}$.
   The true age of the synthetic measurement, $-750\ \mathrm{yr\,BP}$, is inside
   the first.
4. **Precision of the first interval.** Its width, $140\ \mathrm{yr}$, corresponds
   to $s/R$ with $R\approx1^\circ\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$... in the units of Section 3:
   a $2^\circ$ tolerance and a local rate of about $2$–$3^\circ$ per century gives
   $60$–$100\ \mathrm{yr}$, and the interval is wider because the curve is turning
   there.
5. **What to report.** "The last heating occurred at $750\pm70\ \mathrm{yr\,BP}$ if
   the material predates the medieval period, or at $70\pm60\ \mathrm{yr\,BP}$ if
   it does not; the two are distinguished by stratigraphy, not by the magnetisation."

## 8. Python demonstration

`code/archaeomagnetic_dating_demo.py` computes everything above. It:

1. tabulates the GAD inclination and field magnitude against latitude;
2. generates the SYNTHETIC secular-variation curve and prints it;
3. dates a SYNTHETIC measured direction at four tolerances, listing every admitted
   interval separately;
4. computes the rate of change of direction and hence the age precision at four
   epochs;
5. re-dates the same measurement against curves with systematic offsets of up to
   $4^\circ$, showing the shift-then-jump behaviour;
6. lists the four failure modes of a date and says which two the script itself can
   quantify.

It is print-only, uses `np.random.default_rng(20261014)`, needs no network, and
finishes in under a second.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Reporting one age from a multi-valued map.** The direction-to-age map is
   generally many-to-one; report every interval.
2. **Quoting an angular error as an age error.** They are related by the local rate
   of change, which varies by a factor of five in the demonstration's own curve.
3. **Ignoring the curve's uncertainty.** It often exceeds the measurement error.
4. **Using a curve outside its region.** The non-dipole field is several degrees;
   Section 4 measures what a $1$–$4^\circ$ offset costs.
5. **Treating a date as a construction date.** It dates the last heating of the
   material.
6. **Forgetting tilt and rotation.** Both are indistinguishable from field
   anomalies unless measured independently.
7. **Reporting a precision without the tolerance.** The tolerance *is* the
   interval width; different tolerances give different answers.
