# Environmental Magnetism and Pollution Mapping

Environmental magnetism uses the magnetic properties of soils, sediments and dust
as a *proxy*: for the concentration of a pollutant, for the provenance of a
sediment, for the degree of pedogenesis, or for the boundary between a
clean and a contaminated unit. The attraction is economy — a susceptibility meter
reads a sample in seconds, and a field survey covers a site in a day — and the
danger is over-interpretation, because a susceptibility value is a property of
the magnetic minerals, not of the pollutant.

This lesson builds the quantitative skeleton of the method. First, the magnetic
anomaly of a buried magnetised body: what a magnetometer actually measures, and
the cube-of-depth scaling that makes magnetics a superb detector and a poor
quantifier. Second, the **depth-to-size ambiguity**, which is not a limitation of
the instruments or the processing but a property of the physics: one anomaly,
infinitely many bodies. Third, the detection limit with depth and its cube-root
dependence on magnetisation. Fourth, and most important for this application, the
proxy relationship itself: what a correlation coefficient between susceptibility
and a chemical concentration does and does not establish.

All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**. The bodies, susceptibilities,
concentrations and noise are generated by the demonstration from a fixed seed;
there is no real site, sample or measured pollutant anywhere in this lesson.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Derive** the vertical-field anomaly of a dipole and show that a uniformly
   magnetised sphere produces $B_z=\frac{2}{3}\chi H\left(a/d\right)^3\mu_0$
   directly above it.
2. **Explain** the three scalings of that expression — radius, depth,
   susceptibility — and **state** which of them a survey can control.
3. **Demonstrate** the depth-to-size ambiguity by computing the family of bodies
   that reproduce one measured anomaly.
4. **Compute** the maximum detection depth for a stated body and noise floor, and
   **explain** why it depends on the cube root of both the magnetisation and the
   noise.
5. **Interpret** the sign pattern of a magnetic anomaly map over induced-dipole
   bodies, and **state** when the induced assumption fails.
6. **Distinguish** a proxy correlation from a calibration, and **state** what a
   susceptibility–pollutant relationship requires to be quantitative.
7. **List** the normalisation and fractionation effects that break a simple
   susceptibility–concentration relationship.

## 1. What is measured

Four quantities carry almost all of applied environmental magnetism, and they
measure different things:

| Quantity | Symbol | What it responds to | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass-specific susceptibility | $\chi$ (m³ kg⁻¹) | volume fraction of ferrimagnetic minerals | rapid mapping, pollution proxy |
| Saturation isothermal remanence | SIRM (A m² kg⁻¹) | concentration of remanence-carrying minerals | mapping, with less diamagnetic background |
| $\mathrm{SIRM}/\chi$ | — | magnetic grain size and mineralogy | discriminating sources |
| Anhysteretic remanence ratio | ARM/SIRM | fine single-domain content | pedogenesis, bacterial magnetite |

Susceptibility is the cheapest and therefore the most used, and it is also the most
easily corrupted: it responds to *all* magnetic minerals, including the
diamagnetic quartz and the paramagnetic clay fractions, so a susceptibility
difference between two soils can be a difference in their iron-oxide content or in
their clay content. The standard practice is to report a pair of quantities, one
concentration-like and one ratio-like, and to normalise with respect to a
non-magnetic fraction (mass, Al, or Fe content) when comparing samples from
different lithologies.

**What a magnetometer survey measures** is different again: the *field* produced by
the magnetisation of the subsurface. The connection to the laboratory quantities is
through the induced magnetisation $M=\chi H$ and any remanence, and the forward
model is the subject of the next section.

## 2. The anomaly of a buried body

### 2.1 The dipole field

A magnetised body whose largest dimension is small compared with its depth
behaves as a dipole. With the observation point at horizontal offset $\rho$ and the
body at depth $d$ directly below the origin, the vertical component of the field of
a vertical dipole of moment $m$ is

$$B_z=\mu_0H_z=\mu_0\,\frac{m\left(2d^2-\rho^2\right)}
{4\pi\left(\rho^2+d^2\right)^{5/2}}.$$

Directly above the body ($\rho=0$) this reduces to the classic
$B_z=\mu_0 m/(2\pi d^3)$. The off-axis sign reversal — negative lobes on either
side of the positive centre, in the induced case — is the signature that lets an
interpreter recognise a dipole, and its amplitude ratio is set by the geometry,
not by the magnetisation.

### 2.2 A sphere, in closed form

For a sphere of radius $a$ with susceptibility $\chi$ in an ambient field $H$, the
induced moment is $m=\chi H\times\frac{4}{3}\pi a^3$, so

$$B_z(\rho=0)=\mu_0\,\frac{\chi H\left(4/3\right)\pi a^3\left(2d^2\right)}
{4\pi d^5}
=\boxed{\;\frac{2}{3}\,\chi\,H\left(\frac{a}{d}\right)^{3}\mu_0.\;}$$

The demonstration evaluates this for SYNTHETIC bodies with
$H=48\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$ (about $60\ \mathrm{\mu T}$):

| $\chi$ (SI) | radius $a$ (m) | depth $d$ (m) | $a/d$ | anomaly (nT) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $10^{-3}$ | 2.0 | 5.0 | 0.40 | 2.574 |
| $10^{-3}$ | 2.0 | 10.0 | 0.20 | 0.322 |
| $10^{-2}$ | 2.0 | 5.0 | 0.40 | 25.736 |
| $10^{-2}$ | 5.0 | 5.0 | 1.00 | 402.124 |
| $10^{-2}$ | 5.0 | 20.0 | 0.25 | 6.283 |
| $10^{-1}$ | 5.0 | 20.0 | 0.25 | 62.832 |

Three independent scalings, which is the whole content of the expression:
doubling the radius multiplies the anomaly by $8$; doubling the depth divides it
by $8$; a tenfold larger susceptibility multiplies it by $10$. The first two are
geometry, set by nature; the third is a property of the ground that the survey can
only infer. **A magnetic anomaly is therefore an excellent detector and a poor
quantifier**, and the reason is the exponent three.

## 3. The depth-to-size ambiguity

Set the measured anomaly to $B=20\ \mathrm{nT}$ with $\chi=0.01$ and solve for the
radius required at each depth:

| depth (m) | required radius (m) | required volume (m³) |
|---|---|---|
| 2.0 | 0.74 | 1.7 |
| 5.0 | 1.84 | 26.0 |
| 10.0 | 3.68 | 208.3 |
| 20.0 | 7.36 | 1666.7 |
| 50.0 | 18.39 | 26041.7 |

The numbers are all that can be said: **the measurement constrains $a^3/d^3$, i.e.
the ratio $a/d$, and never $a$ and $d$ separately.** A $1.84\ \mathrm{m}$ sphere at
$5\ \mathrm{m}$ and an $18.39\ \mathrm{m}$ sphere at $50\ \mathrm{m}$ produce the
same anomaly to three figures.

This is the same non-uniqueness that
[08-03-04](../03-magnetic-anomalies-and-modelling/04-magnetic-forward-modelling.md)
derives as the potential-field null space, made concrete. Depth estimation from
magnetic data therefore always imports an assumption: a body shape (which fixes the
moment-to-size relation), an independently measured size (a borehole, a pit, a
seismic horizon), or a method that trades the ambiguity for a different one —
Euler deconvolution, for instance, returns a depth and a *structural index*, and
the index is another assumption.

## 4. Detection limits and their cube roots

A body is detectable while its anomaly exceeds the noise. Inverting the sphere
formula, the maximum depth at which a body of radius $a$ and susceptibility $\chi$
is seen against a noise floor $B_N$ is

$$d_{\max}=a\left(\frac{2}{3}\frac{\chi H\mu_0}{B_N}\right)^{1/3}.$$

With $H=48\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$ and $B_N=1\ \mathrm{nT}$:

| $\chi$ (SI) | $a=2\ \mathrm{m}$ | $a=5\ \mathrm{m}$ | $a=20\ \mathrm{m}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $10^{-3}$ | 6.85 | 17.13 | 68.52 |
| $10^{-2}$ | 14.76 | 36.91 | 147.62 |
| $10^{-1}$ | 31.80 | 79.51 | 318.04 |

Two design consequences follow, and they are the numerical form of a rule every
practitioner knows qualitatively:

- **A hundredfold increase in susceptibility buys a $4.6$-fold increase in
  detectable depth** ($100^{1/3}=4.64$). So the difference between a "weakly
  magnetic" and a "strongly magnetic" target is a factor of five in depth, not a
  factor of a hundred.
- **The same holds for the instrument.** Improving the noise floor by a hundredfold
  (from $1\ \mathrm{nT}$ to $0.01\ \mathrm{nT}$) has exactly the same effect on
  detection depth as a hundredfold increase in magnetisation. This is why
  magnetometry is inherently shallow for small sources, and why the sensitivity
  race between instruments yields metres, not kilometres.

## 5. A map, and what a map is made of

The demonstration builds a SYNTHETIC $60\times60$ grid at $2\ \mathrm{m}$ spacing
with three induced-dipole bodies plus $1\ \mathrm{nT}$ of noise:

| body | position (m) | $\chi$ | $a$ (m) | $d$ (m) | peak anomaly (nT) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | (15, 30) | 0.0100 | 3.0 | 8.0 | 21.21 |
| 2 | (80, 40) | 0.0050 | 4.0 | 15.0 | 3.81 |
| 3 | (50, 90) | 0.0200 | 2.0 | 5.0 | 51.47 |

Map statistics: min $-3.59\ \mathrm{nT}$, max $+49.62\ \mathrm{nT}$, rms
$2.217\ \mathrm{nT}$.

Reading the map is an exercise in the cube-of-depth dependence: body 2, at twice
the depth and with twice the radius, is a broad smudge of a few nanoteslas, while
body 3 at $5\ \mathrm{m}$ depth is a sharp $50\ \mathrm{nT}$ peak with clear
negative flanks. The interpreter's task — find bodies, estimate their positions,
and *not* claim their magnetisations — is set by the physics of Section 3, and no
amount of processing or inversion removes it.

**When the induced assumption fails.** All of the above assumes
$m=\chi H$: magnetisation parallel to the ambient field. Remanent magnetisation is
not parallel to the present field, and a body with a Koenigsberger ratio
$Q=M_r/(\chi H)$ of order one or more produces an anomaly whose shape and even
sign pattern do not match the induced model
([08-03-02](../03-magnetic-anomalies-and-modelling/02-koenigsberger-and-remanence.md)).
In environmental applications this matters most for soils that have been fired
(archaeological features, brick, slag, furnace waste), where the remanence can
dominate — which is exactly why archaeomagnetic and environmental surveys must
distinguish the two.

## 6. From a property to a proxy: what a correlation establishes

The demonstration generates two SYNTHETIC data sets of $40$ samples, each with a
"true concentration" index and a susceptibility, modelling a site where the
magnetic minerals and the pollutant share a source and a site where they do not:

| data set | Pearson $r$ | $r^2$ | slope (per unit concentration) | intercept ($10^{-3}$ SI) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| shared source | 0.941 | 0.886 | $1.755\times10^{-5}$ | $+0.0174$ |
| independent | 0.112 | 0.012 | $1.723\times10^{-6}$ | $-0.3056$ |

Three statements, in increasing order of importance.

1. **A high correlation is evidence of co-genesis**, not of concentration. The
   shared-source site's $r=0.941$ supports the use of susceptibility as a proxy
   *there*; the independent site's $r=0.112$ shows the method failing exactly as it
   would at a site where the magnetic and the chemical loads come from different
   sources.
2. **The calibration is site-specific.** The slope differs between the two data
   sets by a factor of ten, and the intercept moves from $+0.017\times10^{-3}$ to
   $-0.31\times10^{-3}$ SI; using a published slope from another site is the commonest
   error in applied environmental magnetism. A quantitative map requires chemical
   analyses from *the same site*, used to fit the slope, with the residual scatter
   reported.
3. **The proxy is one-way.** A susceptibility anomaly tells you where the magnetic
   minerals are; if the pollutant is bound to those minerals it is a pollutant
   marker, and if it is not, it is a marker of something else entirely (a
   landfill cap, a road surface, a geological contact).

**Fractionation and normalisation** break the relationship in specific ways that
should be stated as caveats: grain-size changes alter $\chi$ per unit iron;
organic matter dilution lowers $\chi$ without lowering the pollutant load;
dissolution of magnetite in reducing sediments removes the proxy while leaving the
contaminant; and different pollutants bind to different mineral fractions
(sulphides, oxides, clay). The diagnostic is to measure a *pair* of magnetic
parameters ($\chi$ with SIRM, and SIRM/$\chi$ as a grain-size indicator) so that a
change in concentration and a change in mineralogy can be told apart.

## 7. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Induced magnetisation** ($Q\ll1$) for the anomaly formulas; fails for fired
   and slag-bearing materials (Section 5).
2. **Dipole (point-source) bodies**: body dimensions small compared with depth.
   For a sphere the dipole approximation is exact at the centre and a good
   approximation beyond about one radius.
3. **A single, uniform susceptibility** per body; a heterogeneous body behaves as
   its magnetisation-weighted average, which is a further averaging of unknowns.
4. **A flat, non-magnetic surface layer**: a magnetic topsoil, a road, vehicles
   and buried services all produce large, shallow, high-frequency anomalies that
   are easy to detect and hard to attribute.
5. **A noise floor that is understood**: the demonstration's $1\ \mathrm{nT}$ is a
   synthetic instrument noise; in the field the dominant "noise" is usually
   geological and cultural, not instrumental.
6. **The anomaly is measured at the surface** with the sensor's height added to the
   depth: a sensor at $1.5\ \mathrm{m}$ above the ground reduces the effective
   $d^{-3}$ for shallow bodies significantly, which is why surveys at ground level
   and surveys on a vehicle differ in their apparent content.
7. **The proxy relationship is empirical and site-specific** (Section 6). It is
   never a physical law, and it must be re-established wherever the geology,
   source or land use changes.

## 8. Fully worked numeric example

A survey measures a $20\ \mathrm{nT}$ peak anomaly. The site's soil susceptibility
is $0.01$ (SI) and the ambient field is $48\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$.

1. **What is the product of unknowns?** From
   $B=\frac{2}{3}\chi H(a/d)^3\mu_0$:
   $$(a/d)^3=\frac{B}{\frac{2}{3}\chi H\mu_0}
   =\frac{20\times10^{-9}}{0.667\times0.01\times48\times1.2566\times10^{-6}}
   =\frac{2.0\times10^{-8}}{4.021\times10^{-7}}=0.04974,$$
   so $a/d=0.3678$, i.e. $d=a/0.3678=2.72\,a$.
2. **Convert to a depth** for a believed size. If a trial pit shows the body to be
   a $3\ \mathrm{m}$-radius mass: $d=2.72\times3=8.16\ \mathrm{m}$. If instead the
   body were $1\ \mathrm{m}$: $d=2.72\ \mathrm{m}$. Both explain the data.
3. **Propagate the assumption.** $d\propto a$, so a $20\%$ error in the assumed
   radius gives a $20\%$ error in the depth — much better behaved than the inverse
   problem's usual behaviour, but the point is that **the depth is proportional to
   an assumed size, not measured.**
4. **Detection depth for this body** with a $1\ \mathrm{nT}$ noise floor:
   $d_{\max}=a\left(\frac{2}{3}\chi H\mu_0/B_N\right)^{1/3}
   =3\left(\frac{4.021\times10^{-7}}{10^{-9}}\right)^{1/3}
   =3\times(402.1)^{1/3}=3\times7.38=22.1\ \mathrm{m}$.
   A body of the same size but ten times the susceptibility is detectable to
   $3\times(4021)^{1/3}=3\times15.9=47.7\ \mathrm{m}$: a $2.2$-fold gain for a
   tenfold increase, which is the cube root at work.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Reading a magnetisation from an anomaly.** The anomaly constrains
   $\chi(a/d)^3$.
2. **Quoting a depth without the size assumption.** The two are one number.
3. **Confusing detectability with resolution.** A body may be detectable long after
   its size and depth are separable.
4. **Ignoring remanence.** Fired and slag-bearing materials violate the induced
   assumption.
5. **Using a published proxy slope.** It is a site-specific calibration, not a
   constant.
6. **Reporting $\chi$ alone.** A concentration-like parameter needs a ratio partner
   to separate grain size from concentration.
7. **Adding the sensor height to nothing.** The sensor's own height adds to the
   body depth, and the cube makes that matter.
