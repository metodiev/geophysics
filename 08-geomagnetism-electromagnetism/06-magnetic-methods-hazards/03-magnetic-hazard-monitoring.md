# Magnetic Monitoring of Volcanoes and Faults

If a buried body's magnetisation changes, the field above it changes. Monitoring
volcanoes and faults magnetically means measuring that change continuously, at
nanotesla precision, and deciding whether a change is the volcano or the sky, the
instrument, or the slow grind of the core field itself. This lesson is about that
decision, and it is written in the order in which the difficulties arise.

First the source term: how large a moment change produces a given field change at
a given depth, and why the numbers are favourable. Then the signal budget: what is
actually in a monitoring record — the secular variation is *larger* than any
eruption signal, so the problem is separation, not sensitivity. Then the standard
separation device, a **reference station**, and what it does and does not cancel.
Then the statistics of detection: an estimator's real scatter (measured, not
assumed), the detection limit it implies, and the false-alarm rate that must be
quoted with it. Finally the irreducible ambiguity: a slow source change and a
small secular-variation trend error are the same thing in the data.

All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**. The secular-variation trend,
the diurnal variation, the instrument drift, the transient and the noise are
generated by the demonstration from a fixed seed. No volcano, fault, station,
eruption or field measurement is described.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Derive** the field change produced above a buried dipole by a moment change,
   $\Delta B=\mu_0\Delta m/(2\pi d^3)$, and **invert** it to state what a $1\
   \mathrm{nT}$ change implies at a given depth.
2. **Decompose** a monitoring record into its components and **state** which is
   largest, which is coherent between stations and which is not.
3. **Explain** what a reference station cancels, and **quantify** the residual it
   cannot cancel.
4. **Measure** an estimator's standard error by Monte Carlo, **set** a detection
   threshold from it, and **compute** the detection probability and the
   false-alarm rate rather than asserting either.
5. **Distinguish** an ambiguity in the data (a slow source change versus a trend
   error) from a limitation of the estimator (a ramp truncated by the analysis
   window).
6. **State** the design rule relating the required trend accuracy to the size of
   the change and the length of the observation window.
7. **List** the physical mechanisms by which a volcano or fault changes the field,
   and **name** the observation that separates them.

## 1. The source term

A body whose magnetic moment changes by $\Delta m$ produces, directly above it at
depth $d$, a field change

$$\Delta B=\mu_0\frac{\Delta m}{4\pi d^3}\times 2
=\boxed{\;\frac{\mu_0\,\Delta m}{2\pi d^3}\;}$$

(the factor two is the same $2d^2$ axial factor that produced
$B_z=\mu_0m/(2\pi d^3)$ in
[08-06-02](02-environmental-magnetics.md)). Inverting,

$$\Delta m=\frac{2\pi d^3\Delta B}{\mu_0}.$$

The demonstration tabulates what a $1\ \mathrm{nT}$ change requires, with
$\mu_0=4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{H\,m^{-1}}$:

| depth $d$ (km) | $\Delta m$ for $1\ \mathrm{nT}$ ($\mathrm{A\,m^2}$) | equivalent volume at $M=1\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$ (m³) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | $6.250\times10^{5}$ | $6.250\times10^{5}$ |
| 1.0 | $5.000\times10^{6}$ | $5.000\times10^{6}$ |
| 2.0 | $4.000\times10^{7}$ | $4.000\times10^{7}$ |
| 5.0 | $6.250\times10^{8}$ | $6.250\times10^{8}$ |

The $d^3$ makes the method more sensitive to shallow sources and much less
sensitive to deep ones: a $1\ \mathrm{nT}$ change at $1\ \mathrm{km}$ is a moment
change of $5.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{A\,m^2}$, equivalent to $5\times10^6\ \mathrm{m^3}$
of rock magnetised to $1\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$ (a cube $171\ \mathrm{m}$ on a side),
or $10^4\ \mathrm{m^3}$ at $500\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$. Those are large volumes but
small *changes*: thermal demagnetisation of a few per cent of a magnetite-bearing
volume, or a small change in the stress state of a piezomagnetic body, can supply
them. This is why the method works at all, and why a $1\ \mathrm{nT}$ detection
threshold is worth engineering.

## 2. What is actually in a monitoring record

The demonstration builds a SYNTHETIC two-year record (2922 samples, four per day):

| component | sd (nT) | range (nT) | coherent between stations? |
|---|---|---|---|
| secular variation trend | 11.170 | $-9.18$ to $+23.79$ | yes, over hundreds of km |
| diurnal variation | 5.801 | $-9.29$ to $+6.71$ | yes, to a few per cent over tens of km |
| instrument drift | 0.289 | 0 to $+1.00$ | no |
| transient (source, $2\ \mathrm{nT}$ step) | 0.983 | 0 to $+2.00$ | no |
| noise ($0.5\ \mathrm{nT}$ rms) | 0.498 | $-1.72$ to $+1.58$ | no |

The diurnal peak-to-peak amplitude is $16.0\ \mathrm{nT}$; the transient is
$2.0\ \mathrm{nT}$, an eighth of it, and four times the noise. **The secular
variation's standard deviation, $11.2\ \mathrm{nT}$, is larger than all the rest
combined.** A monitoring problem is therefore a problem of *separating* signals
with different spatial coherence, not of detecting a small signal in a quiet
background. Two consequences:

- **Differencing is the primary tool**, because the two largest terms are the two
  most spatially coherent (Section 3).
- **The instrument is rarely the limit.** With modern magnetometers good to a few
  tenths of a nanotesla, the limiting term is the *residual* after differencing,
  which is set by the field's spatial structure and by the reference's distance.

## 3. Separation by a reference station

Take a second station a few tens of kilometres away, which sees the same core field
and nearly the same external (diurnal) field but not the local source. The
difference of the two records contains the source plus the difference of the
non-shared terms. The demonstration emulates this with a synthetic reference that
shares the deterministic part exactly and has an independent noise realisation:

- **Recovered transient amplitude: $+2.080\ \mathrm{nT}$ (truth $2.00\ \mathrm{nT}$)**,
  i.e. within $4\%$ — and the $4\%$ is the realisation's noise, not a bias.
- **Scatter of the difference before the transient: sd $=0.679\ \mathrm{nT}$**,
  which is $\sqrt2\times0.5\ \mathrm{nT}$, the noise of two independent stations
  added in quadrature. Differencing *doubles* the noise power and *removes* the
  coherent signal: a trade that is almost always worth making.

What differencing cannot remove, and what therefore sets the real floor:

1. **Imperfect coherence of the external field.** The diurnal variation is
   spatially coherent to a few per cent of its amplitude over tens to hundreds of
   kilometres, so a $16\ \mathrm{nT}$ peak-to-peak variation leaves a residual of
   order $0.1$–$1\ \mathrm{nT}$ even after differencing. This is the usual limit for
   volcano monitoring at a few kilometres' separation, and it improves with
   *shorter* baselines until the reference begins to see the source itself.
2. **Induced and geological differences** between the two sites' subsurface
   responses to the external field, which are systematic and seasonal.
3. **The reference's own drift**, which enters the difference directly.

## 4. Detection probability and false-alarm rate, measured

The estimator used here is deliberately simple and standard: the median of the
difference well after the transient minus the median well before it. Its scatter
must be measured, not assumed, because the estimator averages thousands of samples
and its standard error is far smaller than the single-sample noise.

Monte Carlo over 200 SYNTHETIC noise realisations with **no transient present**:

- mean recovered amplitude $-0.0064\ \mathrm{nT}$;
- standard deviation $0.0393\ \mathrm{nT}$;
- largest absolute value $0.1329\ \mathrm{nT}$.

So a $3\sigma$ threshold is $0.1179\ \mathrm{nT}$ — an order of magnitude below the
single-sample noise of $0.5\ \mathrm{nT}$, and *twelve times* smaller than the
pre-transient scatter of the difference ($0.679\ \mathrm{nT}$). This is the
distinction that matters: single-sample scatter is a property of the instrument,
while the estimator's scatter is a property of the *experiment*.

| transient (nT) | detection probability | false alarms in 200 |
|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 0.005 | 1 |
| 0.05 | 0.020 | 0 |
| 0.10 | 0.320 | 0 |
| 0.25 | 0.995 | 0 |
| 0.50 | 1.000 | 0 |
| 2.00 | 1.000 | 0 |

Reading of the table, in the order a reviewer would ask for it:

- **The detection limit is between $0.05$ and $0.25\ \mathrm{nT}$**, a tenth to a
  half of the single-sample noise, because the estimator averages thousands of
  samples.
- **The false-alarm rate is not zero**: in 200 null realisations one crossed the
  $3\sigma$ threshold, i.e. $0.5\%$ — consistent with a Gaussian tail at
  $3\sigma$ ($0.3\%$) given 200 trials. Any operational system must publish this
  number, because a monitoring network that fires on noise is worse than one that
  stays quiet, and because the same statistic is what a public warning will be
  based on.
- **The threshold is a choice, and it should be stated.** A $2\sigma$ threshold
  would detect $0.05\ \mathrm{nT}$ more often and false-alarm far more often; an
  eruption-forecast use case justifies the cost of both numbers being computed and
  reported.

## 5. The irreducible ambiguity: a slow change versus the trend

A source that changes over months can be partly absorbed into the
secular-variation trend used for reference. SYNTHETIC test: a $2\ \mathrm{nT}$ ramp
over a stated duration, recovered with a correct reference and with a reference
whose trend is wrong by $0.2\ \mathrm{nT}$ per year:

| ramp duration (days) | recovered (nT) | with $0.2\ \mathrm{nT\,yr^{-1}}$ trend error (nT) | bias (nT) | predicted bias (nT) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 2.024 | 2.267 | $+0.243$ | 0.255 |
| 30 | 2.024 | 2.267 | $+0.243$ | 0.255 |
| 90 | 1.955 | 2.195 | $+0.239$ | 0.255 |
| 180 | 1.665 | 1.888 | $+0.223$ | 0.255 |
| 365 | 1.105 | 1.340 | $+0.235$ | 0.255 |

Two separate effects appear, and confusing them is the usual error.

- **The trend-error bias** equals (trend error) $\times$ (separation of the
  estimator's two window medians), about $465$ days here, giving
  $0.2\times465/365.25=0.255\ \mathrm{nT}$. The measured bias, $+0.235$ to
  $+0.243\ \mathrm{nT}$, matches it to a few hundredths of a nanotesla and **does
  not depend on the ramp duration**. This is an ambiguity of the *data*: a slow
  source change and a trend error of the same size are indistinguishable, and no
  estimator can separate them.
- **The falling recovered amplitude** ($2.024$ at $2$ days to $1.105$ at
  $365$ days) is a property of the *estimator*: its post-transient window opens
  before a slow ramp has finished. A better estimator (a ramp fit, or a
  longer baseline) fixes this one.

The design rule follows directly and is worth memorising in this form. To detect a
change $\Delta B$ with a reference whose trend is known only to $e$ per year over
an observation window of $T$ days, the ambiguity is $eT/365$; requiring it below
$\Delta B/3$ gives

$$e<\frac{365}{3}\cdot\frac{\Delta B}{T}
=122\,\frac{\Delta B}{T}
\quad[\mathrm{nT\,yr^{-1}},\ \Delta B\ \mathrm{in\ nT},\ T\ \mathrm{in\ days}].$$

For a $1\ \mathrm{nT}$ change observed over a quarter ($T\approx90$ days) that is
$e<1.4\ \mathrm{nT\,yr^{-1}}$; for a $0.2\ \mathrm{nT}$ change, $e<0.27$, which no
single station can guarantee. **The reference station, not the magnetometer, is the
limiting instrument**, and lengthening the baseline is the only structural remedy —
at the cost of the reference seeing the source.

## 6. What physically changes the field, and how to tell the mechanisms apart

| Mechanism | Field signature | Typical timescale | Independent discriminator |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thermal demagnetisation / magnetisation of a shallow body | $1$–$10\ \mathrm{nT}$ near the source, $\propto d^{-3}$ | days–months | temperature, seismicity, gas flux |
| Piezomagnetic effect (stress change) | $\sim0.1$–$1\ \mathrm{nT}$ | minutes–days, correlates with seismicity | strain, local earthquakes |
| Fluid flow / hydrothermal alteration | slow, monotone | months–years | resistivity, self-potential, gas |
| Magma intrusion (new magnetised material) | tens of nT, near-field, sharp | days | seismicity, deformation, gas |
| Core field (secular variation) | $10$–$100\ \mathrm{nT\,yr^{-1}}$, regional | years | reference station, global models |
| External (diurnal, storm) | $10$–$100\ \mathrm{nT}$, transient | hours–days | reference station, indices |
| Instrument drift / orientation change | arbitrary, monotone | weeks–months | calibration, attitude sensors |

The table is the honest core of the method: **magnetometry observes one scalar (or
vector) field, and every mechanism in it produces a similar-looking time series.**
Attribution requires an independent observable, which is why magnetic monitoring is
almost always deployed alongside seismology, geodesy, gas flux and temperature, and
why a magnetic anomaly alone has never been accepted as an eruption forecast.

Two further practical points that behave like assumptions:

- **Total field versus vector.** A scalar magnetometer has no directional
  information and cannot distinguish a change in magnetisation from a rotation of
  the local field direction; the projection of an anomaly onto the total-field
  direction depends on its orientation, so a total-field anomaly is always a
  component measurement in disguise.
- **Sensor placement.** A monitoring station sits on ground whose own
  susceptibility changes with moisture, freezing and snow; the annual cycle of these
  effects is a large coherent signal that a reference station at a different
  elevation does *not* cancel.

## 7. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **A dipole source at depth $d$**, with $d$ large compared with the source's
   dimensions. For a distributed source the anomaly is the sum of the volume's
   contributions, and the $d^{-3}$ law becomes an average over the source depth.
2. **The station is directly above the source.** Off-axis, the anomaly is reduced
   by the geometric factor $(2d^2-\rho^2)/(\rho^2+d^2)^{5/2}$, and at
   $\rho=d/\sqrt2$ the vertical component changes sign — a station at the wrong
   distance can record nothing, or the wrong sign.
3. **The reference is outside the source's influence and inside the external
   field's coherence.** These two requirements pull in opposite directions and set
   the station geometry.
4. **The secular variation is known or common-mode.** Section 5 quantifies the cost
   of a trend error.
5. **Noise is stationary over the analysis window.** Storms, lightning and cultural
   noise are not, and they inflate the apparent scatter; the estimator's Monte
   Carlo scatter must be recomputed for a window containing such events.
6. **Detected changes are real.** Section 4's false-alarm rate is the price of any
   threshold, and it must be quoted alongside any claimed detection.
7. **The physical mechanism is identified independently.** Section 6: no
   magnetometer can distinguish thermal, piezomagnetic and intrusive sources from
   the field alone.

## 8. Fully worked numeric example

A station sits above a hydrothermal system, $1.5\ \mathrm{km}$ below a monitoring
station (so $d=1.5\ \mathrm{km}$), with a reference station $10\ \mathrm{km}$ away.
Over $90$ days the differenced record rises by a recovered $1.2\ \mathrm{nT}$.

1. **Moment change implied.** $\Delta m=2\pi d^3\Delta B/\mu_0$ with
   $d=1500\ \mathrm{m}$ and $\Delta B=1.2\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{T}$:

$$\Delta m=\frac{2\pi\left(1.5\times10^{3}\right)^3\left(1.2\times10^{-9}\right)}
{1.2566\times10^{-6}}
=\frac{6.2832\times3.375\times10^{9}\times1.2\times10^{-9}}
{1.2566\times10^{-6}}
=\frac{25.45}{1.2566\times10^{-6}}
=2.03\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{A\,m^2}.$$

2. **What volume is that?** At $M=1\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$ it is
   $2.0\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m^3}$, a cube $271\ \mathrm{m}$ on a side; at
   $M=10\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$, $2.0\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m^3}$, a cube
   $126\ \mathrm{m}$ on a side. Both are plausible for a hydrothermal system at
   $1.5\ \mathrm{km}$.

3. **Is the detection significant?** With the estimator's measured scatter of
   $0.0393\ \mathrm{nT}$ (Section 4), a $1.2\ \mathrm{nT}$ step is
   $30\sigma$ — significant beyond doubt, *provided* the noise statistics were
   computed for this window and provided the trend is not in error.

4. **What trend accuracy is required?** The ambiguity produced by a trend error
   $e$ over a window of $T$ days is $eT/365$; requiring it below $\Delta B/3$
   gives $e<122\,\Delta B/T=122\times1.2/90=1.6\ \mathrm{nT\,yr^{-1}}$. That is
   achievable with a good reference pair but demanding for a single station, and
   it is the number that belongs in the monitoring plan.

5. **Reportable statement.** "A $1.2\ \mathrm{nT}$ change over 90 days,
corresponding to a moment change of $2.0\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{A\,m^2}$ at
$1.5\ \mathrm{km}$ depth, detected at $30\sigma$ against the measured estimator
scatter, with a trend uncertainty contributing at most $0.4\ \mathrm{nT}$ over the
window; the mechanism is not determined by the magnetic data alone."

## 9. Python demonstration

`code/magnetic_monitoring_demo.py` computes everything above. It:

1. tabulates the moment change implied by a $1\ \mathrm{nT}$ field change at four
   depths;
2. builds a SYNTHETIC two-year record and reports the standard deviation and range
   of each component;
3. recovers a $2\ \mathrm{nT}$ step by differencing against a reference station;
4. measures the estimator's scatter by Monte Carlo over 200 null realisations, sets
   a $3\sigma$ threshold from it, and reports detection probability and false-alarm
   rate against transient amplitude;
5. quantifies the ramp-versus-trend ambiguity, separates the data ambiguity from the
   estimator's truncation, and states the design rule.

It is print-only, uses `np.random.default_rng(20261014)`, needs no network, and
finishes in under a second.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Quoting a detection without a false-alarm rate.** Both come from the same
   threshold.
2. **Using single-sample noise as the detection limit.** An estimator that averages
   thousands of samples is limited by its own scatter, which must be measured.
3. **Assuming the reference cancels everything.** It cancels the coherent part; the
   incoherent part is the floor.
4. **Ignoring the secular-variation trend error.** Section 5 shows it is the
   limiting term, and that it is indistinguishable from a slow source change.
5. **Placing the station at the wrong offset.** The anomaly's vertical component
   changes sign at $\rho=d/\sqrt2$; a badly placed station can see nothing.
6. **Attributing a mechanism from the magnetic record alone.** It cannot be done;
   the time series look alike.
7. **Forgetting the seasonal cycle of the ground itself.** Moisture, freezing and
   snow change the local susceptibility and produce an annual signal that a
   distant reference does not remove.
