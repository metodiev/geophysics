## ★1 — Impulse

**(a)** Starting from $\mathbf{F} = d\mathbf{p}/dt$, integrate over the interval:

$$\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\mathbf{F}\,dt = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\frac{d\mathbf{p}}{dt}\,dt
= \mathbf{p}(t_2)-\mathbf{p}(t_1).$$

The left side is the impulse by definition; the theorem is a statement about the
definition of an integral, and it holds for variable mass as written.

**(b)** $\Delta v = J/m = 500/200 = \mathbf{2.5\ m\,s^{-1}}$.

**(c)** $J = \int_0^{\tau}F_0(1-t/\tau)\,dt = F_0\tau/2 = 10^{4}\times0.05/2
= \mathbf{250\ N\,s}$, so $\Delta v = 250/200 = \mathbf{1.25\ m\,s^{-1}}$.
(For comparison, the half-sine pulse of the lesson with the same $F_0$ and $\tau$
gives $2F_0\tau/\pi = 318$ N s — the pulse *shape* changes the delivered impulse
by 27%.) This is why a source's peak force is a poor description of its strength
while the impulse is a good one.

## ★2 — Collision with $e = 0.5$

**(a)** $p = 2\times3 + 4\times0 = \mathbf{6.0\ kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$.

**(b)** $v_{\text{cm}} = 6/6 = 1.0$ m/s and $v_{\text{rel}} = 3$ m/s, so

$$v_1' = v_{\text{cm}} - \frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2}e\,v_{\text{rel}}
= 1 - \frac{4}{6}(0.5)(3) = \mathbf{0.0\ m\,s^{-1}},$$
$$v_2' = v_{\text{cm}} + \frac{m_1}{m_1+m_2}e\,v_{\text{rel}}
= 1 + \frac{2}{6}(0.5)(3) = \mathbf{1.5\ m\,s^{-1}}.$$

Check momentum: $2(0)+4(1.5) = 6.0$ ✓. Check restitution:
$-(v_1'-v_2')/(v_1-v_2) = -(-1.5)/3 = 0.5$ ✓.

**(c)** Before: $K_0 = \tfrac12(2)(9) = 9.0$ J. After:
$K_1 = \tfrac12(4)(2.25) = 4.5$ J. Loss $= \mathbf{4.5\ J}$ (50%).

**(d)** $\mu = 2\times4/6 = 1.3333$ kg, so
$\tfrac12\mu v_{\text{rel}}^{2}(1-e^{2}) = \tfrac12(1.3333)(9)(1-0.25) = 4.5$ J ✓.
The light block is **stopped dead** and the heavy one moves off at half the
initial relative speed — a consequence of the mass ratio, not a coincidence.

## ★3 — Centre of mass

**(a)** The centre of mass obeys $M\ddot{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}$.
The only forces are internal (the mutual attraction), and internal forces cancel
in pairs by the third law, so $\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}} = \mathbf{0}$ and the
centre of mass does not accelerate — it stays at rest if it started at rest.

**(b)** $X = (2\times0 + 3\times10)/5 = \mathbf{6.0\ m}$ from the 2 kg body.

**(c)** The centre of mass must stay at 6.0 m, so if the 2 kg body moves 1 m
towards the other, the 3 kg body must move away by $2\times1/3
= \mathbf{0.667\ m}$ in the opposite direction: $2(1) + 3(-0.667) = 0$ ✓. This
is the momentum accounting expressed as a geometry statement, and it is the same
reasoning as the "person walking on a raft" example.

## ★4 — Short explanations

**(a)** Momentum conservation follows from the *forces* (internal pairs cancel),
not from the energetics; a collision that converts kinetic energy to heat changes
the energy but not the force system.

**(b)** Kinetic energy is not a conserved quantity when non-conservative internal
forces do work — friction, fracture, plastic deformation. The lost mechanical
energy appears as heat and damage, so the *total* energy of the closed system is
conserved while the mechanical part is not.

**(c)** An explosion is a purely volumetric source: its force system is a
symmetric set of outward point forces whose vector sum is zero at every instant,
so the net momentum injected is zero. It radiates because the *radiated field* is
generated by the time-varying volume change and stress, not by a net force; the
far-field pulse has zero net area, which is the momentum statement.

## ★★5 — Weight drop

**(a)** $v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2\times9.82025\times1.5}
= \mathbf{5.4278\ m\,s^{-1}}$; $K = \tfrac12(500)(5.4278)^{2}
= \mathbf{7.3652\times10^{3}\ J}$.

**(b)** $J = mv(1+e) = 500\times5.4278\times1.3
= \mathbf{3.5281\times10^{3}\ N\,s}$.

**(c)** Rebound energy $= e^{2}K = 0.09\times7365.2 = 662.9$ J; retained
$= K(1-e^{2}) = 7365.2\times0.91 = \mathbf{6.702\times10^{3}\ J}$, i.e.
**91.0%**.

**(d)** The seismic fraction is **not** computable from this information: it is an
efficiency that must be measured (from the radiated elastic energy in a
calibrated recording, or by comparing with a reference source at the same site).
Typical seismic efficiencies for a hammer or weight-drop source are a small
percentage of the retained energy at best; quoting a number without a measurement
would be fabrication.

## ★★6 — Rocket

**(a)** $\Delta v = v_e\ln(m_0/m_1) = 3000\ln(1000/100)
= 3000\times2.302585 = \mathbf{6907.8\ m\,s^{-1}}$.

**(b)** If the mass flow is uniform, $\dot m = 900/20 = 45\ \mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$
and the thrust is $F = \dot mv_e = 45\times3000 = \mathbf{1.35\times10^{5}\ N}$,
independent of the instantaneous mass (which is why the *acceleration* grows
during a burn while the thrust does not).

**(c)** With 1000 kg of extra payload (initial 2000 kg, final 1100 kg):
$\Delta v = 3000\ln(2000/1100) = \mathbf{1793.5\ m\,s^{-1}}$ — only 26% of the
original. The logarithm punishes payload brutally, and the reason is structural:
the $\Delta v$ is generated by throwing mass overboard, so the mass you must keep
directly reduces the achievable velocity. Staging exists to discard the tanks and
engines that have already done their job, recovering some of that factor.

## ★★7 — Force systems

**(a)** $\sum\mathbf{F} = (+F\hat{x}-F\hat{x}) + (+F\hat{y}-F\hat{y}) = (0,0)$:
**net force zero**.

**(b)** Using $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}$ with
$\tau_z = xF_y - yF_x$:

| force | $\tau_z$ |
|---|---|
| $(0,+a)$, $(+F,0)$ | $-aF$ |
| $(0,-a)$, $(-F,0)$ | $-aF$ |
| $(+a,0)$, $(0,+F)$ | $+aF$ |
| $(-a,0)$, $(0,-F)$ | $+aF$ |

Net $\tau_z = 0$: **net torque zero**. (With $F = 10^{6}$ N and $a = 10^{3}$ m,
the demonstration prints net force $(0.000,0.000)$ N and net torque
$0.000$ N m.)

**(c)** If the second couple's signs are reversed — $(+a,0)$ carrying
$(0,-F)$ and $(-a,0)$ carrying $(0,+F)$ — then the net force is still zero but the
torque becomes $-2aF - 2aF = -4aF = -4\times10^{9}$ N m: **non-zero**. The
demonstration's first draft of this exercise produced exactly that error, which is
why the check is worth doing rather than asserting.

**(d)** The system in (a)–(b) — two couples of *opposite* sense, so their moments
cancel — is the **double couple**, and it is the standard representation of a
fault. It injects neither net linear momentum nor net angular momentum, so an
earthquake cannot "push the Earth" or "spin it up"; what it can do is
redistribute internal momentum and change the moment of inertia through the
static displacement field. The system in (c) is not a valid source representation
of a fault at all: it has a net torque, which would require an external agent.
Getting this right is what reduces the moment tensor from nine components to six
and constrains every source inversion.

## ★★8 — Landslide as a single force

**(a)** $J = F\tau = 10^{9}\times20 = \mathbf{2\times10^{10}\ N\,s}$.

**(b)** $\Delta v = J/M = 2\times10^{10}/10^{12} = \mathbf{0.02\ m\,s^{-1}}
= 2$ cm/s.

**(c)** A landslide is a *translation* of mass along a slope: it has a non-zero
net force (gravity's component down-slope, transmitted to the lake through the
collision), so its force system is a single force, not a symmetric couple. The
distinguishing observable is the *net displacement*: a single force leaves a
permanent offset in the far-field displacement (a "ramp" in the long-period
seismogram and a non-zero final displacement), whereas a double couple produces a
pulse whose far-field displacement returns to zero after the event. For a
tsunami-generating landslide the single-force nature is also why the generated
tsunami can be large without a large fault-like radiation pattern.

## ★★★9 — Momentum, moment, energy

**(a)** Linear momentum: $\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-1}}$ (kg m s⁻¹). Impulse:
$\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-1}}$ (N s) — **the same** as momentum, as the impulse–momentum
theorem requires. Seismic moment: $\mathrm{M\,L^{2}\,T^{-2}}$ (N m) — the same as
*energy*, not momentum. So momentum and impulse coincide; moment and energy
coincide. The two pairs are separated by a factor of a length.

**(b)** $J = 10^{9}\times20 = 2\times10^{10}$ N s, so the Earth's momentum changes
by $2\times10^{10}$ kg m s⁻¹. Against the Earth's mass (about $5.97\times10^{24}$
kg, derived from $GM/G$ and limited by $G$) that is a velocity change of
$3.3\times10^{-15}$ m s⁻¹ — utterly negligible as a rigid-body motion, yet the
*local* ground motion near the landslide can be centimetres per second. This is
the momentum version of "local motion, zero net motion".

**(c)** $E_s = (\Delta\sigma/2\mu)M_0 = (3\times10^{6}/6\times10^{10})\times10^{18}
= 5.0\times10^{13}$ J. The Earth's spin angular momentum is
$L = I\omega = 6.18\times10^{33}$ kg m² s⁻¹ (two-layer model). The ratio
$5\times10^{13}/6.18\times10^{33}$ is a comparison between a **joule and a
kg m² s⁻¹** — the numbers are not commensurable at all, and quoting a ratio would
be a category error. Angular momentum and energy are related but distinct; the
comparison that *is* meaningful is between $\Delta L$ from a source and $L$, and
that requires a torque and a duration, not an energy.

**(d)** **Landslide impulse:** the long-period seismic record, integrated to get
the force history (the ramp's amplitude and duration), or direct observation of
the displaced mass and its velocity change. **Fault seismic moment:** the
low-frequency plateau of the far-field displacement spectrum, inverted for the
moment tensor — with the zero-net-force constraint used as a check.

## ★★★10 — Impact

**(a)** $p = mv = 1000\times300 = \mathbf{3.0\times10^{5}\ kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$;
$K = \tfrac12(1000)(300)^{2} = \mathbf{4.5\times10^{7}\ J}$

**(b)** $F_{\text{avg}} = \Delta p/\Delta t = 3.0\times10^{5}/5\times10^{-3}
= \mathbf{6.0\times10^{7}\ N}$.

**(c)** The weight is $mg = 1000\times9.82025 = 9.82\times10^{3}$ N, so the
impact force is larger by a factor of **6.1\times10^{3}**. That ratio is the
reason impact problems are *always* treated with impulse rather than with a force
balance: during the impact the weight is a 0.016% correction, and the duration
(5 ms) is what converts the measurable quantity (momentum) into a force.

**(d)** A vertical impact transmits a *net vertical force*: the projectile's
downward momentum is transferred to the ground, and the momentum change must be
balanced by an impulse on the Earth. That is a **single-force** source, not a
double couple — the same classification as a landslide. The radiated pattern is
therefore asymmetric (a downward-vertical single force radiates a characteristic
"up-down" P-wave pattern with no S-wave nodal planes of the double-couple kind),
and inverting such an event with a moment-tensor-only model leaves a systematic
misfit. This is exactly why meteorite impacts, avalanches and volcanic
explosions are routinely modelled with an added single-force component.
