# Spherical Harmonics and Multipole Expansions

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Derive** the general solution of Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates
   by separation of variables, and explain why the exterior solution contains only
   $r^{-(l+1)}$ powers.
2. **Define** the Legendre polynomials, the associated Legendre functions with the
   Condon–Shortley phase, and the spherical harmonics $Y_{lm}$, and **state** the
   normalisation you are using.
3. **Expand** $1/\lvert\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'\rvert$ in Legendre functions, derive
   the addition theorem, and thereby **derive** the exterior potential as a series
   in $C_{lm}$ or $J_l$.
4. **Compute** the coefficients of a given mass distribution by orthogonality, and
   explain why the origin is conventionally placed at the centre of mass.
5. **Identify** the monopole, dipole and quadrupole terms, and explain why a
   spherically symmetric body is a pure monopole.
6. **Compute** the quadrupole coefficient $J_2$ of a homogeneous oblate spheroid,
   and relate the Earth's $J_2$ to the concentration of its mass.
7. **Explain** how degree maps to surface wavelength, and why deep sources produce
   low-degree coefficients.
8. **Verify** the orthonormality of the harmonics and the convergence of the
   multipole series numerically.

## 1. Why global fields are quoted as spherical harmonic coefficients

A gravity or magnetic model of a whole planet cannot be a grid: the Earth's
surface is a sphere, the field is smooth, and the data come from hundreds of
satellite tracks and thousands of ground stations with wildly different densities.
The natural language is the one in which Laplace's equation *separates* — the one
in which the exterior solution is a sum of terms each of which is itself harmonic.

That language is spherical harmonics, and it has three practical advantages that
are worth stating before the mathematics:

1. **Completeness and orthogonality.** The harmonics form an orthogonal basis on
   the sphere, so coefficients are computed by projection (an integral), not by
   solving a linear system.
2. **The radial dependence is fixed by the degree.** A term of degree $l$ decays
   outward as $r^{-(l+1)}$, and its surface wavelength is fixed. So one number,
   $l$, controls both the wavelength and the attenuation — the single most useful
   fact for interpretation.
3. **Interpretation is hierarchical.** The low-degree coefficients are the
   dominant, deepest, smoothest features; the high-degree ones are shallow and
   short-wavelength. A model can be truncated at a degree chosen by the data's
   resolving power.

The Earth's static gravity field is published as a set of $C_{lm}$ (equivalently
$J_l$ and the tesseral coefficients) up to some maximum degree, and the
geomagnetic field as a set of Gauss coefficients, which are the same objects for
the magnetic scalar potential. Both are distributed by international services; see
[ICGEM](https://icgem.gfz-potsdam.de/) for gravity and
[IGRF](https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/IAGA/vmod/igrf.html) for the magnetic field.
This lesson explains what those numbers are; it does not reproduce them, because
a coefficient set is data and must be taken from the distributing service.

## 2. Precise definitions

**Legendre polynomials.** For $x\in[-1,1]$, the Rodrigues formula defines

$$P_l(x) = \frac{1}{2^l l!}\frac{d^l}{dx^l}(x^2-1)^l , \tag{1}$$

equivalently generated by the Bonnet recurrence
$(l+1)P_{l+1}(x) = (2l+1)xP_l(x) - lP_{l-1}(x)$ with $P_0 = 1$, $P_1 = x$. They
satisfy $P_l(1) = 1$ and $P_l(-1) = (-1)^l$, and are orthogonal:

$$\int_{-1}^{1}P_l(x)P_m(x)\,dx = \frac{2}{2l+1}\delta_{lm} . \tag{2}$$

**Associated Legendre functions** (Condon–Shortley phase included, which is the
convention used in geodesy for the *real* form and in physics for the complex
form):

$$P_l^m(x) = (-1)^m(1-x^2)^{m/2}\frac{d^m}{dx^m}P_l(x) , \qquad 0 \le m \le l .
\tag{3}$$

They solve the associated Legendre equation

$$(1-x^2)\frac{d^2P}{dx^2} - 2x\frac{dP}{dx}
+ \left[l(l+1) - \frac{m^2}{1-x^2}\right]P = 0 . \tag{4}$$

**Complex spherical harmonics.** With $\theta$ the colatitude and $\phi$ the
longitude,

$$Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi) = \sqrt{\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\frac{(l-m)!}{(l+m)!}}\,
P_l^m(\cos\theta)\,e^{im\phi} , \qquad m \ge 0 , \tag{5}$$

and $Y_{l,-m} = (-1)^m Y_{lm}^{*}$. They are orthonormal on the unit sphere:

$$\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi} Y_{lm}\overline{Y}_{l'm'}\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi
= \delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'} . \tag{6}$$

**Exterior potential expansion.** For a body of total mass $M$ whose mass lies
entirely inside the sphere of radius $a$,

$$V(r,\theta,\phi) = \frac{GM}{r}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{a}{r}\right)^l
\sum_{m=-l}^{l} C_{lm}Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi) , \qquad r > a , \tag{7}$$

with the **dimensionless** coefficients

$$C_{lm} = \frac{4\pi}{2l+1}\frac{q_{lm}}{M a^l} , \qquad
q_{lm} = \int r'^l\,\overline{Y}_{lm}(\theta',\phi')\,\rho\,dV' . \tag{8}$$

The $q_{lm}$ carry units kg m$^l$; the $C_{lm}$ are pure numbers. The monopole
term is fixed by the total mass: substituting $Y_{00} = 1/\sqrt{4\pi}$ gives
$C_{00} = \sqrt{4\pi} \approx 3.544\,907\,7018$, and the $l=0$ term of (7) is
exactly $GM/r$.

**Axisymmetric (zonal) form.** If the body is symmetric about the polar axis the
expansion collapses to Legendre polynomials,

$$V(r,\theta) = \frac{GM}{r}\left[1 - \sum_{l\ge1} J_l\left(\frac{a}{r}\right)^l
P_l(\cos\theta)\right] , \qquad
J_l = -\frac{1}{Ma^l}\int r'^lP_l(\cos\theta')\,\rho\,dV' . \tag{9}$$

$J_2$ is the **dynamical form factor** (also called the gravitational flattening),
and $J_l$ for $l\ge2$ are the higher zonal coefficients.

**The real (geodesy) convention.** Many texts write the axisymmetric and tesseral
parts with real functions $\sin(m\phi)$ and $\cos(m\phi)$ rather than $e^{im\phi}$,
and define $\bar{C}_{lm}$, $\bar{S}_{lm}$ on fully normalised real harmonics. The
two conventions differ by normalisation factors and by the Condon–Shortley phase;
they describe the same physics. **Pick one and say which**, and expect to convert
when reading a coefficient set from a different service.

## 3. Derivations

### 3.1 Separation of variables, and why only $r^{-(l+1)}$ survives outside

Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates is

$$\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^2\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\right)
+ \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}
\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta}\right)
+ \frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial\phi^2} = 0 . \tag{10}$$

Substituting $V = R(r)\Theta(\theta)\Phi(\phi)$ separates the equation into three
ordinary differential equations, of which the angular ones combine into the
Legendre equation (4) with $l(l+1)$ as the separation constant and $m$ as the
azimuthal eigenvalue. The radial equation becomes

$$r^2R'' + 2rR' - l(l+1)R = 0 , \qquad
\text{with solutions}\quad R = r^{l},\ r^{-(l+1)} . \tag{11}$$

Only exponents that are integers occur because the $\phi$ equation must be
single-valued, which forces $m$ integral, and then $l$ integral for the Legendre
equation to have a solution regular at both poles.

**The boundary condition selects $r^{-(l+1)}$.** For the *exterior* problem
$V\to0$ as $r\to\infty$, the growing solutions $r^l$ are excluded and only
$r^{-(l+1)}$ survives. For the *interior* problem (the potential inside a body,
used in geodetic normal-field theory) the opposite choice is made and only $r^l$
survives. The physics is in the boundary condition; the equation alone permits
both.

### 3.2 The Green's-function expansion and the addition theorem

The starting point for the coefficients is the expansion of the inverse distance.
For $r > r'$,

$$\frac{1}{\lvert\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'\rvert}
= \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{r'^l}{r^{l+1}}P_l(\cos\gamma) , \tag{12}$$

where $\gamma$ is the angle between $\mathbf{r}$ and $\mathbf{r}'$. This is a
geometric-series statement about the ratio $r'/r$, decorated by the Legendre
polynomials that carry the angular dependence. The demonstration checks it
directly: for $r = 3$, $r' = 1.2$, $\cos\gamma = 0.6$ (exact value
$0.404226041727$),

| truncation $L$ | partial sum | relative error |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $0.333333333333$ | $1.754\times10^{-1}$ |
| $1$ | $0.413333333333$ | $2.253\times10^{-2}$ |
| $2$ | $0.415466666667$ | $2.781\times10^{-2}$ |
| $4$ | $0.404305066667$ | $1.955\times10^{-4}$ |
| $8$ | $0.404241591818$ | $3.847\times10^{-5}$ |
| $16$ | $0.404226056380$ | $3.625\times10^{-8}$ |

Every term is bounded by $(r'/r)^l = 0.4^l$, so convergence is at least
geometric; the partial sums are not monotone at low $L$ because the individual
$P_l(0.6)$ oscillate in sign.

The **addition theorem** rewrites $P_l(\cos\gamma)$ in terms of the harmonics of
each direction separately:

$$P_l(\cos\gamma) = \frac{4\pi}{2l+1}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}
Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)\overline{Y}_{lm}(\theta',\phi') . \tag{13}$$

Substituting (13) into (12) and into the integral $V = G\int\rho/\lvert\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'\rvert\,dV'$
gives equation (7) and the projection (8) directly: the integral over the source
volume is the projection of $\rho r'^l$ onto $Y_{lm}$, which is exactly the
orthogonality statement (6). Nothing else is needed.

### 3.3 Monopole, dipole and quadrupole

The first three terms of (7) are the physically meaningful ones for most
interpretation.

- **$l=0$, the monopole.** $C_{00} = \sqrt{4\pi}$ for any body, so the term is
  $GM/r$: the total mass, and nothing else.
- **$l=1$, the dipole.** $q_{1m} = \int r'\overline{Y}_{1m}\,\rho\,dV'$, i.e. a
  linear moment of the density. If the origin is placed at the centre of mass,
  $\int\mathbf{r}'\rho\,dV' = \mathbf{0}$, and every $q_{1m}$ vanishes identically.
- **$l=2$, the quadrupole.** The first term that survives when the origin is at
  the centre of mass and the body is not spherically symmetric. It measures the
  body's flattening or elongation, and it is the term that dominates the Earth's
  non-spherical field.

The demonstration verifies the dipole statement for two equal masses on the
$x$ axis at $\pm400$ m:

| origin | $\lvert q_1\rvert$ (kg m) |
|---|---|
| at the centre of mass | $0.000000\times10^{0}$ |
| shifted to $x = +200$ m | $9.772050\times10^{12}$ |

The vanishing is exact to the arithmetic, not approximate, which is why global
field models put the origin at the centre of mass: it removes a whole degree of
coefficients from the model. The shifted-origin value shows what a mis-centred
origin would cost.

The convergence of (7) for the same single off-axis point mass
($M = 1.0\times10^{11}$ kg at $r' = 500$ m, observed at $r = 2000$ m) is:

| $L$ | series $V$ (m$^2$ s$^{-2}$) | relative error vs direct sum |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $3.337150000000\times10^{-3}$ | $3.486\times10^{-2}$ |
| $1$ | $3.555735519367\times10^{-3}$ | $2.836\times10^{-2}$ |
| $2$ | $3.472925824744\times10^{-3}$ | $4.410\times10^{-3}$ |
| $3$ | $3.454777945687\times10^{-3}$ | $8.389\times10^{-4}$ |
| $4$ | $3.456579428603\times10^{-3}$ | $3.179\times10^{-4}$ |
| $6$ | $3.457739861865\times10^{-3}$ | $1.776\times10^{-5}$ |
| $8$ | $3.457675834068\times10^{-3}$ | $7.606\times10^{-7}$ |

against a direct evaluation of $GM/\lvert\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'\rvert$ of
$3.457678463951\times10^{-3}$. The series converges at the rate
$(r'/r)^l = 0.25^l$ set by the source-to-observer distance ratio — which is the
operational meaning of "the multipole expansion is a far-field expansion". It is
useless at distances comparable to the body's size.

### 3.4 A spherically symmetric body is a pure monopole

If $\rho$ depends only on $r'$, then

$$q_{lm} = \left(\int_0^{a}\rho(r')r'^{l+2}dr'\right)
\left(\oint\overline{Y}_{lm}\,d\Omega'\right) , \tag{14}$$

and the angular integral vanishes for every $(l,m)\neq(0,0)$ because
$\oint Y_{lm}\,d\Omega = \sqrt{4\pi}\,\delta_{l0}\delta_{m0}$ — the mean of every
non-constant harmonic over the sphere is zero, which is the mean value property of
lesson 02 applied at the centre. Only $l=0$ survives.

The demonstration computes the coefficients of a uniform ball of radius $1000$ m
and density $3000$ kg m$^{-3}$ directly by quadrature and reports

| $l$ | $C_{l0}$ | $\lvert C_{l0}/C_{00}\rvert$ |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $3.5449077018$ | $1$ |
| $1$ | $9.879073\times10^{-19}$ | $2.787\times10^{-19}$ |
| $2$ | $1.380752\times10^{-21}$ | $3.895\times10^{-22}$ |
| $3$ | $1.829667\times10^{-25}$ | $5.161\times10^{-26}$ |
| $4$ | $1.184288\times10^{-27}$ | $3.341\times10^{-28}$ |
| $5$ | $-1.182538\times10^{-31}$ | $3.336\times10^{-32}$ |
| $6$ | $-6.324107\times10^{-35}$ | $1.784\times10^{-35}$ |

with $C_{00} = 3.5449077018$ matching $\sqrt{4\pi}$ to ten decimal places. The
higher coefficients are zero to round-off, as the algebra demands. **Spherical
symmetry is not an approximation in this expansion; it is the statement that only
one coefficient is non-zero.**

### 3.5 The quadrupole of a homogeneous spheroid, and the Earth

For a homogeneous oblate spheroid with equatorial semi-axis $a$ and polar
semi-axis $c = a(1-f)$, the principal moments are $C = \tfrac25Ma^2$ (about the
polar axis) and $A = \tfrac15M(a^2+c^2)$ (about an equatorial axis), so

$$J_2 = \frac{C-A}{Ma^2} = \frac{1-(1-f)^2}{5} = \frac{2f - f^2}{5} . \tag{15}$$

The demonstration computes the same quantity from the volume definition of
equation (9),

$$J_2 = -\frac{1}{Ma^2}\int r'^2P_2(\cos\theta')\,\rho\,dV' , \tag{16}$$

by mapping the ellipsoid onto the unit ball and integrating, and recovers
equation (15) at $f = 0.001$, $0.0033528107$, $0.01$ and $0.05$ — for example at
$f = 0.01$ the quadrature gives $3.9800000000\times10^{-3}$ and the formula gives
$3.9800000000\times10^{-3}$. The worst relative difference over the four cases is
$3.0\times10^{-13}$: the volume integral really does compute the principal-moment
result.

**The Earth.** Using the WGS 84 defining flattening $f = 1/298.257\,223\,563$,
the homogeneous-spheroid value would be

$$J_2^{\text{hom}} = \frac{2f-f^2}{5} = 1.338876\times10^{-3} . \tag{17}$$

The measured value for the Earth is $J_2 \approx 1.08\times10^{-3}$ (a measured
input; obtain its authoritative digits and uncertainty from
[ICGEM](https://icgem.gfz-potsdam.de/)). The ratio is $0.8066$. Lesson 05 shows
that the same number follows from the defining rotation rate and flattening
through the hydrostatic relation $J_2 = (2f-q)/3$ with
$q = \omega^2a^3/GM = 3.461\,391\,8985\times10^{-3}$, giving
$J_2 = 1.081\,409\,8103\times10^{-3}$ — agreement with the measured value to three
figures, and a ratio $0.8077$ against the homogeneous spheroid.

**What the deficit means.** A body with the same shape but its mass spread
uniformly would carry *more* quadrupole. The Earth carries about 19 per cent less,
which is the quantitative statement that its mass is concentrated toward the
centre. This is the same conclusion the seismic density profile reaches, obtained
from the gravity field alone — and it is a genuine, non-obvious inference, made
possible only because the quadrupole is a *measured* degree-2 coefficient and the
shape is measured independently.

## 4. Physical interpretation

**Degree is wavelength, and both are depth.** A degree-$l$ harmonic has $l$ zeros
between pole and pole, so its surface wavelength is about $2\pi a/l$: on Earth,
degree 2 corresponds to roughly $20\,000$ km and degree 100 to roughly $400$ km.
Because a source at depth $d$ attenuates a surface feature of wavelength
$\lambda$ by about $e^{-2\pi d/\lambda}$, a deep source can only produce long
wavelengths. Hence the practical rule: **low degree = deep and large-scale,
high degree = shallow and local.** That is why truncating a model at degree
$360$ (about $110$ km) is appropriate for regional work and a degree-$2$
coefficient is a statement about the whole planet.

**Each degree decays differently with altitude.** In equation (7) the factor
$(a/r)^l$ suppresses degree $l$ at altitude $r$ relative to degree $0$. At a
satellite altitude of $r = 1.1a$, degree 2 is suppressed by $0.83$ and degree 10
by $0.39$ — which is why a satellite in a low orbit is sensitive to
high-degree structure and a high orbit is not. Mission altitude is chosen for
the degree range wanted, and that is a design decision, not a technicality.

**The coefficients are not independent of the reference radius or the origin.**
Changing $a$ rescales the coefficients at degree $l$ by $(a'/a)^l$; changing the
origin mixes degrees (that is exactly the dipole-removal statement of
Section 3.3). Coefficient sets are therefore only comparable when the reference
radius, the origin and the normalisation are all stated.

**Which coefficients are nonzero is a symmetry statement.** A body with $m=0$
only is axisymmetric. A body with no $\sin(m\phi)$ terms is symmetric about the
prime meridian. The pattern of zeros in the coefficient table is a fingerprint of
the source's symmetry, and it is read off in practice to check a model or a
forward calculation.

## 5. Assumptions and regime of validity

- **The observation point is outside ALL the mass.** Equation (7) requires
  $r > a$ where $a$ bounds all sources. For airborne or satellite data this is
  automatic; for a borehole inside the body it is false and the expansion is
  invalid there.
- **Convergence.** The series converges for $r > a$ and diverges at $r = a$ if
  mass reaches the boundary. Convergence is geometric with ratio $a/r$ at worst,
  and faster if the low-order coefficients are small (as they are for the nearly
  spherical Earth).
- **The Earth's field is *not* potential-theoretic in this simple form.** The
  measured gravity field includes the centrifugal term, which is *not* harmonic
  (lesson 02). The harmonic expansion applies to the gravitational part, or to
  the disturbing potential after a reference field is subtracted.
- **Truncation is a choice, and it is not neutral.** A truncated model is a
  low-pass filter with a sharp cutoff, which produces Gibbs-type ringing near
  high-degree features. Regularised or filtered models exist for that reason.
- **The magnetic case has an extra difficulty.** The magnetic scalar potential is
  harmonic outside all currents and magnetised material, but the Earth's core
  field is generated by a dynamo, and the internally generated part must be
  separated from the crustal part before the expansion means anything. The
  magnetic coefficients are not directly comparable to gravity coefficients of the
  same degree even where the numbers look alike.
- **Time dependence.** A satellite-derived gravity model is an average over the
  mission, and the field changes (tides, post-glacial rebound, mass
  redistribution). A "static" model is a temporal average with a stated epoch.
- **Reference radius and normalisation.** Coefficient sets are only comparable
  when $a$, the origin, the normalisation and the phase convention all match.

## 6. Units and dimensional analysis

| Quantity | SI | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| $P_l$, $P_l^m$ | dimensionless | $\lvert P_l\rvert\le1$ on $[-1,1]$ |
| $Y_{lm}$ | dimensionless | $\lvert Y_{lm}\rvert$ has magnitude $O(1)$ on the sphere |
| $q_{lm}$ | kg m$^l$ | degree-dependent units |
| $C_{lm}$ | dimensionless | $C_{00} = \sqrt{4\pi}$ numerically |
| $J_l$ | dimensionless | $J_2$ of order $10^{-3}$ for the Earth |

The dimensional bookkeeping is entirely in the factor $M a^l$ in equation (8) and
in the $(a/r)^l$ in equation (7): the combination $q_{lm}/(Ma^l)$ and $(a/r)^l$
are both dimensionless, so the whole series is a dimensionless modulation of
$GM/r$, which has units m$^2$ s$^{-2}$. A quick check that catches most mistakes:
**if your $C_{lm}$ has units, the reference radius is missing.**

The magnitude of the degree-$l$ term relative to the monopole is $C_{lm}(a/r)^l$.
For the Earth at the surface, $J_2 \approx 10^{-3}$ while $J_3 \approx 10^{-6}$ and
$J_4 \approx 10^{-6}$; the rapid decrease is why a degree-2 model already explains
the gross figure of the Earth and why a degree-20 model is a good "static" model
for many purposes. Those specific magnitudes are properties of the Earth, not
consequences of the theory, and must be taken from a model rather than assumed.

## 8. Common mistakes

1. **Using the expansion inside the body.** It requires $r > a$, with $a$ bounding
   *all* sources. Inside, the $r^{-(l+1)}$ argument fails and the general solution
   includes $r^l$ terms with different coefficients.
2. **Mixing normalisations.** Complex $Y_{lm}$ with the Condon–Shortley phase,
   real fully normalised $\bar{C}_{lm},\bar{S}_{lm}$, and "conventional" $J_l$
   coefficients are three different parameterisations of the same field. The
   numbers are not interchangeable.
3. **Forgetting the reference radius.** Coefficients are meaningless without $a$:
   degree $l$ rescales as $(a'/a)^l$.
4. **Treating $J_2$ as a shape parameter only.** $J_2$ is a *mass* moment. A body
   can be flattened and have small $J_2$ if the mass is concentrated (which is
   exactly the Earth's case) or strongly elongated in its density without changing
   shape.
5. **Reading $J_2$ as "how oblate the Earth is".** The flattening is a geometric
   quantity and $J_2$ a dynamical one; the two are related through the
   hydrostatic relation only under an assumption about the body's equilibrium.
6. **Assuming the Earth's field is harmonic when it is not.** The centrifugal term
   is not harmonic. Expand the gravitational or disturbing potential, not the
   measured gravity field, unless you first remove the reference.
7. **Expecting a truncated series to converge at the Earth's surface.** The
   relevant ratio is $a/r$ with $a$ bounding the sources; a deep mass at
   $r' \ll r$ gives fast convergence, a shallow one does not, and a source at the
   surface gives none.
8. **Quoting a coefficient set without its epoch, degree, origin and
   normalisation.** This is the commonest way to make a correct number
   meaningless.

## 9. Real-world applications

- **Satellite gravimetry and global gravity models.** The $C_{lm}$ are the model;
  the $(a/r)^l$ factor sets the sensitivity of a given orbit, and the maximum
  degree sets the resolution. See
  [07-05-03 Spherical Harmonic Solutions and Coefficient Interpretation](../../07-gravity-geodesy/05-satellite-gravimetry/03-spherical-harmonic-solutions.md)
  and, for the data, <https://icgem.gfz-potsdam.de/> and
  <https://grace.jpl.nasa.gov>.
- **The figure of the Earth.** $J_2$ plus the rotation rate gives the hydrostatic
  flattening through Clairaut's theory, which is why the flattening of a planet or
  satellite constrains its interior. See
  [07-01-03 Reference Ellipsoid, Normal Gravity and Clairaut's Theorem](../../07-gravity-geodesy/01-gravity-fundamentals/03-normal-gravity.md).
- **The geomagnetic field.** The main field is published as Gauss coefficients of
  the magnetic scalar potential; the degree-1 terms are the dipole, and the
  ratio of higher degrees to degree 1 measures how non-dipolar the field is.
  <https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/IAGA/vmod/igrf.html>, and the field itself at
  <https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/geomag/calculators/magcalc.shtml>.
- **Isostasy and lithospheric flexure.** Regional gravity and geoid anomalies
  (low degree) are compared with the flexural response of the lithosphere, and
  the comparison is done in the degree domain where each mechanism has a
  characteristic decay. See
  [07-01-06 Isostasy: Pratt, Airy and Flexural Models](../../07-gravity-geodesy/01-gravity-fundamentals/06-isostasy.md).
- **Airborne and satellite magnetic anomaly mapping.** Long-wavelength
  (low-degree) fields are removed before crustal anomalies are interpreted, and
  the removal is a degree truncation.
- **Planetary and lunar gravity.** The same coefficients give moments of inertia,
  which constrain core size and differentiation; the Moon's $J_2$ and $C_{22}$
  are a standard example of a body whose shape and interior are both
  non-axisymmetric.
- **Tides.** The tide-generating potential is a degree-2 harmonic, and the Love
  numbers that describe the Earth's response are defined in the same language.

## 10. Further reading

- **Blakely, R. J. *Potential Theory in Gravity and Magnetic Applications*
  (Cambridge University Press).** Spherical harmonic expansions and the
  multipole interpretation, with the prism and ellipsoid coefficients worked out.
  Level: advanced.
- **Hofmann-Wellenhof, B., & Moritz, H. *Physical Geodesy* (Springer).** The
  definitive account of the spherical harmonic expansion of the gravity field,
  normal fields, and the conventions. Level: graduate.
- **Heiskanen, W. A., & Moritz, H. *Physical Geodesy* (Freeman).** The classical
  reference; the treatments of $J_2$, Clairaut's theorem and the normal field
  remain standard. Level: graduate.
- **Jackson, J. D. *Classical Electrodynamics* (Wiley).** The multipole expansion
  in its native setting, with the addition theorem and the full spherical
  harmonic formalism. The magnetic analogue used in geomagnetism is this
  material. Level: graduate.
- **Arfken, G. B., Weber, H. J., & Harris, F. E. *Mathematical Methods for
  Physicists* (Academic Press).** The associated Legendre equation, the addition
  theorem and the orthogonality relations, with the alternative normalisations
  tabulated side by side. Level: advanced.
- **ICGEM, International Centre for Global Earth Models.**
  <https://icgem.gfz-potsdam.de/> — coefficient sets, their reference radii and
  their epochs. Level: reference.
- **IERS, *IERS Conventions* (current edition).** <https://www.iers.org> — the
  reference frames and the conventions in which the coefficients are quoted.
  Level: reference.
