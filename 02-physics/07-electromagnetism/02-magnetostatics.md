# Magnetostatics, Biot-Savart and Ampère's Law

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **State** the Biot–Savart law and use it to compute the field of a straight
   segment and of a circular loop.
2. **Derive** the finite-wire field
   $B = \mu_0 IL/(2\pi s\sqrt{s^2+L^2})$ and its infinite-wire limit, and explain
   why the two differ by a factor $1/\sqrt2$ when $L = s$.
3. **State** Ampère's law in integral and differential form, and **verify** it
   numerically for a closed path that does and does not link the current.
4. **Derive** the on-axis field of a circular coil
   $B_z = \mu_0Ia^2/[2(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}]$ and the centre value $\mu_0I/(2a)$.
5. **Derive** the magnetic dipole field
   $\mathbf B = (\mu_0/4\pi r^3)\left[3(\mathbf m\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})\hat{\mathbf r} - \mathbf m\right]$
   as the far field of a loop, including the factor $-2$ between the axial and
   equatorial amplitudes.
6. **Explain** why $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ rules out a magnetic monopole, and
   what that does to the analogy with electrostatics.
7. **Distinguish** $\mathbf B$ (tesla) from $\mathbf H$ (A/m): different
   dimensions, different units, not interchangeable.
8. **Verify** all of the above numerically, including $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$
   to $O(h^2)$ and Ampère's law for a non-symmetric path.

## 1. What magnetostatics describes, and what it does not

Magnetostatics is the theory of magnetic fields produced by **steady currents**,
with no time variation and no electric field energy being exchanged. Its central
result for geophysics is the field of a **current loop**, because every EM
transmitter used in exploration — a TEM or FDEM coil, an airborne EM bird, a
borehole induction sonde — *is* a current loop, and every one of them radiates, at
distances large compared with its size, as a **magnetic dipole**.

Three warnings before starting:

1. **The Earth's main field is not magnetostatic in origin.** The geomagnetic
   field is generated by a dynamic dynamo in a moving, conducting core
   ([02-07-04](./04-em-induction.md) and
   [08-01-05 Dynamo Theory](../../08-geomagnetism-electromagnetism/01-earth-magnetic-field/05-dynamo-theory.md)),
   and it is *observationally* dipole-like at the surface, but it is not the field
   of a stationary current loop in rock. Magnetostatics describes the geometry of
   the field, not its maintenance.
2. **Steady currents in the Earth are not the source of magnetic anomalies.**
   Anomalies come from induced and remanent **magnetisation** of minerals
   ([02-07-07](./07-magnetisation-materials.md)), not from currents.
3. **Any real EM system is time-varying**, so magnetostatics is the
   low-frequency, near-field limit of the full theory. It is an extremely good
   limit close to a transmitter coil, and it is the correct starting point for
   everything in lessons 03–06.

## 2. The Biot–Savart law

The magnetic flux density $\mathbf B$ produced by a steady current $I$ flowing
along a curve $C$ is

$$\mathbf B(\mathbf r) = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\oint_C \frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol\ell \times (\mathbf r - \mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r'|^3}
= \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\oint_C \frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol\ell\times\hat{\mathbf R}}{R^2} , \tag{1}$$

with $\mathbf R = \mathbf r - \mathbf r'$ pointing from the source element to the
observation point. Two features are worth naming at once:

- The field falls as $1/R^2$ for a *segment*, but a closed loop's far field falls
  as $1/R^3$ (Section 5) because opposing elements cancel the monopole-like term.
  There is no inverse-square magnetic field from a static source, ever.
- $\mathrm d\mathbf B \propto \mathrm d\boldsymbol\ell\times\hat{\mathbf R}$ is
  **perpendicular** to both the current and the line of sight. The field of a
  straight wire therefore circles the wire, and the field of a loop on its own
  axis points along the axis.

**The force between currents, and the definition of the ampere.** Two long
parallel wires separated by $d$, carrying $I_1$ and $I_2$, attract or repel with
force per unit length

$$\frac{F}{L} = \frac{\mu_0 I_1I_2}{2\pi d} . \tag{2}$$

Before 2019 this relation *defined* the ampere (with $\mu_0$ exactly
$4\pi\times10^{-7}$ N/A²). Since the 2019 SI redefinition the ampere is fixed by
the elementary charge, and $\mu_0 = 1.256\,637\,062\,12\times10^{-6}$ N/A² is a
**measured** constant with a relative uncertainty near $1.5\times10^{-10}$.
Equation (2) remains exact; only which quantity is defined and which is measured
has changed. Use $4\pi\times10^{-7}$ only when the difference
(about $1.5\times10^{-10}$ relative) cannot matter — and say so.

**Field of a finite straight wire.** For a wire of half-length $L$ along $z$,
carrying $I$, the field at perpendicular distance $s$ from its centre is

$$\boxed{\ B(s) = \frac{\mu_0 I L}{2\pi s\sqrt{s^2+L^2}}\ }
\qquad\xrightarrow[L\to\infty]{}\qquad B_\infty = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi s} . \tag{3}$$

At $L = s$ the factor is $L/\sqrt{s^2+L^2} = 1/\sqrt2 = 0.707\,107$: a wire only
twice as long as your distance gives just 70.7 % of the infinite-wire field.

## 3. Ampère's law

The curl of (1) is

$$\nabla\times\mathbf B = \mu_0\mathbf J \qquad\text{(magnetostatic Ampère's law),} \tag{4}$$

and integrating (4) over a surface bounded by a loop $\Gamma$ and applying
Stokes' theorem gives the integral form:

$$\oint_\Gamma \mathbf B\cdot\mathrm d\boldsymbol\ell = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}} , \tag{5}$$

where $I_{\text{enc}}$ is the net current threading $\Gamma$. Equation (5) is the
magnetic analogue of Gauss's law and is used the same way: exploit symmetry.

- **Long straight wire:** a circle of radius $s$ around the wire gives
  $2\pi s B = \mu_0 I$, hence $B = \mu_0I/(2\pi s)$ — reproducing (3).
- **Solenoid:** $n$ turns per unit length gives $B = \mu_0 n I$ inside, a result
  that is approximate near the ends.
- **Toroid:** $B = \mu_0NI/(2\pi r)$ inside the core.

Two subtleties that matter in practice:

1. **Equation (4) is incomplete.** The full law is
   $\nabla\times\mathbf B = \mu_0\mathbf J + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial\mathbf E/\partial t$;
   the displacement-current term is what makes electromagnetic waves possible
   ([02-07-03](./03-maxwell-equations.md)). In a conductor at geophysical
   frequencies $\mu_0\varepsilon_0|\partial\mathbf E/\partial t|$ is negligible
   compared with $|\mathbf J|$ (Section 8 of the next lesson makes this
   quantitative), which is precisely why the quasi-static approximation is the
   right one for EM methods.
2. **Ampère's law holds exactly only for closed circuits.** For a finite wire the
   line integral of $\mathbf B$ around a linking path is
   $\mu_0I\,L/\sqrt{s^2+L^2}$ rather than $\mu_0I$, because a finite wire has no
   return path and current is not conserved. The demo quantifies the shortfall.

## 4. The field of a circular loop

Place a loop of radius $a$ in the plane $z = 0$, centred at the origin, with
current $I$ circulating anticlockwise seen from $+z$. Parametrise the source by
$\phi$: $\mathbf r' = a(\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0)$ and
$\mathrm d\boldsymbol\ell = a\,\mathrm d\phi\,(-\sin\phi,\cos\phi,0)$. At the
on-axis point $\mathbf r = (0,0,z)$, $\mathbf R = (-a\cos\phi,-a\sin\phi,z)$ with
$R = \sqrt{a^2+z^2}$. The cross product
$\mathrm d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf R$ has $x$ and $y$ components that
integrate to zero and a $z$ component $a^2\,\mathrm d\phi$:

$$\boxed{\ B_z(z) = \frac{\mu_0 I a^2}{2\,(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\ } ,
\qquad
B_z(0) = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2a} . \tag{6}$$

Off axis there is no elementary closed form; the integral in (1) must be
evaluated numerically, and the demo does so. Two limits are worth remembering:

- **Near field** ($z \ll a$): $B_z \approx \mu_0I/(2a)$, nearly uniform.
- **Far field** ($z \gg a$): $B_z \approx \mu_0Ia^2/(2z^3)$ — the $1/z^3$ dipole
  decay, which is the reason EM signals die quickly with distance (Section 5).

## 5. Physical interpretation: the magnetic dipole as the central object

Define the **magnetic dipole moment** of a loop of area $A$ carrying current $I$:

$$\mathbf m = I\mathbf A , \qquad |\mathbf m| = I\pi a^2 \ \text{(for a circular loop)}, \tag{7}$$

with direction along the normal given by the right-hand rule. Expanding (6) for
$r\gg a$ and generalising to a vector form that is valid in every direction gives

$$\boxed{\ \mathbf B_{\text{dip}}(\mathbf r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^3}\left[3(\mathbf m\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})\hat{\mathbf r} - \mathbf m\right]\ } . \tag{8}$$

Two consequences of (8) are worth stating explicitly:

- **On the axis** ($\hat{\mathbf r}\parallel\mathbf m$): $B = \mu_0(2m)/(4\pi r^3)$.
- **On the equator** ($\hat{\mathbf r}\perp\mathbf m$): $B = -\mu_0m/(4\pi r^3)$ —
  *opposite* to $\mathbf m$, and half the axial amplitude. The demo verifies the
  ratio $-2$ numerically at $r/a = 100$.

The $1/r^3$ decay is the practical fact behind a hundred design decisions: to
double the depth of investigation at fixed moment you need $2^3 = 8$ times the
current–area product, which is why airborne EM systems use large, high-current
loops and why deep targets need large transmitter moments or long offsets.

**The Earth as a dipole.** The equatorial surface field of about 30 000 nT
(a **nominal** value; it varies with longitude, and the dipole moment itself
changes measurably over decades) combined with the mean Earth radius
$R = 6.371\times10^6$ m gives

$$m_\oplus = \frac{4\pi R^3 B_{\text{eq}}}{\mu_0}
= \frac{4\pi\,(6.371\times10^6)^3\,(3.0\times10^{-5})}{1.2566\times10^{-6}}
= 7.76\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{A\,m^2}, \tag{9}$$

which agrees with the handbook's nominal $8\times10^{22}$ A m². To obtain the
current value you must evaluate $g_1^0$ from the *current* International
Geomagnetic Reference Field, not quote a decaying number from a textbook.

**Relation to electrostatics.** The electric dipole potential falls as $1/r^2$
and its field as $1/r^3$; the magnetic dipole field also falls as $1/r^3$. But the
magnetic *source* is a circulation, not a pair of opposite poles, and there is no
$1/r^2$ magnetic term — see Section 6.

## 6. No magnetic monopole, and how the analogy with electrostatics breaks

The divergence of (1) is zero identically, because
$\nabla\cdot(\mathrm d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf R/R^3) = 0$ for every
$\mathrm d\boldsymbol\ell$. Hence

$$\boxed{\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0 \quad\text{everywhere}\ }
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\oint_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf B\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf A = 0 \ \text{for every closed surface.} \tag{10}$$

Field lines of $\mathbf B$ never begin or end; they close on themselves. The
consequences are structural, not cosmetic:

| | Electrostatics | Magnetostatics |
|---|---|---|
| Source term | $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \rho/\varepsilon_0 \neq 0$ | $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ always |
| Elementary source | point charge | current loop; the lowest multipole is the dipole |
| Field of the lowest multipole | $1/r^2$ (monopole) | $1/r^3$ (dipole) |
| Scalar potential | always, $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$ | only where $\mathbf J = 0$, then $\mathbf B = -\nabla\Phi_m$ |
| Vector potential | optional | structural: $\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A$ |
| Force on a test object | $\mathbf F = q\mathbf E$ | $\mathbf F = q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ (does no work) |
| $\mathbf H$ versus $\mathbf B$ | one field, $\mathbf D$ and $\mathbf E$ | two fields, $\mathbf H$ and $\mathbf B$ |

Three of these deserve emphasis.

**The lowest magnetic multipole is the dipole.** Gravity is monopole-dominated;
electrostatics is monopole-dominated *if* there is net charge; magnetism has no
monopole term at all. This is why a magnetised body produces an anomaly that is
comparable to the ambient field it rides on, while a density anomaly produces a
fractional change of $g$ of order $10^{-7}$ (the standard unit of gravity
surveying is the mGal, $10^{-5}$ m/s², against $g \approx 9.81$ m/s²).

**The magnetic force does no work.** $\mathbf F = q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ is
perpendicular to $\mathbf v$, so it changes the direction of motion but not the
kinetic energy. This is why magnetic fields confine plasmas and why — unlike an
electric field — a magnetic field cannot accelerate a stationary charge at all.

**$\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf H$ differ in dimension.** In vacuum
$\mathbf B = \mu_0\mathbf H$, and inside matter

$$\mathbf B = \mu_0(\mathbf H + \mathbf M) , \tag{11}$$

with $\mathbf H$ in A/m, $\mathbf M$ (magnetisation per unit volume — a magnetic
dipole moment per unit volume) also in A/m, and $\mathbf B$ in tesla. The
relation is *not* a unit conversion: $\mu_0$ has dimensions
$\mathrm{T\,m\,A^{-1}} = \mathrm{H/m}$, so $\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf H$ are
different physical quantities and cannot be added. Lesson
[02-07-07](./07-magnetisation-materials.md) works the consequences out
numerically.

**Vector and scalar potentials.** Because $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$, there
always exists a vector potential $\mathbf A$ with $\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A$;
in the Coulomb gauge $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A = 0$,
$\,
\nabla^2\mathbf A = -\mu_0\mathbf J$. Where the current is zero, $\mathbf B$ is
also curl-free and a scalar magnetic potential exists with
$\mathbf B = -\mu_0\nabla\Phi_m$ and $\nabla^2\Phi_m = 0$ — Laplace's equation
again, which is why potential-field magnetics and potential-field gravity share
their mathematics ([02-08 Potential Theory](../08-potential-theory/README.md)).

## 7. Assumptions and regime of validity

| Assumption | Where it entered | What breaks outside it |
|---|---|---|
| Steady currents ($\partial\rho/\partial t = 0$) | Biot–Savart and Ampère | Time-varying sources: the displacement current is required and the field becomes a wave or a diffusive transient (lessons [02-07-03](./03-maxwell-equations.md), [02-07-06](./06-em-in-conductors.md)) |
| Displacement current neglected | Equation (4) | Fails when $\sigma/(\omega\varepsilon)\lesssim1$: in resistive ground at MHz frequencies |
| Closed circuits for Ampère's law | Equation (5) | Finite wires give $\mu_0IL/\sqrt{s^2+L^2}$ instead of $\mu_0I$; the demo quantifies the shortfall |
| Non-magnetic medium ($\mu = \mu_0$) | Throughout | Magnetite-rich rock has $\mu_r$ measurably above 1, which changes the field and the skin depth (lesson [02-07-07](./07-magnetisation-materials.md)) |
| Superposition | Adding coil contributions | Nonlinear magnetic materials at high fields; not an issue at geomagnetic amplitudes |
| Quasi-static near-field geometry | Treating a transmitter coil as a dipole | Fails within a few coil radii (the demo shows a 38 % error at $r = 2a$); at large distances the *finite* propagation time of the field matters instead, and the wave description takes over |
| $\mu_0$ exact | Any formula written with $4\pi\times10^{-7}$ | Since 2019 $\mu_0$ is measured; the difference is $1.5\times10^{-10}$ relative — far below geophysical precision, but the statement "exact" is wrong |
| The geomagnetic field is a *dipole* | Section 5 | It is dipole-*dominated*: non-dipole terms are of order 10 % of the field and are exactly what the IGRF models in detail |

Two of these are worth carrying forward as a habit. First: **the quasi-static
approximation is not "low frequency" in the abstract; it is a comparison of two
currents**, $\sigma E$ against $\omega\varepsilon E$, and it must be checked
numerically before it is invoked (lesson [02-07-03](./03-maxwell-equations.md) §5).
Second: **the dipole is an asymptotic far-field description**, and the sentence
"a coil is a dipole" is false within a few coil radii; the demo's ratio table shows
how slowly the limit is approached.

## 8. Units and dimensional analysis

- Biot–Savart (1): $[\mu_0 I/R^2] = \mathrm{T\,m\,A^{-1}\cdot A\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{T}$ ✓.
- Dipole moment (7): $[\mathbf m] = \mathrm{A\,m^2}$; moment per unit volume
  (magnetisation) is $\mathrm{A/m}$ ✓, the same dimension as $\mathbf H$.
- Equation (8): $[\mu_0 m/r^3] = \mathrm{T\,m\,A^{-1}\cdot A\,m^2\,m^{-3}} = \mathrm{T}$ ✓.
- Equation (4): $[\nabla\times\mathbf B] = \mathrm{T/m}$ and
  $[\mu_0 J] = \mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\cdot A\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{T/m}$ ✓.
- **The tesla is a large unit for geophysics.** $1\ \mathrm{T} = 10^9$ nT; the
  geomagnetic field is tens of thousands of nT, i.e. tens of $\mu$T, and
  exploration anomalies are nanoteslas to hundreds of nanoteslas. A 100-turn,
  0.1 m-radius coil at 5 A has $m = 15.7$ A m² and produces only
  $3.1\times10^{-12}$ T (3.1 pT) at 100 m — four orders of magnitude below the
  natural background. EM surveys therefore measure the *time-varying*,
  coil-referenced part of the field, not the total field.

## 10. Common mistakes

1. **Adding $\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf H$.** They have different dimensions.
   $\mathbf B = \mu_0\mathbf H$ only in vacuum; inside a magnetised material the
   correct form is (11).
2. **Using the infinite-wire formula at finite distance from a finite source.**
   See the $1/\sqrt2$ result.
3. **Applying Ampère's law to a path that does not link the current.** You get
   zero, which is correct but useless; and, as the demo shows, for a *finite*
   wire even a linking path gives slightly less than $\mu_0I$ because the wire
   is not a closed circuit.
4. **Forgetting the sign of the equatorial dipole field.** Equation (8) has a
   minus sign; the field on the equator *opposes* $\mathbf m$. Mis-signing it
   flips the polarity of every modelled anomaly.
5. **Treating a loop as a dipole too close to the loop.** At $r/a = 2$ the
   amplitude is wrong by 4 % and the *direction* structure is wrong; the demo's
   ratio column shows the approach to $-2$ only slowly.
6. **Confusing magnetic moment with magnetisation.** The moment
   $\mathbf m$ (A m²) is an extensive property of a body; the magnetisation
   $\mathbf M$ (A/m) is intensive. The relation is
   $\mathbf m = \int\mathbf M\,\mathrm dV$.
7. **Quoting $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ as exact.** True before 2019, false now.
   The correction is far below any geophysical measurement precision, but the
   statement is wrong in principle and, in a curriculum, wrong statements are
   expensive.
8. **Assuming the geomagnetic dipole moment is a constant.** It changes
   measurably on human timescales; evaluate it from the current IGRF
   ([08-01-02 Dipole and IGRF](../../08-geomagnetism-electromagnetism/01-earth-magnetic-field/02-dipole-and-igrf.md)).

## 11. Real-world applications

- **Every EM transmitter.** A TEM or FDEM coil, an airborne EM bird, a
  borehole induction sonde and (at longer distances than the antenna dimension) a
  GPR antenna all radiate as magnetic dipoles. Transmitter design is the
  $1/r^3$ trade-off of Section 5.
- **Magnetic surveying.** The anomaly of a magnetised body is a sum of dipole
  contributions from volume elements; the dipole formula (8) is the kernel of
  every magnetic inversion ([02-07-07](./07-magnetisation-materials.md),
  [08-02 Magnetic Instruments and Surveys](../../08-geomagnetism-electromagnetism/02-magnetic-instruments-and-surveys/README.md)).
- **The geomagnetic field as a dipole.** The first-order description of the
  main field, the definition of magnetic latitude, the basis of palaeomagnetic
  pole determination and the reason a compass works
  ([08-01-02](../../08-geomagnetism-electromagnetism/01-earth-magnetic-field/02-dipole-and-igrf.md)).
- **Proton-precession magnetometers.** The precession frequency of protons in the
  ambient field, $\omega = \gamma_p B$, is a *magnetostatic* measurement of
  $|\mathbf B|$ with superb absolute accuracy
  ([08-02-02 Proton Precession](../../08-geomagnetism-electromagnetism/02-magnetic-instruments-and-surveys/02-proton-overhauser.md)).
- **Induction logging.** A borehole sonde's coil induces currents in the
  formation and measures the secondary field; the geometry is the loop problem of
  Section 4 with a conducting medium added.
- **Shielding design.** Because steady magnetic fields cannot be screened by a
  conductor (no magnetic monopoles, no image-charge analogue for a static
  $\mathbf B$ in a non-magnetic material), magnetic shielding uses high-
  permeability alloys that redirect flux rather than block it.

## 12. Further reading

- **Griffiths, D. J. *Introduction to Electrodynamics* (4th ed., Cambridge
  University Press, 2017).** Chapter 5 derives Biot–Savart, Ampère's law and the
  dipole expansion, and states clearly why $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ is
  fundamental. Level: intermediate.
- **Jackson, J. D. *Classical Electrodynamics* (3rd ed., Wiley, 1999).** The
  vector-potential formulation, multipole expansions and the magnetostatic
  boundary-value problem. Level: graduate.
- **Telford, W. M., Geldart, L. P., & Sheriff, R. E. *Applied Geophysics* (2nd
  ed., Cambridge University Press, 1990).** The magnetic and electromagnetic
  chapters connect the dipole formulas to survey geometry and to the units used
  in practice (gamma, nT). Level: intermediate.
- **Grant, F. S., & West, G. F. *Interpretation Theory in Applied Geophysics*
  (McGraw-Hill, 1965).** The classical treatment of dipole and prism anomalies,
  still the clearest derivation of the anomaly kernels. Level: advanced.
- **Nabighian, M. N. (ed.) *Electromagnetic Methods in Applied Geophysics*, Vol.
  1 (SEG, 1988).** Transmitter and receiver modelling from the Biot–Savart
  starting point. Level: advanced.
- **Blakely, R. J. *Potential Theory in Gravity and Magnetic Applications*
  (Cambridge University Press, 1995).** How the dipole kernel becomes a practical
  inversion, with the vector-potential viewpoint used throughout. Level: graduate.
