# Natural Variability: ENSO, NAO, PDO, Solar and Volcanic

Everything in [16-04-01](01-climate-system-components.md) and
[16-04-02](02-climate-feedbacks.md) was about a forced response. This lesson is
about the other half: the climate varies without being forced, and the
variability has structure — preferred timescales, preferred patterns and
preferred amplitudes — that can be mistaken for a forced signal. The lesson's
central quantitative claim is uncomfortable and easy to verify: in a
40-sample annual record with lag-1 autocorrelation 0.6, applying the textbook
white-noise trend threshold produces false positives **31%** of the time
instead of 5%. The climate's own persistence, not the analyst's carelessness,
is what breaks the test.

The individual modes are treated in the same spirit. ENSO is a coupled mode
whose period and delay are linked (derived in
[16-03-02](../03-atmospheric-circulation/02-monsoons-and-regional.md) §6) and
whose amplitude the linear theory does not predict. The NAO is a short-memory,
weather-like mode: a 10-day decorrelation time means monthly averaging already
removes about half its variance, so it cannot integrate forcing and cannot
manufacture a trend. Solar and volcanic forcings are *periodic* and *impulsive*
respectively, and a slab's response to each is computable exactly: an 11-year
forcing is attenuated to 82.6% of its equilibrium response with a ~1-year lag,
while a pulse's temperature response outlasts the forcing because the response
carries the slab's own relaxation time.

All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**. Every timescale,
amplitude, decay rate and noise level below is a **chosen teaching value**
printed by [`code/natural_variability_demo.py`](code/natural_variability_demo.py).
No observed record, index or eruption is used, and no amplitude, period or trend
is quoted from measurement.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Define** red noise as an AR(1) process, compute its decorrelation time, and
   **explain** why its trend distribution is wider than white noise's.
2. **Compute** the distribution of spurious trends from a stated null and
   **state** the false-positive rate that a white-noise threshold produces.
3. **Estimate** the record length needed for a trend to stand out from red
   noise, and **explain** why the threshold falls more slowly than $1/\sqrt{n}$.
4. **Distinguish** a spectral peak's period from the information in its width,
   and **state** what a peak does not identify.
5. **Classify** a mode by its memory: which modes can integrate a forcing and
   which cannot.
6. **Compute** the amplitude attenuation and phase lag of a slab's response to a
   periodic forcing, and **verify** them numerically.
7. **Compute** the response to an impulsive forcing and **explain** why its
   duration exceeds the forcing's.
8. **Apply** a multiple-testing correction and state why an uncorrected scan
   over lags, regions or seasons inflates significance.

## 1. Intuition: the null hypothesis is the hard part

An unforced climate is not a constant. Internal variability is generated by
instabilities, noise and nonlinearity, and it has memory: a month that is warm
tends to be followed by another warm month. That memory is the difficulty. Any
statistical statement about a trend is a statement *relative to a null*, and the
null must reproduce the memory, or the test will reject it far too often — not
because the data are unusual, but because the test is comparing the data with
the wrong kind of nothing.

The demonstration's numbers make this concrete. For 40 annual samples:

- the correct white-noise OLS slope threshold is
  $2/\sqrt{\sum(t-\bar t)^2} = 0.0274$ per year;
- applying that threshold to AR(1) records with $\phi = 0.6$ produces false
  positives at **31.1%**, and with $\phi = 0.8$ at **49.4%**;
- the correct 5% thresholds are 0.0477 and 0.0603 respectively.

Everything else in this lesson is a variation on that theme.

## 2. Definitions

| Term | Meaning used here |
|---|---|
| Red noise / AR(1) | $x_k = \phi x_{k-1} + \eta_k$, $\eta$ white with unit variance |
| Decorrelation time | $\tau = -1/\ln\phi$ sampling units |
| Effective sample size | $n_\text{eff} \approx n(1-\phi)/(1+\phi)$ for AR(1) |
| OU process | Continuous-time AR(1): exponential autocorrelation |
| Quality factor $Q$ | $\pi(\text{damping time})/(\text{period})$ for a damped oscillator |
| Forcing pathway | The time profile of an applied forcing |
| Multiple testing | Repeating a test many times (lags, regions, seasons) without correction |

## 3. Red noise and the trend problem

### 3.1 The null

AR(1) with unit innovation variance has variance $1/(1-\phi^2)$, lag-$k$
autocorrelation $\phi^k$ and decorrelation time

$$\tau = -\frac{1}{\ln\phi}: \quad
\phi = 0.2 \Rightarrow 0.62,\;
\phi = 0.5 \Rightarrow 1.44,\;
\phi = 0.6 \Rightarrow 1.96,\;
\phi = 0.8 \Rightarrow 4.48
\ \text{(annual samples)}.$$

The effective sample size $n_\text{eff} = n(1-\phi)/(1+\phi)$ is 10 for
$n = 40$, $\phi = 0.6$: a 40-year record of a 2-year-memory process carries about
a decade's worth of independent information.

### 3.2 What noise looks like as a trend

Regressing the first 30 years of 100-year AR(1) records on time (20 000
realisations, series normalised to unit variance):

| $\phi$ | 5th pct | median | 95th pct | ratio to white-noise threshold |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | $-0.0380$ | 0.0006 | 0.0383 | 0.91 |
| 0.2 | $-0.0430$ | $-0.0002$ | 0.0435 | 1.03 |
| 0.5 | $-0.0518$ | $-0.0001$ | 0.0524 | 1.24 |
| 0.6 | $-0.0564$ | $-0.0004$ | 0.0565 | 1.34 |
| 0.8 | $-0.0903$ | $-0.0004$ | 0.0916 | 2.17 |

With $\phi = 0$ the Monte Carlo 95th percentile (0.0383) and the textbook value
agree to the sampling error. As persistence grows, the null distribution
*widens*: at $\phi = 0.8$ the 95th percentile is 2.17 times the white-noise
value. A trend of 0.06 per year in a series of unit variance would be
"significant" against white noise and entirely unremarkable against a
four-year-memory null.

### 3.3 How long a record?

For $\phi = 0.6$, the 95th percentile of spurious trends and the ratio of a
genuine 0.02-per-year trend to it:

| length (yr) | 95th pct (per year) | 0.02 / threshold |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 0.1057 | 0.19 |
| 40 | 0.0403 | 0.50 |
| 60 | 0.0238 | 0.84 |
| 80 | 0.0152 | 1.31 |
| 120 | 0.0084 | 2.38 |

The threshold falls by a factor of 12.6 between 20 and 120 years, i.e. roughly
$n^{-1.55}$ rather than $n^{-1/2}$: **long records buy detectability faster than
the naive $1/\sqrt{n}$ argument suggests**, because persistence becomes a smaller
fraction of the record. And note what the table does not say: exceeding the
threshold means the trend is unlikely *under that null*. It does not mean it is
forced, and it does not establish a mechanism.

## 4. Oscillations: peak and width

A damped oscillator driven by white noise has a spectrum with a peak and a
finite width. For the demonstration's synthetic case (period 1400 d, damping
time 1000 d):

| quantity | measured | analytic |
|---|---|---|
| peak period | 1352 d | 1400 d |
| quality factor $Q = f_\text{peak}/\text{bandwidth}$ | 2.16 | $\pi\tau_\text{damp}/T = 2.24$ |

Both the period and the damping are recoverable — the second from the *width*,
not the peak position. That is the whole diagnostic value of a spectrum, and it
also shows the limit: two quite different mechanisms (a weakly damped
oscillator, or narrow-band stochastic forcing of a linear system) can produce
the same peak and similar widths. **A spectral peak constrains a timescale; it
does not identify a mechanism.** Identification requires a dynamical argument —
a phase relationship, a spatial pattern, an energy budget — that the spectrum
alone cannot supply.

## 5. Short-memory modes cannot integrate forcing

An Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with a 10-day decorrelation time, the
demonstration's stand-in for a weather-like mode such as the NAO:

- lag-1 (daily) autocorrelation 0.9048;
- autocorrelation at 30 days 0.0498 — essentially zero;
- variance retained by a 30-day average: 0.4555, so monthly averaging removes
  about half the variance.

The consequence is structural, not numerical. A mode with ten days of memory
cannot accumulate a small persistent forcing over years, because its own
autocorrelation decays long before the forcing does. A mode with *years* of
memory (ENSO's delayed feedback, or the ocean's heat capacity) can. This is the
criterion for deciding whether a mode can produce a trend: **compare the mode's
memory with the duration of the forcing.** The NAO can reorganise one season's
weather; it cannot make a decade.

## 6. Periodic forcing: solar

A slab of capacity $C$ driven by $F_0\sin\omega t$ responds with

$$|\Delta T| = \frac{F_0}{|\lambda|\sqrt{1+(\omega\tau)^2}},
\qquad
\varphi = \frac{\arctan(\omega\tau)}{\omega},
\qquad
\tau = \frac{C}{|\lambda|}.$$

For $C = 2.0423\times10^8$ J m⁻² K⁻¹, $|\lambda| = 5.418\
\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}K^{-1}}$ (so $\tau = 1.195$ yr), $F_0 = 0.5\
\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ and an 11-year period:

| quantity | analytic | measured from a 400-year integration |
|---|---|---|
| attenuation $1/\sqrt{1+(\omega\tau)^2}$ | 0.8259 | — |
| response amplitude | 0.0762 K | 0.0767 K |
| phase lag | 1.049 yr | 0.957 yr |

The amplitude agreement to 0.7% and the phase to about 9% (limited by the
finite record used for the fit) confirm the *integrator*: a linear system driven
by a sinusoid must give the analytic answer, so agreement here tests the code,
not the model. The physical content is the attenuation factor: an 11-year
forcing produces only 82.6% of the equilibrium response it would produce if
applied steadily, and the response lags by about a year. **Both are properties
of the slab's relaxation time alone** — a faster-responding surface would
attenuate less and lag less.

## 7. Impulsive forcing: volcanic

A negative forcing of $-2.0\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ decaying with a 1.5-year
timescale, applied to the same slab:

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| peak response | $-0.1511$ K at $t = 1.34$ yr |
| time to recover to 10% of the peak | 6.58 yr |
| forcing e-folding time | 1.5 yr |
| slab relaxation time | 1.20 yr |

The response *outlasts the forcing* — the temperature returns to its
pre-event value only after several times the forcing's decay time — because the
slab carries its own relaxation time. This is the reason a series of eruptions
can produce a multi-decadal temperature signature even though each individual
eruption's forcing lasts a couple of years, and it is also why attributing a
short cooling to a specific eruption requires the response function, not just
the forcing.

## 8. Multiple testing

Testing many things and reporting the best one inflates significance. In the
demonstration's synthetic experiment the effect is visible directly: with
$\phi = 0.6$, the white-noise threshold applied to 40-sample AR(1) records gives
false positives at **31.1%**; the correct threshold is 0.0477 against 0.0274.

The same arithmetic applies to any scan:

- **over lags** — a teleconnection test that scans 20 lags and reports the best
  one has an effective significance far below the nominal value;
- **over regions** — a pattern search over many grid boxes will find something;
- **over seasons** — four chances at the same 5% level;
- **over indices** — redefining a monsoon index and re-testing doubles the
  opportunity for a false positive.

**Corrections** (Bonferroni, false-discovery rate, or a maximum-statistic
Monte Carlo) are not optional refinements; they are what makes a $p$-value mean
what it says. And a $p$-value is not a magnitude: it does not describe how
strong the relationship is, only how surprising it is under a stated null.

## 9. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **AR(1) is a null, not a climate.** It is the cheapest null that carries
   persistence. If the real variability has long memory, spikes or regime
   shifts, AR(1) underestimates the width of the trend distribution.
2. **Gaussian innovations.** The Monte Carlo thresholds assume them; heavy tails
   widen the tails of the trend distribution further.
3. **Stationarity.** Real variability drifts; a "null" fitted to one period may
   not describe another.
4. **The slab is linear and single-reservoir.** Sections 6 and 7 use it with a
   constant $\lambda$ and no feedbacks; a feedback changes both attenuation and
   lag.
5. **The oscillator is linear.** Real ENSO has amplitude-dependent and
   asymmetric behaviour that a linear oscillator cannot produce.
6. **The forcing amplitudes are chosen.** No solar or volcanic amplitude is
   quoted from measurement; the *transfer functions* (attenuation, lag,
   response duration) are the results.
7. **No mode is identified by its spectrum alone.** Sections 4 and 5 give two
   examples of the gap between a statistic and a mechanism.

## 10. Worked examples

### Example 1 — the white-noise threshold and the correct one

For 40 annual samples,
$\sum(t-\bar t)^2 = \sum_{k=0}^{39}(k-19.5)^2 = 5330$, so the white-noise OLS
slope threshold for a unit-variance series is

$$\frac{2}{\sqrt{5330}} = 0.0274\ \text{per year}.$$

The Monte Carlo 5% threshold for $\phi = 0.6$ is 0.0477, a factor of 1.74
larger. Applying the smaller threshold gives false positives at 31.1%, not 5%.

### Example 2 — attenuation and lag

With $\tau = C/|\lambda| = 2.0423\times10^8/5.418/3.156\times10^7 = 1.195$ yr and
$\omega = 2\pi/11 = 0.5712\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$:

$$\omega\tau = 0.6826,\qquad
\text{attenuation} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+0.466}} = 0.8259,$$

$$\varphi = \frac{\arctan(0.6826)}{0.5712} = \frac{0.5986}{0.5712}
= 1.049\ \text{yr},$$

and the response amplitude is $(0.5/5.418)(0.8259) = 0.0762$ K against an
unattenuated 0.0923 K.

### Example 3 — how far off is a rule of thumb?

For a unit-variance series the OLS slope has standard deviation
$1/\sqrt{\sum(t-\bar t)^2}$ for white noise. For AR(1) with autocorrelation
$\phi$, the well-known variance inflation factor is $(1+\phi)/(1-\phi)$, so the
standard deviation is inflated by $\sqrt{(1+\phi)/(1-\phi)}$:

$$\phi = 0.6:\quad \sqrt{\frac{1.6}{0.4}} = 2.00
\quad\text{against the Monte Carlo's}\quad \frac{0.0477}{0.0274} = 1.74 .$$

So the rule of thumb *overestimates* the threshold by about 15% for
$n = 40$, $\phi = 0.6$: it is conservative rather than wrong, and it costs
detectability. The effective sample size
$n_\text{eff} = n(1-\phi)/(1+\phi) = 10$ describes the same effect — a 40-year
record of a two-year-memory process contains about a decade of independent
information — but it should not be substituted into the threshold formula
directly, because the trend's variance does not scale as $1/n_\text{eff}^3$.

## 13. Common mistakes

1. **Using the white-noise threshold on a persistent series.** Section 8
   quantifies the cost.
2. **Reporting a peak period as a mechanism.** Section 4's quality factor shows
   what a spectrum does contain.
3. **Assuming a short-memory mode can produce a trend.** Section 5's timescale
   comparison is the test.
4. **Ignoring the response's own duration.** Section 7's pulse outlasts its
   forcing by a factor of four.
5. **Scanning many lags, regions or seasons and quoting the best result.**
6. **Treating a $p$-value as an effect size.**
7. **Fitting a null to the whole record and testing a trend on part of it** —
   the null must be fitted to the same variability the test uses.

## Common pitfalls

- **Confusing the decorrelation time with the period of an oscillation.** One is
  a memory, the other is a repeated event.
- **Assuming a wider null means "no signal".** It means a signal must be larger
  to be detectible.
- **Reporting an unsmoothed spectrum's peak bin.** Bandwidth and windowing
  control what a peak means.
- **Comparing a trend from a short record with a null fitted to a long one.**
- **Forgetting that a volcanic response's recovery time is not the forcing's
  decay time.**
