# Rate-and-State Friction and Dieterich Seismicity Model

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Write** the Dieterich–Ruina rate-and-state friction law with a named state
   evolution law, and state the dimensions of $a$, $b$, $D_c$ and $\theta$.
2. **Derive** the Dieterich seismicity-rate response to an instantaneous stress
   step, $r(t)/r_0 = 1/[1-(1-\gamma^{-1})e^{-t/t_a}]$, and verify its two limits
   $r(0^+) = \gamma r_0$ and $r(\infty) = r_0$.
3. **Derive** Omori's law $n(t)=K/(t+c)^p$ as the high-rate limit of that
   response, with $p = 1$ and $c = t_a/(\gamma-1)$.
4. **Explain** why a stress step produces a transient rather than a permanently
   elevated rate, and what does set the new steady state.
5. **Measure** the fitted Omori parameters from a catalogue generated by the
   Dieterich model, and quantify their trade-off — including the counter-intuitive
   result that the fitted $p$ is *not* 1 even when the model is exactly
   Dieterich's.
6. **State** why a fitted $(K,c,p)$ is meaningless without its observation window,
   completeness threshold and detection model.

## 1. Intuition: friction has memory, and that is where the transient comes from

A Coulomb criterion is a *switch*: a fault is either below failure or above it.
Real faults are not switches. They creep, they strengthen when they sit still and
weaken when they slide, and the slide itself changes the conditions for the next
slide. Rate-and-state friction is the laboratory-verified description of that
memory: the friction coefficient depends on the instantaneous slip rate $V$ and
on a **state variable** $\theta$ that has units of time and represents, roughly,
the age of the population of microscopic contacts holding the fault.

Two consequences follow immediately, and they are the whole content of this
lesson.

1. **A stress step does not move a fault closer to a fixed threshold; it moves
   every point on the fault along its own memory.** Some points are nearer to
   instability than others, and the step advances all of them by an amount set by
   the local normal stress.
2. **A population of such points responds to a step with a rate that rises
   instantaneously and then decays back.** Not to a new permanently elevated
   level, but back to the rate that the background stressing rate supports. That
   decay is Omori's law, and it is *derived* here rather than fitted.

Numbers in this lesson are **SYNTHETIC**: every figure quoted below is printed
by `code/rate_state_omori_demo.py`, or is an exact standard constant stated with
its units. No real field, well or catalogue is measured, and no published number
is quoted.

## 2. Precise definitions

### 2.1 The rate-and-state friction law

With $\mu_f$ the friction coefficient, $V$ the slip rate, $\sigma_n$ the effective
normal stress and $\theta$ the state variable,

$$\mu_f(V,\theta) = \mu_0 + a\ln\!\left(\frac{V}{V_0}\right)
+ b\ln\!\left(\frac{V_0\,\theta}{D_c}\right),$$

where $\mu_0$ is the friction coefficient at the reference rate $V_0$, and $a$
and $b$ are dimensionless constitutive parameters (the **direct effect** and the
**evolution effect**). $D_c$ is the characteristic slip distance (m); $V_0$ is an
arbitrary reference rate.

**Aging (Dieterich) evolution:**
$$\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}t} = 1 - \frac{V\theta}{D_c}.$$

The alternative **slip law** replaces the $1$ with a term proportional to
$V\ln(V\theta/D_c)$; the two agree at steady state and differ in details of the
transient. Which one is used must be stated, because they predict measurably
different restrengthening.

**Steady state.** Setting $\mathrm{d}\theta/\mathrm{d}t = 0$ gives $\theta_{ss} =
D_c/V$ and

$$\mu_{ss}(V) = \mu_0 + (a-b)\ln(V/V_0).$$

The parameter combination that governs stability is thus $a-b$: **velocity
weakening** ($a-b<0$) permits instability, **velocity strengthening**
($a-b>0$, typically the deeper or the more clay-rich parts of a fault) does not.
That is why a creeping section can sit next to a seismogenic one on the same
fault.

### 2.2 The Dieterich seismicity model

Let $\tau$ be the shear traction, $\bar\sigma$ the effective normal stress, and
define

$$A\sigma \equiv (a - b)\,\bar\sigma \quad\text{(note: this is a stress)},$$

$$\gamma = \exp\!\left(\frac{\Delta\tau}{A\sigma}\right),$$

$$t_a = \frac{A\sigma}{\dot\tau_r} \quad\text{(the aftershock duration, units of time)},$$

where $\dot\tau_r$ is the background tectonic stressing rate on the faults in
question. Then the seismicity rate response to a step $\Delta\tau$ at $t = 0$ is

$$\boxed{\ \frac{r(t)}{r_0} = \frac{1}{1 - \left(1 - \gamma^{-1}\right)e^{-t/t_a}}\ }$$

## 3. Derivations

### 3.1 Why the response has that form

**Step 1 — nucleation time depends on stress.** In Dieterich's model each
potential hypocentre is a patch that will nucleate an event after a time that
depends on the stress it carries. A population of patches has a distribution of
nucleation times; the rate of events is minus the time derivative of the number
of un-nucleated patches.

**Step 2 — a stress step reorganises the clock.** A step $\Delta\tau$ at $t=0$
does not add events; it shortens or lengthens every patch's remaining nucleation
time. In terms of the state variable, a step in $\tau$ at fixed $\theta$ is
equivalent to an instantaneous jump in the effective position on the
$\mu_f$–$\ln V$ line, and the corresponding jump in the "distance to
instability" is $\Delta\tau/(A\sigma)$. Hence $\gamma$.

**Step 3 — the state variable relaxes.** The population's effective state relaxes
back towards its steady value on the timescale $t_a$, which is the ratio of the
constitutive stress $A\sigma$ to the background stressing rate. This is the
crucial step: **the relaxation timescale is set by the background loading, not by
the step.** Two identical stress steps in a fast-deforming and a slow-deforming
region produce the same initial rate jump and very different durations.

**Step 4 — solve the evolution.** Writing $S(t)$ for the normalised "state" of
the population, $\mathrm{d}S/\mathrm{d}t = -\left(S - S_\infty\right)/t_a$ with
the initial condition set by the step gives $S(0) = \gamma^{-1}$ and
$S_\infty = 1$. The seismicity rate is inversely proportional to $S$ (the further
from instability, the longer until the next event), so

$$\frac{r(t)}{r_0} = \frac{1}{S(t)}.$$

Solving the linear relaxation and inverting:

$$S(t) = \gamma^{-1} - \left(\gamma^{-1} - 1\right)e^{-t/t_a}
= \frac1\gamma + \left(1 - \frac1\gamma\right)e^{-t/t_a},$$

$$\frac{r(t)}{r_0} = \frac{1}{\tfrac1\gamma + \left(1-\tfrac1\gamma\right)e^{-t/t_a}}
= \frac{1}{1 - \left(1 - \tfrac1\gamma\right)e^{-t/t_a}}.$$

That is the boxed result.

**Step 5 — check the limits.**

- $t = 0$: $r/r_0 = 1/(1 - 1 + 1/\gamma) = \gamma$. A stress increase
  ($\Delta\tau>0$, $\gamma>1$) gives an immediate rate increase by $\gamma$.
- $t \to \infty$: $r/r_0 = 1/1 = 1$.
- $\Delta\tau < 0$: $\gamma < 1$, so $r(0^+) = \gamma < 1$ — a rate *decrease*, i.e.
  quiescence, returning to background.

The demonstration measures $r(0^+)/r_0 = 20.00$ for $\gamma = 20$ and
$r(10^6\,\mathrm{d})/r_0 = 1.000000$, both exactly as required.

### 3.2 Deriving Omori's law as the high-rate limit

When $\gamma \gg 1$ and $t \ll t_a$,

$$\left(1 - \gamma^{-1}\right)e^{-t/t_a} \approx 1 - \frac{t}{t_a}
\qquad\text{(first order in } t/t_a\text{)},$$

$$\frac{r}{r_0} \approx \frac{1}{1 - 1 + t/t_a} = \frac{t_a}{t}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
r \approx \frac{\gamma r_0\,t_a}{t}.$$

So the high-rate limit is a pure $1/t$ decay. Writing $\gamma r_0 t_a = K$ and
$p = 1$ identifies **Omori's law**,

$$n(t) = \frac{K}{(t+c)^p}\ \ \text{with}\ \ p = 1,$$

and the small-$t$ regulariser $c$ comes from the next order of the expansion:
expanding $1 - (1-\gamma^{-1})e^{-t/t_a}$ to first order in $t$ gives
$t/t_a + 1/\gamma$, so

$$r \approx \frac{\gamma r_0}{1/\gamma + t/t_a}
= \frac{\gamma r_0 t_a}{t + t_a/\gamma}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
c = \frac{t_a}{\gamma} = \frac{t_a}{\gamma-1}\cdot\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}
\approx \frac{t_a}{\gamma - 1}\ \text{for } \gamma \gg 1.$$

The demonstration computes $t_a/(\gamma-1) = 30/(20-1) = 1.579$ days for
$t_a = 30$ d and $\gamma = 20$.

**So Omori's law is not a separate empirical law here: it is the short-time limit
of a friction constitutive law.** That is the strongest form of the result, and
it is why the Dieterich model is the standard mechanism-level explanation for
aftershock decay.

### 3.3 What limits the derived Omori exponent

The approximation in §3.2 requires $r/r_0 \gg 1$ *throughout* the observation
window. The measured rate over a one-year window with $t_a = 30$ d and
$\gamma = 20$ is already down to $r/r_0 = 1.54$ after one $t_a$ and 1.00 by 300
days, so most of the catalogue sits in the decaying-to-background part, not in the
$1/t$ part. The fitted Omori exponent is therefore **not** 1, and the
demonstration measures exactly how far off it is:

| $\gamma$ | $r(0^+)/r_0$ | fitted $p$ | fitted $c$ (d) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 5 | 0.2330 | 0.0845 |
| 20 | 20 | 0.4331 | 0.0352 |
| 100 | 100 | 0.5635 | 0.0001 |
| 1000 | 1000 | 0.6798 | 0.0009 |

All with $t_a = 30$ d and a 365-day window. **The fitted $p$ never reaches 1**,
and $\gamma = 1000$ — a rate jump of three orders of magnitude — still gives only
$p = 0.68$. The commonly quoted "p is usually near 1" is a statement about
catalogues that are observed over a window short compared with $t_a$; it is not a
prediction of the theory. This is a genuinely important and under-appreciated
point, and it is the kind of statement that a remembered value would have got
wrong.

## 4. Physical interpretation

Three statements that follow directly from §3, and each is a common error when
stated loosely:

1. **A stress step is a transient.** A positive $\Delta\tau$ raises the rate by
   $\gamma$ and then the rate returns to $r_0$ — if the stressing rate has not
   changed. If a change in the operations *does* change the stressing rate, the
   new steady-state rate is different, and it is the change in $\dot\tau$ that
   produces it.
2. **The duration is an observable.** $t_a = A\sigma/\dot\tau_r$ is measurable
   from a catalogue as the time at which the rate has decayed. That makes the
   product $A\sigma$ (a stress) inferable, and hence $\Delta\tau$ from a measured
   $\gamma$. This is the one route from a seismicity rate change to a stress
   change that does not require assuming the receiver plane — which is why it is
   attractive, and why the assumptions of §5 matter.
3. **A rate change is a population statement.** $\gamma$ is a jump in the rate of
   a *population* of nucleation sites, not the probability of one specific fault
   failing. A hidden fault with no local nucleation sites contributes nothing to
   the rate no matter how large the stress change.

## 5. Assumptions and their consequences

| Assumption | Consequence if violated |
|---|---|
| $A\sigma$ spatially uniform | $\gamma$ varies with the effective normal stress; with depth, $\bar\sigma$ grows, so $\Delta\tau/(A\sigma)$ shrinks and the rate response weakens |
| Single stress step, instantaneous | A gradual or oscillating stressing needs a full time-domain solution |
| Transient is clipped at the next large event | A big aftershock resets the clock; real sequences are superpositions |
| $a > b$ throughout the causative region | Any velocity-strengthening patch does not participate but can load its neighbours |
| Background rate $r_0$ constant in space and time | Real background rates are heterogeneous; the derived $\gamma$ is then a spatial average |
| Nucleation-site density independent of $\Delta\tau$ | If the step activates *new* sites, the rate response exceeds the model's prediction |
| Depth-independent $D_c$ | $D_c$ is a laboratory parameter with a large and poorly constrained range; using one value is a stated simplification |
| Elastic interaction between events neglected | Cascades and Omori excitation by aftershocks need a full epidemic solution |

## 6. Units and dimensional analysis

| Quantity | Symbol | Dimension | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Friction coefficient | $\mu_f$ | 1 | |
| Direct / evolution effect | $a$, $b$ | 1 | Typically $10^{-2}$–$10^{-1}$ — a range, not a constant |
| Characteristic slip | $D_c$ | m | Microns to centimetres |
| State variable | $\theta$ | s | $V_0\theta/D_c$ must be dimensionless |
| Effective normal stress | $\bar\sigma$ | Pa | |
| Constitutive stress | $A\sigma = (a-b)\bar\sigma$ | Pa | $a-b$ dimensionless, so this is a stress |
| Background stressing rate | $\dot\tau_r$ | Pa/s | |
| Aftershock duration | $t_a$ | s | $[A\sigma/\dot\tau_r] = \mathrm{Pa/(Pa\,s^{-1})} = \mathrm{s}$ ✓ |
| Rate jump | $\gamma$ | 1 | $[\Delta\tau/A\sigma] = 1$ ✓ |
| Seismicity rate | $r$, $n$ | $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ (or per day) | |
| Omori $c$ | $c$ | s | Must have the same units as $t$ |
| Omori $K$ | $K$ | $\mathrm{s^{-1}\times s^{p}}$ | Depends on $p$; dimensionally awkward by construction |

**A dimensional warning about Omori.** $n = K/(t+c)^p$ only makes sense if $K$ is
allowed to carry the unit $\mathrm{s^{p-1}}$. That is why $K$ is not comparable
between catalogues: two studies quoting $K = 5$ and $K = 50$ may be quoting
dimensionally different objects. Always convert to a rate at a stated reference
time before comparing, e.g. $n(t=1\,\mathrm{day})$.

Confirming the $\gamma$ limit: with $\Delta\tau = 0.2$ MPa and
$A\sigma = 1$ MPa, $\gamma = e^{0.2} = 1.221$, a 22 % rate increase. With
$\Delta\tau = 2$ MPa and $A\sigma = 0.1$ MPa, $\gamma = e^{20} = 4.85\times10^{8}$,
which is absurd as a rate and is a sign that $A\sigma$ has been misestimated.

## 7. Worked examples

### 7.1 Computing $t_a$, $\gamma$ and the response

**Problem.** $\Delta\tau = 0.15\ \mathrm{MPa}$, $\bar\sigma = 40\ \mathrm{MPa}$,
$a - b = -0.005$, background stressing rate $\dot\tau_r =
10^{-3}\ \mathrm{MPa/yr}$. Find $A\sigma$, $\gamma$, $t_a$, $c$ and the rate
increase at $t = 1$ day.

**Step 1 — constitutive stress.**
$$A\sigma = |a-b|\,\bar\sigma = 0.005 \times 40\ \mathrm{MPa} = 0.2\ \mathrm{MPa}.$$
(With $a-b<0$ the stability analysis is satisfied; the rate-and-state seismicity
model uses $|a-b|\bar\sigma$ as the stress scale.)

**Step 2 — rate jump.**
$$\gamma = e^{\Delta\tau/A\sigma} = e^{0.15/0.2} = e^{0.75} = 2.117.$$

**Step 3 — aftershock duration.**
$$t_a = \frac{A\sigma}{\dot\tau_r} = \frac{0.2\ \mathrm{MPa}}{10^{-3}\ \mathrm{MPa/yr}}
= 200\ \mathrm{yr}.$$

**Step 4 — Omori $c$.**
$$c = \frac{t_a}{\gamma-1} = \frac{200}{1.117} = 179\ \mathrm{yr}.$$

**Step 5 — the rate at 1 day.** With $t/t_a = 1/(200\times365.25) =
1.37\times10^{-5}$,

$$\frac{r}{r_0} = \frac{1}{1 - (1-1/2.117)(1 - 1.37\times10^{-5})}
\approx 2.117\ (1 - 1.1\times10^{-5}) \approx 2.117 .$$

So at one day the rate is 2.12 times background, and it stays near that for
decades. **The timescale is set by the tectonic loading rate, and it is this slow
for a slowly loaded fault.** That is the quantitative reason a small
fluid-injection stress change can produce a rate change that is invisible on a
monthly plot but obvious on a decadal one.

### 7.2 The demonstration's fitted values

Run

```text
<repo>/.venv/bin/python code/rate_state_omori_demo.py
```

with MODEL inputs $r_0 = 1$ event/day, $\gamma = 20$, $t_a = 30$ d, a 365-day
window.

**Catalogue.** 481 events sampled by thinning from the Dieterich rate; the model
expectation is 455, so the sampler is taking the correct population.

**MLE fit.** $K = 9.144$, $c = 0.134$ d, $p = 0.4186$, with $\log L = -274.30$.

**Bootstrap over 200 resamples** (parameters transformed to
$(\ln K, \ln c, p)$):

| | $\ln K$ | $\ln c$ | $p$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ln K$ | +1.0000 | +0.4174 | **+0.9997** |
| $\ln c$ | +0.4174 | +1.0000 | +0.4003 |
| $p$ | +0.9997 | +0.4003 | +1.0000 |

$$\text{spread (95/5)}:\quad K\ 1.69,\quad c\ 5.82,\quad p\ 1.34 .$$

The correlation between $\ln K$ and $p$ is **0.9997**: they are, to the precision
of the fit, the same unknown. $K$ and $p$ cannot be reported independently, and
$c$ has a 5.8-fold 90 % interval even though the generating model had an exactly
known $c$.

**Window truncation** — the same catalogue observed for less time:

| $T$ (d) | $n_{\text{ev}}$ | $K$ | $c$ (d) | $p$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30 | 116 | 47.455 | 2.9076 | 0.9967 |
| 60 | 162 | 21.501 | 1.3046 | 0.7160 |
| 120 | 222 | 17.873 | 0.9446 | 0.6522 |
| 365 | 481 | 9.144 | 0.1336 | 0.4186 |

A study that observed for a month would have reported $p \approx 1.0$; the same
events seen for a year give $p = 0.42$. **The window is part of the measurement.**

**Fixing $c$ and refitting.** Holding $c$ at 0.05, 0.20, 0.50, 1.00 and 1.60 d
gives fitted $p$ of 0.410, 0.423, 0.436, 0.450 and 0.461, with
$\Delta\log L$ of $-0.14$, $0.00$, $-0.69$, $-2.03$ and $-3.48$. Since a
$\Delta\log L$ below about 2 is not a constraint for a single parameter, the data
admit $c$ anywhere in roughly 0.05–1.0 d.

**Discarding the early catalogue** (which is what happens when the detection
threshold is not constant):

| $t_{\min}$ (d) | events above $t_{\min}$ | fitted $p$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0.00 | 481 | 0.4186 |
| 0.25 | 477 | 0.4041 |
| 1.00 | 469 | 0.3870 |
| 5.00 | 429 | 0.2561 |
| 20.00 | 384 | 0.1447 |

Removing the first day changes $p$ by 8 %; removing the first 20 days changes it
by a factor of three. **Early-time incompleteness and the Omori parameters are
not separable without an explicit detection model**, and the first hours of a
sequence are precisely where detection is worst.

## 8. Python demonstration

`code/rate_state_omori_demo.py`:

1. evaluates the Dieterich response and prints its limits;
2. samples a catalogue by thinning (Lewis–Shedler) from the model rate;
3. fits Omori–Utsu by maximum likelihood with the exact log-likelihood
   $\sum\ln n(t_i) - \int_0^Tn\,\mathrm{d}t$;
4. bootstraps to measure the parameter correlation matrix;
5. truncates the window to show the $(c,p)$ drift;
6. fixes $c$ and refits $p$, reporting $\Delta\log L$;
7. discards early events to show the incompleteness degeneracy.

Exercises against the output: set `T_OBS = 30` and check that the bootstrap
correlation between $\ln K$ and $p$ changes; set `GAMMA = 1000` and confirm the
fitted $p$ is still well below 1.

## 10. Common mistakes

1. **Quoting $p \approx 1$ as a prediction.** The demonstration measures
   0.233 to 0.680 across $\gamma = 5$ to 1000, never 1.
2. **Reporting $K$ and $p$ independently.** Their fitted correlation is 0.9997.
3. **Reporting $(c,p)$ without the observation window.** The same catalogue gives
   $p = 0.997$ over 30 days and $p = 0.419$ over 365 days.
4. **Assuming a stress step gives a permanent rate increase.** It gives a
   transient; the steady state is set by the stressing rate.
5. **Using $t_a$ as a property of the rock.** $t_a = A\sigma/\dot\tau_r$ depends
   on the local tectonic loading rate.
6. **Treating $D_c$ as known to better than orders of magnitude.** It is a
   laboratory parameter with a wide range and a poor upscaling record.
7. **Confusing $A\sigma$ with $\bar\sigma$.** $A\sigma = (a-b)\bar\sigma$ and
   $a-b$ is of order $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-1}$, so the two differ by one to two
   orders of magnitude.
8. **Applying the model where $a<b$.** The seismicity-rate model presupposes
   nucleation; velocity-strengthening patches do not nucleate.
9. **Ignoring early-time incompleteness.** Measured: $p$ changes by a factor of
   three when the first 20 days are removed.
10. **Reading a rate change as a stress change without an error bar on $A\sigma$.**
    $\Delta\tau = A\sigma\ln\gamma$ multiplies the uncertainty in $A\sigma$ into
    the answer directly.

## 11. Real-world applications

- **Traffic-light design.** The decaying tail of a rate increase is why a
  protocol can use a rate-of-change criterion as well as a magnitude threshold
  (lesson 4).
- **Aftershock forecasting.** Omori's law with the parameters of §7 is the
  backbone of operational aftershock forecasting.
- **Discriminating induced from natural.** A rate that tracks an operation and
  then decays with a plausible $t_a$ is stronger evidence than a spatial
  coincidence, because the time-domain signature has a specific form.
- **Fault-stress inference.** The only route from a catalogue to $\Delta\tau$ that
  does not require assuming a receiver plane.
- **Hazard after a large event.** The same machinery gives the elevated rate and
  its decay, which feeds directly into
  [06-11-02 Aftershocks](../11-earthquake-statistics-and-hazard/02-aftershocks.md)
  and into probabilistic hazard calculations.
- **Data.** Inversion for $A\sigma$ and $\Delta\tau$ needs a well-constrained
  background rate; catalogues are retrieved from the
  [USGS FDSN event service](https://earthquake.usgs.gov/fdsnws/event/1/query)
  and the
  [International Seismological Centre](https://www.isc.ac.uk) for historical
  completeness.

- **Utsu, T., Ogata, Y., & Matsu'ura, R. S. *The centenary of the Omori formula
  for a decay law of aftershock activity*, J. Phys. Earth (1995).** The empirical
  law, its modifications, and the $\ln K$–$p$ trade-off in print.
- **Ogata, Y. *Statistical models for earthquake occurrences and residual
  analysis for point processes*, J. Am. Stat. Assoc. (1988).** The maximum
  likelihood machinery used in the demonstration.
- **Scholz, C. H. *The Mechanics of Earthquakes and Faulting* (Cambridge
  University Press).** Rate-and-state friction in its tectonic context.
