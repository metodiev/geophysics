# Volcano Seismology: LP Events, Tremor and Eruption Forecasting

Volcano seismology is the part of the subject where the measurement and the
decision are closest together. A volcano's seismicity is generated by the
movement of magma, gas and water at shallow depth, so the signals are
intrinsically *source* signals rather than the propagating phases of tectonic
seismology, and the operational question — will it erupt, and when — is asked
continuously.

The honest difficulty has two halves, and they are usually confused with each
other. The first is physical: an LP (long-period) event is a **resonance**, and a
resonance has a frequency and a quality factor, only one of which is usually
measured well. The second is statistical: even a precursor with a excellent hit
rate is nearly useless when eruptions are rare, because the non-eruptions vastly
outnumber the eruptions. A precursor's performance cannot be stated without the
base rate it is applied to, and a detection threshold cannot be chosen without
stating which error costs more. This lesson derives both halves and measures
them.

All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**. The volcano, its event
rate and the precursor statistics below are chosen teaching models. No real
volcano, monitoring network or eruption record is used, and no forecast skill
figure is quoted from any source.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Derive** the spectral signature of a damped oscillator source, including the
   position of the spectral peak and the relation $Q = f_0/\Delta f$.
2. **Measure** the recovered $Q$ from a synthetic LP spectrum and **state** the
   regime in which the estimate is biased.
3. **Derive** the RSAM detector and **quantify** the trade-off between detection
   latency and false alarms as a function of the averaging window.
4. **Define** hit rate, false-alarm rate, precision and recall, and **derive**
   the relation between precision and the base rate.
5. **Compute** what a precursor with a stated hit rate is worth at a stated base
   rate, and explain why the same precursor can be excellent and useless.
6. **Design** a monitoring rule that states its window, threshold, latency and
   false-alarm cost, and say what each choice gives up.
7. **Distinguish** measurements this module makes from qualitative background
   about volcanic sources, and state the assumptions behind the spectral model.

## 1. What makes a volcanic signal a source signal

A tectonic seismogram is dominated by propagation: the source is a double couple
and the waveform is filtered by the path. A volcanic LP event is different in
kind. The source is a *resonator* — a fluid-filled crack or conduit whose
geometry and fluid properties set a natural frequency and a damping — and the
signal recorded at a few kilometres is recognisably the resonator's impulse
response, often with little path distortion at the frequencies of interest.

That is why LP signals carry information about the *fluid*, and why they are
useful. It is also why they are hard to invert: a resonance is described by a
frequency, a quality factor and an amplitude, and a short, noisy, band-limited
record rarely determines more than two of those. The conventional classification
of volcanic seismicity — long-period events, volcano-tectonic events, tremor,
explosion quakes — is by **frequency content and duration**, and the boundary
values differ between catalogues. In this lesson the classification is used only
qualitatively; every number that follows is derived from or printed by the
demonstration.

## 2. The LP event as a damped oscillator

Model an LP event as a single-degree-of-freedom resonator: a damped sinusoid
starting at $t = 0$,

$$u(t) = A\,e^{-\sigma t}\sin(2\pi f_0 t) \quad (t \ge 0), \qquad u(t) = 0 \quad (t<0).$$

This is a MODEL, chosen for transparency; a fluid-filled crack has many modes and
a radiation pattern. Its Fourier transform is elementary,

$$U(\omega) = \int_0^{\infty} u(t)e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\,\mathrm{d}t
= \frac{A\,\omega_0}{(\sigma + \mathrm{i}\omega)^{2} + \omega_0^{2}},
\qquad \omega_0 = 2\pi f_0,$$

with amplitude

$$|U(\omega)| = \frac{A\,\omega_0}
{\sqrt{\left(\omega_0^{2} + \sigma^{2} - \omega^{2}\right)^{2} + 4\sigma^{2}\omega^{2}}} .$$

Two results follow by differentiating the denominator, and both are measurable:

**The spectral peak is *not* at $f_0$.** Minimising the squared denominator gives

$$\omega_{\text{peak}}^{2} = \omega_0^{2} - \sigma^{2}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
f_{\text{peak}} = f_0\sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{4Q^{2}}},$$

where the last step uses the damping convention $\sigma = \pi f_0/Q$. Damping
pulls the peak down, and the size of the pull is $0.501\%$ at $Q = 5$ and
$0.031\%$ at $Q = 20$ — visible only for strongly damped events.

**The half-power width measures $Q$.** With $\sigma = \pi f_0/Q$ the $-3\
\mathrm{dB}$ width is $\Delta f = \sigma/\pi = f_0/Q$, so

$$\boxed{\;Q = \frac{f_{\text{peak}}}{\Delta f}\;}$$

for the high-$Q$ case. The demonstration measures it directly by finding the
frequencies where $|U|$ falls to $1/\sqrt{2}$ of its peak:

| $Q$ input | $f_{\text{peak}}$ measured (Hz) | closed form (Hz) | $\Delta f$ (Hz) | $Q$ recovered |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.0 | 1.9720 | 1.9720 | 0.6866 | 2.91 |
| 5.0 | 1.9900 | 1.9900 | 0.4040 | 4.95 |
| 10.0 | 1.9975 | 1.9975 | 0.2005 | 9.98 |
| 20.0 | 1.9994 | 1.9994 | 0.1000 | 20.00 |
| 50.0 | 1.9999 | 1.9999 | 0.0400 | 50.02 |

Two honest readings. First, the peak position and the closed form agree to the
resolution of the grid at every $Q$ — the model and the measurement are
consistent. Second, **the width-based $Q$ is biased low at very low $Q$**
(2.91 against 3.0, 4.95 against 5.0) because the spectrum is skewed there: peak
position and bandwidth stop being separable, and a "dominant frequency" is only
meaningful together with the $Q$ that produced it. Reporting a dominant frequency
without a bandwidth is reporting half of a measurement.

## 3. Tremor: many LP events, and a detector

Tremor is, operationally, the superposition of many small LP events and
continuous flow noise; at the resolution of a monitoring instrument it is a
signal whose *envelope* evolves. That is why the standard monitoring quantity is
not a pick but the **RSAM** (real-time seismic amplitude measurement): the
running root-mean-square of a velocity record over a moving window,

$$\mathrm{RSAM}(t; T_w) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T_w}\int_{t-T_w}^{t} v^{2}(t')\,\mathrm{d}t'} .$$

RSAM is parameter-free except for one choice — the window $T_w$ — and that choice
is a decision about which error to prefer. The demonstration measures it. The
SYNTHETIC timeline is 1200 s long at 100 Hz with an event rate rising
exponentially toward an eruption at $t = 900\ \mathrm{s}$,

$$\lambda(t) = \lambda_0\exp\left(\frac{t - 900}{300}\right),$$

so the rate is four times its initial value (defined as the onset) at
$t = 300\ln 4 = 415.9\ \mathrm{s}$. LP events are generated as a Poisson process
with that rate, each contributing a $2\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $Q = 20$ damped sinusoid
(decay constant $\sigma = \pi f_0/Q = 0.3142\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, decay time
$1/\sigma = 3.18\ \mathrm{s}$), with lognormal amplitude scatter, on top of
synthetic noise of rms $1.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The detector is a
fixed rule: alarm when RSAM exceeds the median of the first 300 s plus
three robust standard deviations ($3 \times 1.4826 \times$ MAD). Over 20
realisations:

| RSAM window $T_w$ | mean threshold ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$) | mean latency after onset | false alarms before onset (runs with $\ge 1$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 s | $1.650\times10^{-6}$ | $4.76 \pm 3.95\ \mathrm{s}$ | 56.05 (20/20) |
| 5 s | $1.765\times10^{-6}$ | $10.48 \pm 8.63\ \mathrm{s}$ | 18.40 (20/20) |
| 20 s | $2.809\times10^{-6}$ | $47.29 \pm 44.46\ \mathrm{s}$ | 3.40 (13/20) |
| 60 s | $2.416\times10^{-6}$ | $80.78 \pm 68.63\ \mathrm{s}$ | 1.70 (15/20) |

The trade-off is measured, not asserted: from a $1\ \mathrm{s}$ to a
$60\ \mathrm{s}$ window the mean latency grows by a factor of about $17$, while
the mean number of false crossings falls by a factor of about $33$. A longer
window averages more noise but takes longer to register a rising level. **There is
no window that is best for both**, and the choice is a decision about which error
costs more — a monitoring-policy question, not a seismological one.

Two further measured lessons:

- The latency scatter is large ($4.76 \pm 3.95\ \mathrm{s}$ for the shortest
  window; $80.78 \pm 68.63\ \mathrm{s}$ for the longest). A single registered
  latency is not a property of the method.
- The threshold itself varies between realisations ($1.65$ to $2.81\
  \mu\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$) because it is estimated from a finite baseline. A
  monitoring rule that is recalibrated on every quiet window is not a fixed
  detector, and its false-alarm rate drifts.

## 4. Forecasting: the base-rate problem

Suppose a precursor has a measured **hit rate** $h$ (probability it sounds before
an eruption) and **false-alarm rate** $f$ (probability it sounds with no
eruption), and eruptions occur with base rate $p$. Then, over many episodes of
observation,

$$\text{precision} \equiv P(\text{eruption} \mid \text{alarm})
= \frac{p\,h}{p\,h + (1-p)f},$$

a two-line consequence of Bayes' rule. The demonstration measures the same
quantity by simulation: 2000 independent unrest episodes, $h = 0.90$,
$f = 0.10$ (both MODEL choices standing in for a measured precursor), and

| base rate $p$ | eruptions | alarms | true positives | false positives | precision | recall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.500 | 982 | 969 | 876 | 93 | 0.904 | 0.892 |
| 0.100 | 222 | 383 | 201 | 182 | 0.525 | 0.905 |
| 0.020 | 43 | 222 | 34 | 188 | 0.153 | 0.791 |
| 0.005 | 11 | 178 | 9 | 169 | 0.051 | 0.818 |

and the closed form predicts $0.900$, $0.500$, $0.155$ and $0.0433$ for those
base rates — agreement within the Monte Carlo noise, which is largest at the
smallest $p$ because only 11 eruptions occur in 2000 episodes there. (The
measured recall also fluctuates, for the same reason.)

Read the table as a warning about how precursor performance is reported. **Recall
stays near the hit rate while the base rate falls; precision collapses.** The
same precursor that is right 9 times in 10 when eruptions are common is wrong 19
times in 20 when they are rare — not because the precursor changed, but because
the non-eruptions outnumber the eruptions. A statement such as "the precursor
preceded 90% of eruptions" is a statement about $h$ alone, and it is compatible
with an alert system that is wrong 95% of the time.

The corollary is that improving $h$ is not the only lever, and usually not the
best one. From the closed form, halving $f$ from $0.10$ to $0.05$ at
$p = 0.02$ raises the precision from $0.155$ to $0.269$ — a larger relative gain
than raising $h$ from $0.90$ to $0.99$ would give ($0.155$ to $0.169$). **For
rare eruptions, false-alarm reduction is worth more than hit-rate improvement.**

## 5. What this means for a monitoring rule

Assemble the measured pieces into a rule, at the level of abstraction that a
monitoring protocol actually needs:

1. **State the quantity** (e.g. RSAM), its band, and the station.
2. **State the window** $T_w$ and the resulting latency, measured on the site's
   own noise. The numbers of §3 show the latency scale with $T_w$: a $60\
   \mathrm{s}$ window is a minute-scale promise, a $1\ \mathrm{s}$ window is a
   seconds-scale promise with an order of magnitude more false alarms.
3. **State the threshold and how it is set** (here: median $+ 3$ robust standard
   deviations of a quiet baseline) and how often it is re-estimated.
4. **State the decision cost**, which the physical measurement cannot supply. If
   a false alarm costs a costly evacuation, the threshold moves up and the
   latency grows; if a missed eruption is unacceptable, the opposite.
5. **Report the operational quantity that matters**: precision at the site's base
   rate, not hit rate alone.

A further honest caveat: the base rate $p$ is itself an estimate, from a short
and heterogeneous record of unrest episodes, and the precision is proportional to
$p$ when $p$ is small. The inference is therefore limited by the *catalogue*, not
by the seismology, and any reported precision should carry the base-rate
uncertainty rather than a single number.

## 6. Worked numeric examples

### Example 1 — resonance parameters from a measured spectrum

A synthetic LP event has a spectral peak at $f_{\text{peak}} = 1.9994\
\mathrm{Hz}$ and a $-3\ \mathrm{dB}$ width of $0.1000\ \mathrm{Hz}$.

$$Q = \frac{f_{\text{peak}}}{\Delta f} = \frac{1.9994}{0.1000} = 19.99 .$$

The damping constant follows from the convention $\sigma = \pi f_0/Q$: with
$f_0 \approx f_{\text{peak}}\sqrt{1 + 1/(4Q^{2})}$ — invert the closed form —
$f_0 = 1.9994/\sqrt{1 - 1/(4\times19.99^{2})} = 1.9994/0.99969 = 2.0000\
\mathrm{Hz}$, and

$$\sigma = \frac{\pi \times 2.0000}{19.99} = 0.3142\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},
\qquad \tau_{\text{decay}} = \frac{1}{\sigma} = 3.18\ \mathrm{s}.$$

The ringing dies out in about $6\tau = 19\ \mathrm{s}$: an LP event of this $Q$
occupies a window of a few tens of seconds, which is why LP and tremor are often
indistinguishable on a short-window display.

### Example 2 — precision of a precursor

For $h = 0.85$, $f = 0.15$ (SYNTHETIC), at base rates $p = 0.5$ and $p = 0.01$:

$$P(E\mid A)_{p=0.5} = \frac{0.5 \times 0.85}{0.5 \times 0.85 + 0.5 \times 0.15}
= \frac{0.425}{0.500} = 0.850,$$

$$P(E\mid A)_{p=0.01} = \frac{0.01 \times 0.85}{0.01 \times 0.85 + 0.99 \times 0.15}
= \frac{0.0085}{0.157} = 0.0541 .$$

The same precursor: right 85% of the time in a place where eruptions are common,
wrong 19 times in 20 where they are rare. A useful sanity check follows from the
formula: as $p \to 0$, precision $\to p\,h/f$, so the *odds* on an alarm are
$p h / f$ — the base rate multiplied by the likelihood ratio $h/f$.

### Example 3 — what a window costs

From the measured table, moving from a $1\ \mathrm{s}$ to a $20\ \mathrm{s}$ RSAM
window changes the mean latency from $4.76$ to $47.29\ \mathrm{s}$ (a factor
$9.9$) and the mean false-alarm count from $56.05$ to $3.40$ (a factor $16.5$).
Expressed as a rate over the $415.9\ \mathrm{s}$ of quiet record in each
realisation, that is from $0.135$ to $0.0082$ false alarms per second — from one
false alarm every $7.4\ \mathrm{s}$ to one every $122\ \mathrm{s}$ — bought with
$42.5\ \mathrm{s}$ of extra latency. Whether that exchange is worth it is not a
seismological question — which is the point of the exercise.

## 7. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **LP events are damped oscillators with a single mode.** Real LP and tremor
   spectra often show two or more peaks, implying either several modes or several
   sources; fitting one mode then aliases the rest into $Q$.
2. **No path or site effect.** The recorded spectrum is the source spectrum
   multiplied by the transfer function of path and site. Attenuation and
   scattering both broaden the apparent peak and lower the apparent $Q$ (this is
   the ambiguity of [06-05-02](../05-attenuation-and-anelasticity/02-scattering-vs-intrinsic.md)
   in a different guise).
3. **No instrument or band-limit effect.** A peak near the corner of the
   instrument response or of the analysis band is not a source peak.
4. **Poisson event statistics.** Real volcanic seismicity is clustered, and
   clusters inflate false alarms in the same way that a wrong Poisson assumption
   inflates hazard estimates.
5. **A stationary noise level.** Wind, thermal and cultural noise vary; a
   threshold estimated in a quiet hour is wrong in a noisy one.
6. **A known base rate $p$.** Precision scales with it, and the catalogue from
   which it is estimated is the weakest link in any forecast claim.
7. **The precursor is defined operationally** as a threshold crossing of a stated
   quantity. Different choices of quantity, window and threshold give different
   $(h, f)$ pairs, so $(h, f)$ is not a property of the volcano.

## 8. Units and dimensional analysis

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| frequency | $f_0$, $f_{\text{peak}}$ | Hz | |
| decay constant | $\sigma$ | $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ | $\pi f_0/Q$ |
| quality factor | $Q$ | dimensionless | $f_{\text{peak}}/\Delta f$ |
| amplitude | $A$ | $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ (velocity record) | |
| RSAM | — | $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ | RMS of a velocity |
| event rate | $\lambda$ | $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ | events per second |
| latency | — | s | |
| hit rate, false-alarm rate, base rate, precision | $h$, $f$, $p$ | dimensionless | probabilities |

The spectral peak formula carries its own check: $f_{\text{peak}} \le f_0$
always, with equality only as $Q \to \infty$, and the correction vanishes as
$1/(8Q^{2})$ for large $Q$. If a computation returns $f_{\text{peak}} > f_0$, a
sign or a factor of 2 has been lost in the damping convention.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Reporting a dominant frequency without a bandwidth.** They are one
   measurement together (§2).
2. **Reading $Q$ from a peak whose width is set by the path or the instrument.**
3. **Assuming the peak is at the source's natural frequency** at low $Q$.
4. **Quoting a precursor's hit rate as its skill.** Precision is what an operator
   experiences, and it depends on the base rate (§4).
5. **Comparing $(h, f)$ pairs measured with different windows, thresholds or
   quantities.**
6. **Choosing a detection window by habit.** State the latency it implies.
7. **Treating a fluctuated Monte Carlo value as the expectation** at small base
   rates, where the number of eruptions is tiny (§4).
