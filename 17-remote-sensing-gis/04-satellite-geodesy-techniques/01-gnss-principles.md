# GNSS Principles and Signal Structure

> All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**. The constellation
> geometry, orbit radius, clocks, noise, TEC and multipath used here are teaching
> data generated by the demonstration. The two carrier frequencies
> ($1575.42\ \mathrm{MHz}$ and $1227.60\ \mathrm{MHz}$), the first-order
> ionospheric coefficient $40.308\ \mathrm{m^{3}\,s^{-2}}$, the speed of light and
> the Earth radius are stated interface and physical constants. No real
> constellation, satellite, station or observation file is used.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Derive** the pseudorange observation equation from the signal travel time,
   **state** the four unknowns it contains, and **explain** why four satellites
   are the minimum.
2. **Solve** the linearised positioning problem by least squares appearing as a
   Gauss-Newton iteration, and **interpret** the recovered receiver clock offset.
3. **Derive** the dilution-of-precision matrix from the geometry and **compute**
   the position scatter it predicts for a stated range noise.
4. **Explain** why a delay common to all satellites is absorbed by the clock, and
   **derive** the dual-frequency ionosphere-free combination and its noise price.
5. **Derive** the $1/f^{2}$ ionospheric dependence from the first-order
   coefficient and **compute** the delay for a stated total electron content.
6. **State** the carrier-phase ambiguity and **quantify** the range precision of a
   phase measurement against a code measurement.

## 1. Intuition: four unknowns, one equation each

A GNSS receiver does something simple: it times how long a broadcast signal took
to arrive, multiplies by the speed of light, and calls the result a range. The
complication is that the timing is done with a cheap quartz clock, so the
measured quantity is a range that is wrong by the receiver's own clock error
times $c$ — tens of kilometres. Adding one unknown (the clock offset, in metres)
to the three position unknowns makes four, and four satellites make the system
solvable. Everything else in GNSS is an error term attached to that equation.

That single structure — four unknowns, four equations, then a large catalogue of
biases — is what this lesson builds. Two features matter for the rest of the
module: the geometry enters only through the *unit vectors* from the receiver to
the satellites (so it can be summarised by one matrix and its DOP), and any delay
that is *common to all satellites* is indistinguishable from a clock error, so it
must be removed by a measurement rather than by estimation.

## 2. Definitions and the observation equation

$$P = \rho + c\left(\delta t_r - \delta t^{s}\right)
+ I + T + M + \varepsilon,
\qquad
\rho = \left|\mathbf{x}^{s} - \mathbf{x}_r\right|,$$

| symbol | meaning | unit |
|---|---|---|
| $P$ | pseudorange, $c\times$measured travel time | m |
| $\rho$ | geometric range | m |
| $\delta t_r, \delta t^{s}$ | receiver and satellite clock offsets | s |
| $I$ | ionospheric group delay | m |
| $T$ | tropospheric delay | m |
| $M$ | multipath | m |
| $\varepsilon$ | receiver noise | m |
| $f_1, f_2$ | carrier frequencies | Hz |

The **wavelength** is $\lambda = c/f$, and the two carriers are separated enough
that the ionospheric delay differs between them by a measurable amount — the
subject of Section 3.4.

## 3. Derivations

### 3.1 Frequencies, wavelengths and the phase scale

*Numerical check (stated constants).*

| carrier | $f$ | $\lambda = c/f$ |
|---|---|---|
| $f_1$ | 1575.42 MHz | 0.190294 m = 19.0294 cm |
| $f_2$ | 1227.60 MHz | 0.244210 m = 24.4210 cm |

The ratio $f_1/f_2 = 1.283333$ is what makes the ionosphere removable, and the
wavelength is the unit of the carrier-phase ambiguity: one whole cycle of
unknown integer is $19\ \mathrm{cm}$ of range at $f_1$.

### 3.2 The position solution, and what the clock absorbs

Linearising about an initial guess $\mathbf{x}_0$, with $\mathbf{u}_k$ the unit
vector from the receiver to satellite $k$,

$$\begin{pmatrix} P_1 - \rho_1(\mathbf{x}_0) - \delta t_0 \\ \vdots \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} -\mathbf{u}_1^{\mathsf T} & 1 \\ \vdots & \vdots \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} \delta\mathbf{x} \\ \delta t \end{pmatrix},$$

solved iteratively. *Numerical check (SYNTHETIC).* The demonstration uses a
receiver at $(4,3,4)\ \mathrm{Mm}$, six satellites, a receiver clock of
$45.0\ \mathrm{km}$ (in range units), a teaching $\mathrm{TEC} = 20$ TECU, a
$2.4\ \mathrm{m}$ tropospheric delay and $0.30\ \mathrm{m}$ of range noise, and
starts from an $8.8\ \mathrm{km}$-wrong guess:

| quantity | result |
|---|---|
| position error (E, N, U) | $(-22.85,\ +35.35,\ -30.68)$ cm |
| total position error | 52.09 cm |
| recovered clock | $+44974.14$ m against a true $+44968.87$ m |
| discrepancy | $+5.27$ m |
| ionospheric delay at $f_1$ | 3.248 m |
| tropospheric delay | 2.4 m |

The clue is in the last three rows: the recovered clock is wrong by exactly the
**common part** of the two delays ($3.248 + 2.4 = 5.648\ \mathrm{m}$, less the
noise contribution). A delay that affects every satellite equally is
indistinguishable from a clock error — the residuals are flat, and no amount of
data resolves it. This is why the ionosphere is *modelled or measured*, never
simply estimated, and why the dual-frequency combination is not optional for
high-accuracy work.

### 3.3 Dilution of precision

With $G$ the design matrix above, the covariance of the estimate is
$\sigma^{2}(G^{\mathsf T}G)^{-1}$ for independent range errors of variance
$\sigma^{2}$. The standard scalars are

$$\text{GDOP} = \sqrt{\operatorname{tr}\left[(G^{\mathsf T}G)^{-1}\right]},\quad
\text{PDOP},\ \text{HDOP},\ \text{VDOP}$$

for the full, three-component, horizontal and vertical sub-blocks.
*Numerical check,* from the demonstration:

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| GDOP | 3.0041 |
| PDOP | 2.5529 |
| HDOP | 1.3722 |
| VDOP | 2.1527 |
| horizontal scatter for $\sigma = 0.30$ m | 0.412 m |
| vertical scatter | 0.646 m |

and with a deliberately clustered constellation the GDOP rises to **247.09** — a
factor of 82 worse for the same range noise. The geometry is as much a part of
the measurement as the electronics, and it is the reason a receiver reports its
DOP alongside its position.

### 3.4 The ionosphere and its removal

The first-order ionospheric group delay is

$$I = \frac{40.308\times\mathrm{TEC}}{f^{2}}
\qquad (\mathrm{TEC\ in\ electrons\,m^{-2}}),$$

so it scales as $f^{-2}$. *Numerical check,* from the demonstration:

| TEC | $I$ at $f_1$ | $I$ at $f_2$ | ratio | $(f_2/f_1)^{2}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 TECU | 0.8120 m | 1.3374 m | 0.607185 | 0.607185 |
| 20 TECU | 3.2481 m | 5.3494 m | 0.607185 | 0.607185 |
| 60 TECU | 9.7443 m | 16.0483 m | 0.607185 | 0.607185 |

The ratio is exactly $(f_2/f_1)^{2}$ for every electron content, which is what
makes the delay removable by combining the two frequencies:

$$P_{\text{IF}} = aP_1 + bP_2, \qquad
a = \frac{f_1^{2}}{f_1^{2}-f_2^{2}} = 2.545728, \qquad
b = \frac{-f_2^{2}}{f_1^{2}-f_2^{2}} = -1.545728,$$

with $a+b = 1.000000$ so a common range is preserved, and
$a/f_1^{2} + b/f_2^{2} = 0$ so the ionospheric term vanishes identically. The
demonstration verifies the cancellation numerically to $3.7\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m}$
— double-precision zero. **The price is noise:**

$$\sigma_{\text{IF}} = \sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\;\sigma = 2.9783\,\sigma,$$

so a bias of metres is traded for a factor of three in noise. That trade is
favourable whenever $I$ exceeds $\sim3\sigma$, i.e. almost always.

### 3.5 The carrier-phase ambiguity

The carrier phase measures the *fractional* part of the accumulated cycles, so

$$\Phi = \frac{\rho}{\lambda} + N + \cdots, \qquad N \in \mathbb{Z},$$

and the range is known only up to $N\lambda = 19.0294\ \mathrm{cm}$ at $f_1$
until $N$ is resolved (lesson [02](02-gnss-processing.md)). The precision is
extraordinary:

| phase error | range error at $f_1$ | at $f_2$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0.01 cycle | 1.903 mm | 2.442 mm |
| 0.001 cycle | 0.190 mm | 0.244 mm |
| 0.0001 cycle | 0.019 mm | 0.024 mm |

A phase measurement is a hundred to a thousand times more precise than a code
measurement — and a *wrong* integer displaces the solution by a whole wavelength.
The whole of the next lesson is about resolving and validating that integer.

## 4. Worked example — from four pseudoranges to a position, by hand

*All values SYNTHETIC.* A receiver with a zero clock offset is at the point where
the ranges to four satellites are
$\rho = (20\,100\,000,\ 20\,400\,000,\ 19\,800\,000,\ 20\,250\,000)\ \mathrm{m}$,
and the measured pseudoranges are each $6.0\ \mathrm{m}$ larger (a common delay).

1. **Unknowns.** Three coordinates plus the clock, in metres.
2. **Common-delay test.** All four residuals have the same sign and magnitude, so
   the extra $6.0\ \mathrm{m}$ is not a position error: shifting the position
   would make some ranges larger and some smaller. It is absorbed by the clock,
   which will solve as $+6.0\ \mathrm{m}$.
3. **Consequence.** The reported position is then *correct to the extent that the
   common delay is genuinely common*. A delay that differs between satellites
   (a low-elevation one) does move the position — this is the mechanism behind
   Section 3.3's DOP amplification.
4. **Ionosphere.** At 20 TECU the delay is 3.248 m at $f_1$ and 5.349 m at $f_2$;
   the difference between the two frequencies is $2.101\ \mathrm{m}$, which is
   enough to estimate the delay *per satellite* rather than absorbing it.
5. **Interpretation.** The single-frequency solution is a valid position up to a
   clock-plus-common-delay ambiguity of several metres; the dual-frequency
   solution removes the ionosphere at the cost of $\sqrt{a^2+b^2} = 2.98$ times
   the noise. Both statements belong in any accuracy claim.

## 5. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Line-of-sight propagation at $c$**, with the delays as additive corrections.
   Multipath and diffraction violate this and are mitigated, not modelled.
2. **Known satellite positions and clocks.** Broadcast ephemerides give metres;
   precise products give centimetres. The demonstration treats the satellite
   clock error as a known correction; only the *residual* is the budget item.
3. **Receiver clock offset is a single unknown.** True for one receiver; a network
   has one per station, which is what double differencing removes (lesson 02).
4. **Independent, zero-mean range errors** of equal variance. Correlated multipath
   and elevation-dependent errors break the assumption, which is why elevation
   weighting exists.
5. **The ionospheric first-order term dominates.** Second-order and ray-bending
   terms reach centimetres at low elevation and high solar activity.
6. **Teaching values.** The constellation, orbit radius, TEC, tropospheric delay
   and noise are synthetic. The carrier frequencies and the $40.308$ coefficient
   are stated constants, not measurements taken here.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Treating a pseudorange as a range.** It contains the clock and the
   atmosphere.
2. **Estimating a common delay instead of removing it.** It is absorbed by the
   clock and invisible in the residuals.
3. **Ignoring the DOP.** The same noise gives an 82-fold worse position in a
   clustered constellation.
4. **Using the ionosphere-free combination without its noise penalty.** It costs
   a factor of 2.98 in scatter.
5. **Quoting a carrier-phase precision before the integer is resolved.** A wrong
   integer is a 19 cm error.
6. **Assuming broadcast ephemerides are enough for centimetre work.** They are
   not.
