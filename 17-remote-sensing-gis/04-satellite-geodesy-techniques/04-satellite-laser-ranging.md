# Satellite Laser Ranging, DORIS and VLBI

> All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**. The stations, targets,
> baselines, source directions, noise and series in this lesson are teaching data
> generated by the demonstration. The constants ($c$, the Earth radius, the Earth
> rotation rate) are stated standards. No real station, satellite, baseline or
> catalogue source is used.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Convert** a laser round-trip time into a range and **quantify** the averaging
   that turns single-shot noise into a normal point.
2. **Estimate** a station's radial velocity from a ranging series and **identify**
   the systematic errors that limit it once the timing is sufficient.
3. **Derive** the degeneracy between a constant range bias and a global scale, and
   **quantify** the correlation that makes them inseparable from radial data
   alone.
4. **Derive** the Doppler observation of DORIS and **compute** the frequency shift
   for a stated range rate.
5. **Derive** the VLBI delay and fringe rate, **compute** the delay resolution of
   a bandwidth, and **relate** the angular precision to the baseline length.
6. **Quantify** the sensitivity of all three techniques to Earth rotation, and
   **explain** why VLBI determines rather than suffers from it.
7. **State** what each technique contributes to a reference frame that could not
   be obtained from GNSS alone.

## 1. Intuition: three techniques that define rather than measure

GNSS gives a position in a frame. The three techniques of this lesson are the ones
that *establish* the frame: laser ranging to satellites, Doppler tracking of a
satellite from beacons, and very long baseline interferometry of extragalactic
radio sources. They are less famous than GNSS and, for geodesy, more fundamental —
the definition of the celestial reference frame and of the Earth's orientation
within it belongs to VLBI, and the realisation of the terrestrial scale belongs to
laser ranging and VLBI together.

What unites them is that all three measure a **time** and convert it to a length
with $c$: a round-trip time, a Doppler shift, or an interferometric delay. It is
the most direct metrology in geodesy, and it is why these techniques, with no
satellite constellations or networks of receivers, still set the accuracy of the
others.

## 2. Definitions

| symbol | meaning | unit |
|---|---|---|
| $\tau$, $\Delta t$ | round-trip time, delay | s |
| $B$ | baseline vector between two antennas | m |
| $\hat{s}$ | unit vector to the radio source | — |
| $f_0$ | beacon frequency | Hz |
| $f_d$ | Doppler shift | Hz |
| $\rho$ | range, $\dot\rho$ its rate | m, m/s |

The three observation equations are

$$r = \frac{c\,\Delta t}{2},
\qquad
f_d = -\frac{f_0}{c}\dot\rho,
\qquad
\tau = \frac{\mathbf{B}\cdot\hat{s}}{c},$$

and the contrast between them is instructive: the first measures a length
directly, the second measures its rate, and the third measures a projection.

## 3. Derivations

### 3.1 Ranging: time to millimetres, and normal points

$$r = \frac{c\Delta t}{2}.$$

*Numerical check:* a picosecond is $0.1499\ \mathrm{mm}$ of range, so

| timing precision | range precision |
|---|---|
| 1000 ps | 149.8962 mm |
| 100 ps | 14.9896 mm |
| 10 ps | 1.4990 mm |
| 1 ps | 0.1499 mm |

Single-shot ranging is limited by the number of returned photons and by the pulse
width, not by the clock. Repeated shots are averaged into a **normal point**:

| shots | error, if one shot has 50 mm |
|---|---|
| 1 | 50.00 mm |
| 10 | 15.81 mm |
| 100 | 5.00 mm |
| 1000 | 1.58 mm |

A thousand shots turn $50\ \mathrm{mm}$ into $1.6\ \mathrm{mm}$, at which level
decadal plate motion is measurable. **Then the limit stops being the timing**: the
retroreflector array's centre-of-mass offset and its orientation-dependent range
signature are systematic at the centimetre level, and they must be *modelled* —
averaging cannot help, because they do not average away.

### 3.2 Velocity from a ranging series

A ten-year series of normal points with $6\ \mathrm{mm}$ of noise and an
$8\ \mathrm{mm}$ annual signal gives, from the demonstration's synthetic data with
radial velocity $-12.0\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$:

| model | velocity | bias | recovered annual amplitude |
|---|---|---|---|
| linear only | $-12.1015\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ | $-0.1015$ | — |
| linear + annual | $-11.9519\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ | $+0.0481$ | 7.9121 mm (true 8.0) |

A decade of data resolves the annual term, so the bias is small; with a two-year
record it would not be (measured in
[17-04-02](02-gnss-processing.md) as $-2.34\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$). And the formal
error of a fitted velocity falls as $1/\sqrt{N}$ **only while the noise is white**:
satellite rotation, atmospheric delay and monument instability all add correlated
terms, so the noise model belongs in the report alongside the velocity —
the same warning as the GNSS time series, and measured there as a factor of 7.3.

### 3.3 The radial-bias degeneracy

A constant range bias $\delta$ shifts a station radially by $\delta$. A global
scale error $s$ shifts it by $s\,r$. Since every station sits at nearly the same
radius, these are the same parameter. Making that quantitative: with $N$ stations
whose radii span $\Delta r$ about $R_E$, the estimates of $(\delta, s)$ have a
covariance $\sigma^{2}(G^{\mathsf T}G)^{-1}$ with
$G = [\mathbf{1},\ \mathbf{r}/R_E]$. *Numerical check,* with 40 stations spread
over $2.9\ \mathrm{km}$ of radius:

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| correlation between the bias and scale estimates | $-1.000000$ |
| variance inflation of the bias estimate | $1.540\,650\times10^{6}$ |
| standard error of the bias | $1241.2291\,\sigma$ |
| fractional radius spread | $4.71\times10^{-4}$ |

The correlation is $-1$ to six decimals: the two parameters are the same as far as
radial observations are concerned, and the bias is essentially undetermined
(its standard error is inflated by a factor of 1241). **A global scale is never
estimated from ranging alone.** It is constrained by the geometry that breaks the
degeneracy — the transverse directions measured by VLBI and GNSS, and the varying
radius of the satellite's orbit. This is the same non-uniqueness argument as the
InSAR height trade-off and the receiver-function depth trade-off elsewhere in the
curriculum: **any parameter whose signature mimics the error you are estimating
will absorb it**, and the correlation between the two is the honest statement of
what the data can separate.

### 3.4 DORIS: the Doppler observation

$$f_d = -\frac{f_0}{c}\dot\rho.$$

*Numerical check,* with $f_0 = 2.0\ \mathrm{GHz}$:

| range rate | Doppler shift |
|---|---|
| $-7.50\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ | $+50.0346\ \mathrm{kHz}$ |
| $-3.00$ | $+20.0138\ \mathrm{kHz}$ |
| $0.00$ | 0 |
| $+3.00$ | $-20.0138\ \mathrm{kHz}$ |
| $+7.50$ | $-50.0346\ \mathrm{kHz}$ |

Tens of kilohertz are trivially measurable; the science is in the *departures*
from a smooth Doppler history, because the shape near closest approach encodes the
geometry. Note the sign: approaching (negative range rate) gives a positive shift.
The observation is geometrically the same as ranging — a distance or its rate
between a ground station and a satellite — so it inherits the radial-bias
degeneracy of Section 3.3, and it needs the same independent constraints to be
useful for scale.

### 3.5 VLBI: delay, resolution and direction

Two antennas separated by $\mathbf{B}$ see a source in direction $\hat{s}$ with a
geometric delay

$$\tau = \frac{\mathbf{B}\cdot\hat{s}}{c}.$$

The delay resolution is set by the recorded bandwidth, $1/\mathrm{BW}$, and its
length equivalent is $c/\mathrm{BW}$. *Numerical check,* with $\mathrm{BW} = 1\
\mathrm{GHz}$: $1/\mathrm{BW} = 1000\ \mathrm{ps} = 299.8\ \mathrm{mm}$ of path.
One nanosecond is $300\ \mathrm{mm}$ — so a delay measured to a picosecond locates
a source to a fraction of a milliarcsecond on a baseline of thousands of
kilometres:

| baseline | maximum geometric delay | fringe period at 8.4 GHz |
|---|---|---|
| 500 km | 1667.820 µs | 0.119 ns |
| 2000 km | 6671.282 µs | 0.119 ns |
| 6000 km | 20013.846 µs | 0.119 ns |
| 12000 km | 40027.691 µs | 0.119 ns |

The delay is *enormous* compared with the resolution (hours of microseconds against
picoseconds), which is exactly why the direction is so well determined: the
precision comes from the *ratio*. Solving for the direction from three baselines
with $10\ \mathrm{ps}$ of delay noise gives, from the demonstration,

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| mean baseline | 3686.3 km |
| expected precision, $c\sigma_\tau/B$ | 0.0002 milliarcsecond |
| realized error (mean of 200 noise draws) | 0.0002 mas $= 0.22$ µas |
| delay magnitude | 5606.25 µs, i.e. a fractional precision of $10^{-5}$ |

The angular precision scales as $c\sigma_\tau/B$: longer baselines buy
proportional precision, which is why VLBI baselines are intercontinental and why
its products *define* the celestial reference frame.

### 3.6 Earth rotation is in all three

The rotation rate $\Omega$ enters every geometry: it moves the stations (ranging,
DORIS) and rotates the baseline (VLBI). For a $6000\ \mathrm{km}$ baseline a UT1
error $\mathrm{d}t$ rotates the Earth by $\Omega\,\mathrm{d}t$ and changes the
delay by $(B/c)\Omega\,\mathrm{d}t$:

| UT1 offset | rotation | delay change | versus 1 ps of delay noise |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10.00 ms | 0.7292 µrad | 14594.33 ps | $1.5\times10^{4}$× |
| 1.00 ms | 0.0729 µrad | 1459.43 ps | $1.5\times10^{3}$× |
| 0.10 ms | 0.0073 µrad | 145.94 ps | $1.5\times10^{2}$× |
| 0.01 ms | 0.0007 µrad | 14.59 ps | $1.5\times10^{1}$× |

These changes dwarf the picosecond-level delay precision, so VLBI does not
*suffer* from an uncertain Earth rotation — it **measures** it, and its products
define the Earth-orientation parameters that every other technique must then use.
For the analyst the direction of the argument matters: a solution must state
whether those parameters were estimated, fixed to an external series, or
predicted, because all three choices are defensible and they give different
station coordinates.

### 3.7 What each technique contributes

| technique | observation | dominant contribution | what it cannot do alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| laser ranging | round-trip time | station position and **scale**; orbit validation; low-degree gravity | no absolute frame, no Earth orientation, radial bias degenerate (Section 3.3) |
| DORIS | Doppler | orbit determination for altimetry; station position | inherits the radial-bias degeneracy; needs laser/VLBI for scale |
| VLBI | interferometric delay | **celestial frame**, Earth orientation, and the tie between them | poor for a dense network; few stations |

None of the three can establish a full frame by itself; the frame is the
*combination*, tied by co-located instruments whose local vectors must themselves
be measured. That is why the reference-frame module
([07-04](../../07-gravity-geodesy/04-geodesy-reference-frames/README.md)) is a
prerequisite rather than a sequel to this lesson: the techniques here produce
numbers that only mean something inside a stated frame, and the frame is
manufactured by their intersection.

## 4. Worked example — a picosecond delay turned into a plate motion

*All values SYNTHETIC.* A laser station's range to a target changes by
$1.20\ \mathrm{m}$ over ten years, with an annual term of $8\ \mathrm{mm}$ and
$6\ \mathrm{mm}$ of normal-point noise.

1. **Velocity.** $1.20\ \mathrm{m}/10\ \mathrm{yr} = 120\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$
   toward the satellite (a fast convergence rate).
2. **Formal precision.** With $N = 400$ normal points and $6\ \mathrm{mm}$ of
   noise over ten years, a linear fit has a formal scatter of order
   $\sigma\sqrt{12/(N\,\Delta t^{2})} = 6\sqrt{12/40\,000} = 0.10\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$
   (the standard formula for a straight line through $N$ points). The
   demonstration's synthetic series, which includes an $8\ \mathrm{mm}$ annual
   term, recovers the velocity with a bias of only $+0.048\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$
   once that term is fitted -- so the measurement is at the $0.05\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$
   level, i.e. $0.04\%$ of the $120\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ signal.
3. **Realistic limit.** The centre-of-mass offset of the target is modelled to
   $\sim2\ \mathrm{mm}$, and that constant range bias shifts the station radially
   by the same amount — irrelevant for the *rate* in this example, but fatal for
   the *scale* (Section 3.3).
4. **Frame.** The velocity is radial in a stated frame; a plate-motion interpretation
   requires the frame to be realised by a global solution, and the difference
   between a solution in two frame realisations is the magnitude of the frame
   error, not a physical signal.
5. **Interpretation.** The measurement is a decadal radial velocity to a fraction
   of a millimetre per year, provided three non-instrumental things are stated:
   the frame, the target's centre-of-mass model, and the noise model.

## 5. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Known $c$** and a round-trip path; the atmospheric delay must be modelled
   (the delay is several metres at zenith and larger at low elevation).
2. **Detected photons** for laser ranging: low-elevation shots are noisier and the
   return rate falls off steeply, so the normal-point noise is
   elevation-dependent.
3. **A stable, well-modelled target.** Centre-of-mass offsets, array tilt and
   satellite rotation are systematic, not random.
4. **A point source** for VLBI: structure in the source adds delay and biases the
   frame if unmodelled, which is why catalogue sources are selected for
   compactness.
5. **A satisfied geostationary assumption** for DORIS beacons: station coordinates
   are *assumed* known in the standard DORIS processing for orbit determination,
   and estimated in the geodetic mode — two different products from the same data.
6. **Stated Earth-orientation treatment** (Section 3.6).
7. **Synthetic teaching values throughout.** Baselines, noise levels, series and
   anomalies are chosen for arithmetic clarity.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Quoting a laser range without the atmospheric delay** or the centre-of-mass
   correction.
2. **Assuming averaging removes systematics.** It removes only the random part.
3. **Estimating a global scale from radial data.** Section 3.3 measures the
   degeneracy.
4. **Treating a Doppler curve as a single measurement.** The geometry is in its
   shape.
5. **Forgetting the source structure in VLBI.**
6. **Comparing solutions in different Earth-orientation treatments.**
7. **Believing any single technique defines a frame.** The frame is the
   intersection of the four.
