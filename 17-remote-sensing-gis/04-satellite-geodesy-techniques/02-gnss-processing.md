# GNSS Processing: Networks, PPP and Error Sources

> All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**. The stations, clocks,
> atmospheres, noise realisations and time series in this lesson are teaching data
> generated by the demonstration. The physical constants are stated standards. No
> real network, station, product or observation file is used.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Derive** single and double differences and **demonstrate** which error terms
   cancel and which do not.
2. **Integrate** a refractivity profile to obtain the zenith hydrostatic and wet
   delays, and **compare** the plane-parallel and curved-Earth mapping functions.
3. **Convert** a zenith wet-delay error into a height error and **state** why the
   vertical coordinate is the noisiest.
4. **Explain** why integer ambiguity resolution improves a solution only while the
   fix is correct, and **quantify** the failure mode.
5. **Estimate** a velocity from a daily position time series and **measure** the
   bias introduced by omitting a seasonal term.
6. **Quantify** how correlated (coloured) noise inflates the velocity uncertainty
   beyond the white-noise prediction.
7. **Explain** how a reference-frame error propagates into every station's
   coordinates while leaving the relative solution untouched.

## 1. Intuition: differences remove what is common

The observation equation of [lesson 01](01-gnss-principles.md) carries a large
catalogue of errors, several of which are *common* to two nearby stations or to
one station observed twice. Differencing is the systematic exploitation of that
commonality: subtract two satellites seen by one receiver and the receiver clock
disappears; subtract two receivers seeing the same satellite and the satellite
clock disappears; do both and, to the extent that the paths are close, the
atmosphere largely disappears too. The residual — geometry plus differential
atmosphere plus noise — is what centimetre positioning is made of.

The alternative strategy, **precise point positioning (PPP)**, refuses the
differences and instead models everything: precise orbits and clocks, a
troposphere model with an estimated residual, and the ionosphere-free combination.
It trades the cancellation of differencing for the independence of a single
receiver. Both strategies are covered here because the choice between them is a
choice about *which errors you are willing to model*, and that is the recurring
theme of this module.

## 2. Definitions

| symbol | meaning | unit |
|---|---|---|
| $\Phi$ | carrier phase | cycles |
| $N$ | integer ambiguity | cycles |
| $m(z)$ | mapping function, slant $=$ zenith $\times\,m$ | — |
| $ZHD$, $ZWD$ | zenith hydrostatic, wet delay | m |
| $\sigma_v$ | uncertainty of a fitted velocity | mm/yr |

A **single difference** is between two receivers for one satellite (or two
satellites for one receiver); a **double difference** is between two receivers and
two satellites.

## 3. Derivations

### 3.1 What differencing removes

With $P_A^k$ the pseudorange from receiver $A$ to satellite $k$,

$$\Delta P = P_A^k - P_B^k
= \left(\rho_A^k-\rho_B^k\right) + c\left(\delta t_A-\delta t_B\right)
+ \left(I_A^k - I_B^k\right) + \left(T_A^k-T_B^k\right) + \text{noise},$$

so the **satellite** clock cancels exactly because both receivers see the same
satellite. One more difference, against a reference satellite,

$$\nabla\Delta P = \Delta P^k - \Delta P^{0},$$

removes the **receiver** clock as well. *Numerical check (SYNTHETIC),* from the
demonstration, with a $100\ \mathrm{km}$ baseline, receiver clock offsets of
$+45.0$ and $-24.0\ \mathrm{km}$ and satellite clocks of $31\,023\ \mathrm{m}$ rms:

| observation | largest surviving term |
|---|---|
| single difference | common offset $68\,952.464$ m $= c(\delta t_A-\delta t_B)$, spread 0.700 m |
| double difference | residuals $-0.010$, $+1.181$, $+1.559$ m about the reference satellite |

The spread about the mean in the double difference (0.700 m) is consistent with
$\sqrt{2}\times0.424 = 0.600\ \mathrm{m}$ for the noise of a difference of two
single differences, given the tiny sample of four satellites. **The check is that
the residual is metres, not kilometres**: both clocks are gone. That is the entire
argument for relative positioning.

### 3.2 The troposphere and its mapping function

The slant delay is the refractivity integrated along the ray. For the zenith
direction,

$$L_{\text{zenith}} = 10^{-6}\int N(z)\,\mathrm{d}z ,$$

split conventionally into a hydrostatic and a wet part. *Numerical check
(SYNTHETIC),* with $N = 315\exp(-h/8\,\mathrm{km}) + 40\exp(-h/2\,\mathrm{km})$
ppm:

$$ZHD = 2.5186\ \mathrm{m}, \qquad ZWD = 0.0800\ \mathrm{m}, \qquad
\text{total} = 2.5986\ \mathrm{m}.$$

Mapping to a slant direction is where the approximations begin. Two forms,

$$m_{\text{flat}}(z) = \frac{1}{\cos z}, \qquad
m_{\text{curved}}(z) = \left[1-\left(\frac{R}{R+H}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}z\right]^{-1/2},$$

with $H$ the effective layer height, compared in the demonstration:

| $z$ | $1/\cos z$ | curved | flat overestimate |
|---|---|---|---|
| $30^\circ$ | 1.1547 | 1.1542 | $+0.04\%$ |
| $60^\circ$ | 2.0000 | 1.9925 | $+0.38\%$ |
| $75^\circ$ | 3.8637 | 3.7980 | $+1.73\%$ |
| $85^\circ$ | 11.4737 | 9.9584 | $+15.22\%$ |

For the $2.52\ \mathrm{m}$ zenith delay, the flat mapping overestimates the slant
delay by $3.816\ \mathrm{m}$ of range at $85^\circ$. The mapping function is a
model with its own error budget, and it is one of the reasons low-elevation
observations are down-weighted even though they are geometrically valuable.

### 3.3 Why height is the noisiest coordinate

Because the slant delay is the zenith delay times the mapping function, a zenith
error appears in the height solution multiplied by $m$:

| $z$ | $m_{\text{curved}}$ | height error from 1 cm of $ZWD$ |
|---|---|---|
| $0^\circ$ | 1.0000 | 1.000 cm |
| $30^\circ$ | 1.1542 | 1.154 cm |
| $60^\circ$ | 1.9925 | 1.993 cm |
| $75^\circ$ | 3.7980 | 3.798 cm |
| $85^\circ$ | 9.9584 | 9.958 cm |

A centimetre of wet-delay error is a centimetre of height at zenith and
$9.96\ \mathrm{cm}$ at $85^\circ$. Since $ZWD$ itself varies by centimetres
between stations, the wet delay dominates the height error budget — the
quantitative companion to the geometric VDOP argument of
[lesson 01](01-gnss-principles.md) §3.3.

### 3.4 Integer ambiguity resolution

Carrier phase gives a range good to a millimetre but known only up to $N\lambda$.
Resolving $N$ to integers converts a precise but ambiguous measurement into a
precise and unambiguous one. *Numerical check (SYNTHETIC),* with
$\lambda = 0.190294\ \mathrm{m}$ and the float ambiguity having a stated standard
deviation:

| $\sigma$ of the float ambiguity | wrong-fix rate | rms range error, fixed | float |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05 cycle | 0.0000 | 0.947 cm | 0.947 cm |
| 0.10 cycle | 0.0000 | 1.885 cm | 1.885 cm |
| 0.20 cycle | 0.0115 | **4.796 cm** | 3.762 cm |
| 0.40 cycle | 0.2167 | **15.712 cm** | 7.675 cm |

Read the last two rows together. At $\sigma = 0.20$ cycles only $1.15\%$ of fixes
are wrong, and yet the *fixed* solution is **worse** than the float one
(4.796 against 3.762 cm), because each wrong fix displaces the range by a whole
wavelength ($19\ \mathrm{cm}$) and the rms is dominated by those outliers. Fixing
is not automatically an improvement; it is an improvement *conditional on the fix
being right*, which is why a fix is accepted only after validation
(ratio or difference tests) and why the validation statistic — not the post-fit
residual — is the number to report.

### 3.5 Velocity from a daily time series

A station's coordinates over time give its velocity, usually fitted as a linear
trend with seasonal terms and jumps. *Numerical check (SYNTHETIC),* truth
$15.0\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ plus a $4.0\ \mathrm{mm}$ annual term, $1000$ daily
solutions with $1.0\ \mathrm{mm}$ of position noise:

| record | model | bias (mm/yr) | scatter (mm/yr) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.0 yr | linear only | $-0.814$ | 0.039 |
| 3.0 yr | linear + annual | $-0.002$ | 0.038 |
| 1.2 yr | linear only | $-2.343$ | 0.137 |
| 1.2 yr | linear + annual | $+0.007$ | 0.140 |

The bias from omitting the annual term ($-0.81\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ over three
years, $-2.34\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ over 1.2 years) is **twenty times the formal
scatter**. A comfortable-looking error bar is therefore no protection: the
uncertainty statement must include the model, not only the covariance. The
statistics of the fit are identical to the InSAR time series of
[17-03-03](../03-radar-remote-sensing/03-insar-time-series.md), because the
problem — a sinusoid projected onto a linear term — is the same.

### 3.6 Noise colour

Position time series are not white: they contain power-law (flicker and random
walk) components from multipath, monument motion and orbit and atmosphere
modelling errors. *Numerical check (SYNTHETIC),* the same fit with white and with
$1/f$ noise of equal variance:

| noise | velocity scatter (mm/yr) |
|---|---|
| white | 0.0355 |
| flicker | 0.2591 |

A factor of **7.3**. The effective number of independent observations is far
smaller than the number of days, so a white-noise error bar can be optimistic by
an order of magnitude. Any quoted velocity uncertainty must state the noise model
(simply: white, flicker, random walk, or an estimated combination), or it is not
reproducible. This is the same lesson as the correlated-pair argument in
[17-03-03](../03-radar-remote-sensing/03-insar-time-series.md) §3.3, in a different
guise.

### 3.7 The reference frame

A network solution is only defined relative to something. *Numerical check:* an
assumed-frame error of $1.00\ \mathrm{cm}$ shifts **every** station by $1.00\
\mathrm{cm}$, while the internal relative accuracy ($0.37\ \mathrm{cm}$ in the
demonstration) is unaffected. No residual of the adjustment reveals the common
shift, because it is a datum choice, not a misfit. Two consequences:

- "precise" and "accurate" are different claims, and only the first is testable
  from the data alone;
- comparisons between solutions in different frames produce differences as large
  as the frame differences, which is why reference-frame realisation (module
  [07-04](../../07-gravity-geodesy/04-geodesy-reference-frames/README.md)) is part
  of the measurement and not an afterthought.

## 4. Worked example — a height error budget

*All values SYNTHETIC.* A station is observed at elevations from $15^\circ$ to
$85^\circ$ over 6 hours. Range noise $0.5\ \mathrm{m}$ on code, $5\ \mathrm{mm}$ on
phase; VDOP $= 2.2$; zenith wet delay known only to $1.5\ \mathrm{cm}$.

1. **Geometry term.** $\text{VDOP}\times\sigma_{\text{phase}} = 2.2\times5\
\mathrm{mm} = 11\ \mathrm{mm}$.
2. **Wet-delay term.** At a mean elevation of $45^\circ$,
   $m = 1.4142$; the height error is $1.5\times1.4142 = 2.12\ \mathrm{cm}$.
3. **Mapping-model term.** If the flat mapping were used up to $85^\circ$,
   the overestimate at $85^\circ$ is $15.2\%$ of the slant delay; with one
   low-elevation observation among many the effect is diluted, but at
   $z = 75^\circ$ it is still $1.7\%$ of a $2.52\ \mathrm{m}$ delay, i.e.
   $4.3\ \mathrm{cm}$ of slant range.
4. **Total.** The wet delay dominates: $2.12\ \mathrm{cm}$ against $1.1\
\mathrm{cm}$ from geometry. Adding the mapping-model term in quadrature gives
   $2.5\ \mathrm{cm}$.
5. **Interpretation.** The honest height accuracy is set by the atmosphere, not by
   the electronics, and improving the receiver cannot change that. The remedies
   are an independent water-vapour measurement, a longer session so the
   troposphere is better estimated, and — decisively — a stated treatment of the
   wet delay in the report.

## 5. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Differential atmosphere is small** for short baselines; the assumption
   degrades roughly linearly with baseline, and it is the reason networks are
   denser for high accuracy.
2. **The satellite clock is exactly common** to both receivers — true except for
   tiny relativistic and hardware corrections that are supplied with the products.
3. **Integer ambiguity is constant** between cycle slips. An undetected slip is
   a whole-wavelength error, which is why slip detection runs continuously.
4. **The velocity model is adequate.** Section 3.5 measures the cost of omitting a
   seasonal term.
5. **Noise model stated.** Section 3.6 measures a factor of 7.3 between two
   defensible choices.
6. **The frame is stated and realised.** Section 3.7.
7. **Teaching values.** The network, weather, noise and time series are synthetic;
   the constants are standard.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Believing differencing removes the atmosphere.** It removes the *common*
   part only, and the residual grows with baseline.
2. **Using $1/\cos z$ at low elevation.** It overestimates by $15\%$ at $85^\circ$.
3. **Fixing ambiguities without validation.** A small wrong-fix rate can degrade
   the solution.
4. **Quoting a velocity with a white-noise error bar.** Correlated noise inflates
   it by factors of several.
5. **Omitting a seasonal term** from a rate fit whose record is a couple of years.
6. **Comparing solutions in different frames.**
7. **Reporting a formal uncertainty as an accuracy.** Only repeatability and
   external comparison test accuracy.
