# Satellite Gravimetry as an Observation System

> All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**. The orbits, mass
> anomalies, error spectrum and noise in this lesson are teaching values
> generated by the demonstration. The constants ($GM$, the Earth radius, the
> rotation rate, $J_2$, $G$) are standard values with units. No real mission,
> satellite, product or gravity field is used. The inversion of these
> observations into a spherical-harmonic model is the subject of module
> [07-05](../../07-gravity-geodesy/05-satellite-gravimetry/README.md); this
> lesson is about the *observation system*.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Derive** the orbital period and speed from $GM$ and the radius, and
   **explain** the trade between a low orbit and a long lifetime.
2. **Compute** the $J_2$ nodal precession rate and **show** that it couples the
   inclination to the altitude for a sun-synchronous orbit.
3. **Compute** repeat-cycle ground-track spacing and **state** why the repeat
   cycle is a scientific choice.
4. **Derive** the attenuation of a compact mass anomaly with altitude and **state**
   which depths are best observed from orbit.
5. **Quantify** the non-gravitational acceleration budget and **explain** why the
   accelerometer and the orbit determination are part of the gravity measurement.
6. **Compute** the aliasing of a periodic mass signal by the repeat cycle, and
   **explain** why a coherent alias cannot be averaged away.
7. **Identify** the degree at which a signal sinks below the noise, and **state**
   what the reported solution above that degree represents.

## 1. Intuition: this is a *difference* measurement

A satellite cannot weigh the Earth. It falls, and what it measures is a
*difference*: between the orbits of two spacecraft, or between the motions of
proof masses carried together. The gravity field is then whatever explains the
difference. Two consequences follow immediately and they organise this lesson.

First, the **signal is weak**: the accelerations of interest are $10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$
or smaller, against a drag acceleration that is hundreds of times larger at
$250\ \mathrm{km}$. Every non-gravitational force must therefore be measured, not
modelled — which is why an accelerometer is part of the instrument, and why the
orbit determination is part of the measurement rather than a preprocessing step.

Second, the **sampling is geometric**: the satellite sweeps the sphere in about
an hour and a half, but returns to the same ground track only after a repeat
cycle of days. Temporal signals are therefore aliased in exactly the way the tides
are aliased in radar altimetry
([17-03-07](../03-radar-remote-sensing/07-radar-altimetry.md)), and the resolution
of the recovered field is bounded by the altitude through downward continuation —
the same mathematics as the gravity-field continuation of module 07-05.

| symbol | meaning | unit |

| $n$ | mean motion, $n=\sqrt{GM/r^{3}}$ | rad/s |
| $J_2$ | zonal coefficient of the gravity field | — |
| $\dot\Omega$ | nodal precession rate | rad/s |
| $M$, $d$ | mass and depth of a compact anomaly | kg, m |
| $\sigma_n$ | measurement noise at degree $n$ | m/s² |

## 3. Derivations

### 3.1 Orbit geometry: what the altitude buys and costs

For a circular orbit,
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^{3}}{GM}},
\qquad n = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r^{3}}},$$
with $GM = 3.986\,004\,418\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^{3}\,s^{-2}}$ and
$R_E = 6371\ \mathrm{km}$. *Numerical check (SYNTHETIC orbits):*

| altitude | radius | speed | period | period (days) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 200 km | 6571 km | 7788.5 m/s | 5301.0 s | 0.0614 |
| 250 km | 6621 km | 7759.0 m/s | 5361.6 s | 0.0621 |
| 400 km | 6771 km | 7672.6 m/s | 5544.9 s | 0.0642 |
| 700 km | 7071 km | 7508.1 m/s | 5917.4 s | 0.0685 |

A lower orbit is faster and repeats more often (better temporal sampling and less
downward-continuation attenuation), but it decays faster and sits deeper in the
atmosphere (larger drag, shorter mission). The choice of altitude is therefore a
choice between sampling and lifetime, and it is made with the science target in
mind, not for resolution alone.

### 3.2 Nodal precession couples inclination and altitude

The oblateness of the Earth precesses the orbit plane:

$$\dot\Omega = -\tfrac{3}{2}J_2\,n\left(\frac{R_E}{a}\right)^{2}
\frac{\cos i}{(1-e^{2})^{2}},$$

with $J_2 = 1.082\,626\,68\times10^{-3}$ and, for a circular orbit, $e = 0$.
*Numerical check.* At $250\ \mathrm{km}$, $n = 1.171\,88\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$;
at $i = 98^\circ$ the demonstration computes

$$\dot\Omega = 2.4523\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}
= 1.214\ \mathrm{deg\,day^{-1}},$$

whereas a sun-synchronous orbit requires the plane to follow the Sun, i.e.
$360^\circ/365.25\ \mathrm{d} = 0.9856\ \mathrm{deg\,day^{-1}}$. The chosen
inclination over-precesses, which shows the point: the sun-synchronous condition
$\dot\Omega(i, a) = 0.9856\ \mathrm{deg\,day^{-1}}$ **fixes** $i$ once $a$ is
chosen. Altitude and inclination are not independent design variables; a mission
cannot tune them separately, and a change of orbit changes the illumination and
the sampling at the same time.

### 3.3 Repeat cycles and ground-track spacing

A repeat orbit returns to the same ground track after $N$ revolutions (or after a
chosen number of days), giving an equatorial spacing

$$\Delta x = \frac{2\pi R_E}{N}.$$

*Numerical check:*

| revolutions per repeat | repeat cycle | equatorial spacing |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.6206 d | 4003.0 km |
| 15 | 0.9308 d | 2668.7 km |
| 16 | 0.9929 d | 2501.9 km |
| 30 | 1.8617 d | 1334.3 km |

The repeat cycle is a *choice with scientific content*: it sets how often a given
location is revisited (hence which time-variable signals can be separated) and how
densely the tracks are spaced (hence the along-track sampling of the static
field). A non-repeating orbit gives denser spatial coverage but destroys the
regular temporal sampling that a time-variable gravity solution needs.

### 3.4 How much of an anomaly survives at altitude

For a compact anomaly of mass $M$ at depth $d$, the acceleration at altitude $h$ is
$G M/(d+h)^{2}$, so relative to the surface value the attenuation is

$$\frac{g(h)}{g(0)} = \left(\frac{d}{d+h}\right)^{2}.$$

*Numerical check:* with $h$ in kilometres,

| depth $d$ | $h = 200$ km | $h = 250$ km | $h = 500$ km | $h = 700$ km |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 km | 0.00059 | 0.00038 | 0.00010 | 0.00005 |
| 20 km | 0.00826 | 0.00549 | 0.00148 | 0.00077 |
| 100 km | 0.11111 | 0.08163 | 0.02778 | 0.01562 |

A shallow anomaly is attenuated brutally (a factor $10^{4}$ for a 5 km source
seen from 250 km), while a deep one loses much less: **the orbit is a
deep-source instrument**. This is the complementarity that makes satellite
gravimetry and terrestrial gravimetry different measurements rather than better
and worse versions of each other. It also gives the sensitivity scale: to produce
$1\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ (about 1 µGal) at $250\ \mathrm{km}$ from a
surface mass,

$$M = \frac{1\times10^{-8}\times(2.5\times10^{5})^{2}}{6.674\,30\times10^{-11}}
= 9.364\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{kg},$$

an ice body a few hundred metres across. That is the sensitivity the whole
observation system must deliver.

### 3.5 The non-gravitational acceleration budget

A *synthetic* budget at $250\ \mathrm{km}$ (teaching values, but of realistic
magnitude):

| source | acceleration |
|---|---|
| atmospheric drag | $3.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ |
| solar radiation pressure | $1.2\times10^{-7}$ |
| Earth radiation pressure | $2.0\times10^{-8}$ |
| thruster and outgassing | $5.0\times10^{-9}$ |
| tidal and relativistic corrections | $1.0\times10^{-10}$ |

Drag exceeds the signal by two orders of magnitude and is unpredictable at the
required level, so no model of the thermosphere suffices: an **accelerometer is
part of the instrument**. The alternative design flies high enough that drag falls
to the level of the radiometer error, which sacrifices sensitivity to the
low-degree field. Both designs exist because the trade is real, and the choice is
scientific rather than technical.

### 3.6 Aliasing by the repeat cycle

The satellite sees the whole sphere every 1.49 h at this altitude, so nothing
geophysical is undersampled *in time*. What matters is that a **fixed ground
location** is observed once per repeat cycle, so a signal of frequency $f$ appears
at $|f - n f_s|$ with $f_s = 1/T_r$. *Numerical check,* for the M2 tide
($12.4206012\ \mathrm{h}$):

| repeat cycle | sample rate | M2 alias period |
|---|---|---|
| 0.62 d | 0.067204 h⁻¹ | 3.1312 d |
| 0.99 d | 0.042088 h⁻¹ | 11.3729 d |
| 1.86 d | 0.022401 h⁻¹ | 4.5816 d |
| 3.00 d | 0.013889 h⁻¹ | 14.7653 d |
| 10.00 d | 0.004167 h⁻¹ | 30.9851 d |

A tide appearing as an 11-day variation is coherent with the sampling: it does not
average away, and it must be modelled with the same geometry or placed where the
science target does not live. The repeat cycle therefore decides which
time-variable signals are separable — the same design constraint as the
altimeter's, and the reason gravity missions choose unusual repeat cycles.

### 3.7 Where the signal sinks below the noise

The field of degree $n$ is attenuated from the surface by $(R_E/(R_E+h))^{n}$.
Comparing a *synthetic* teaching signal spectrum $\sigma_n = A/n^{2}$ with
$A = 10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ against a *synthetic* noise of
$10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ per degree:

| altitude | highest degree above noise | spatial scale |
|---|---|---|
| 200 km | 22 | 910 km |
| 250 km | 21 | 953 km |
| 400 km | 18 | 1112 km |
| 700 km | 14 | 1430 km |

The **crossover degree** — where the attenuated signal equals the noise — is what
the mission can deliver. Above it, the reported coefficients are the analyst's
regularisation rather than data, and saying so is part of reporting the model.
Lower orbits reach higher degrees (less attenuation per degree) but have larger
non-gravitational accelerations and shorter lifetimes; the numbers above are a
teaching illustration of that competition, and the true spectrum is steeper than
$n^{-2}$, which lowers every entry. The full inversion, including the covariance
and the regularisation, is module 07-05.

## 4. Worked example — sizing an observation system

*All values SYNTHETIC.* A mission must detect a $5\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$
signal at degree 60 from $250\ \mathrm{km}$.

1. **Space scale.** $\pi R_E/n = \pi\times6371/60 = 334$ km half-wavelength.
2. **Attenuation.** $(R_E/(R_E+250))^{60} = (6371/6621)^{60}$. The ratio is
   $0.962238$; raising to the 60th power gives
   $e^{60\ln 0.962238} = e^{-2.3097} = 0.09925$. So about $10\%$ of the degree-60
   field survives to orbit.
3. **Required precision.** $5\times10^{-8}\times0.09925 = 4.96\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$
   must be resolved, i.e. about $0.5$ µGal — three orders of magnitude below the
   drag acceleration of Section 3.5.
4. **Consequence.** Drag must be removed to $10^{-3}$ of its own size, which no
   model achieves: the accelerometer is mandatory, and its calibration error
   becomes a systematic error in the gravity field at the same degree.
5. **Sampling.** The repeat cycle must place the aliases of the seasonal and tidal
   signals of interest where the target is not (Section 3.6), which constrains the
   orbit design independently of the instrument.
6. **Interpretation.** The numbers above are the reason satellite gravimetry is
   an *instrument* problem before it is a modelling problem: each step from the
   signal to the answer requires an error term three orders of magnitude smaller
   than the largest force acting on the spacecraft.

## 5. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Keplerian motion plus small perturbations.** The demonstration's orbit
   formulas are the two-body ones plus $J_2$; higher harmonics and third-body
   effects are perturbations with their own budgets.
2. **Circular orbit** ($e = 0$) for the precession expression used here.
3. **A point-mass anomaly** for the attenuation estimate; an extended source
   attenuates less steeply and its shape is not recoverable from the ratio alone.
4. **The drag budget is synthetic.** The magnitudes are realistic, but they are
   teaching values; the lesson's *method* — measure, don't hope — is the content.
5. **The signal and noise spectra in Section 3.7 are synthetic teaching models.**
   They illustrate the crossover concept; they are not any real mission's error
   curve.
6. **A repeat orbit** for the aliasing analysis; a drifting orbit has a
   time-varying alias structure.
7. **The inversion is out of scope here.** This lesson ends at the observation;
   the spherical-harmonic solution, its covariance and its regularisation are
   module 07-05.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Treating the orbit as a free resolution knob.** Altitude trades against
   lifetime, drag and aliasing simultaneously.
2. **Confusing the repeat cycle with the orbital period** when discussing
   sampling.
3. **Assuming the inversion is the hard part.** The observation system's error
   budget is harder.
4. **Quoting coefficients above the crossover degree as data.**
5. **Forgetting that the measurement is a difference.** A common-mode error
   cancels; a differential one does not.
6. **Comparing satellite and terrestrial gravity without the depth filter** of
   Section 3.4.
7. **Ignoring the aliasing design constraint** until the time series is
   contaminated.
