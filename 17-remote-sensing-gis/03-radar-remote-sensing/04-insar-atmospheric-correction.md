# Atmospheric and Orbital Error Correction in InSAR

> All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**. The atmosphere here is an
> exponential teaching profile, and the screens, ramps, scene and noise are
> generated by the demonstration. No weather model product, orbit product or
> measured delay is used anywhere.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Compute** the zenith path delay of a given refractivity profile by
   integration, and **state** the size of the hydrostatic delay at sea level.
2. **Convert** a differential path delay into phase, fringes and apparent
   line-of-sight displacement, and **explain** why a delay and a displacement are
   indistinguishable in a single interferogram.
3. **Model** the stratified component as a function of elevation, **estimate** it
   by regression, and **measure** what the regression leaves behind.
4. **Measure** the structure function of a turbulent screen, **state** the
   scaling law it should follow, and **explain** why a fitted exponent is
   meaningless without the separations and the field extent that produced it.
5. **Estimate** an orbital ramp from a justified quiet-pixel mask and **quantify**
   how much of the deformation signal such a fit absorbs.
6. **Predict** whether stacking reduces an atmospheric error, and **explain** the
   role of temporal correlation between acquisitions.
7. **Quantify** the bias that one uncorrected epoch introduces into a rate.

## 1. Intuition: the largest signal in the interferogram is not the Earth

A two-way path through the atmosphere is longer than a path through vacuum by
about two and a half metres at sea level. Two repeat-pass acquisitions differ in
pressure, temperature and water vapour, so their excess paths differ by a few
millimetres to a few centimetres. Since one fringe corresponds to $2.83\
\mathrm{cm}$ of range change at this wavelength, that difference appears as a
displacement of up to a fringe — often larger than the deformation being
measured, and always shaped by the weather rather than by the Earth.

The lesson treats three error sources in the order of their size and their
character: the **stratified** delay (smooth, correlated with topography), the
**turbulent** delay (random, correlated over a few kilometres) and the
**orbital** ramp (a low-order polynomial across the whole scene, from errors in
the baseline and the orbit solution). The emphasis is on what each correction can
and cannot remove, measured rather than asserted — because a correction that
removes part of the signal is worse than none.

## 2. Definitions

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| $N$ | refractivity, $N = 10^{6}(n-1)$ | ppm |
| $L$ | excess one-way path, $\int N\,10^{-6}\,\mathrm{d}z$ | m |
| $\Delta L$ | differential excess path between two acquisitions | m |
| $z$ | zenith angle of the look direction | rad |
| $n_x$ | number of pixels along a profile in the demonstration | — |

The interferometric phase produced by a differential path delay is

$$\Delta\phi = \frac{4\pi}{\lambda}\,\Delta L,$$

with the same $4\pi$ as for a range change: the radar cannot tell a longer path
from a farther target. This identity is the whole problem of the lesson.

## 3. Derivations

### 3.1 The delay integral

The excess path along the line of sight is the refractivity integrated along the
ray, $L = 10^{-6}\int N\,\mathrm{d}s$. For an exponential profile
$N(z) = N_s\exp(-z/H)$ viewed at zenith,

$$L_{\text{zenith}} = 10^{-6}\int_0^\infty N_s e^{-z/H}\,\mathrm{d}z
= 10^{-6}N_s H.$$

*Numerical check (SYNTHETIC).* With $N_s = 315\ \mathrm{ppm}$ and
$H = 8.0\ \mathrm{km}$ the demonstration integrates numerically to
$L_{\text{zenith}} = 2.5030\ \mathrm{m}$ over a 40 km column, against the
analytic $10^{-6}\times315\times8000 = 2.5200\ \mathrm{m}$ for the full column.
The $0.017\ \mathrm{m}$ difference is the mass of atmosphere above 40 km, and
the convergence of the two numbers is the check.

Off zenith the path lengthens. In the plane-parallel approximation
$L(z) = L_{\text{zenith}}/\cos z$, giving $3.0556\ \mathrm{m}$ at
$z = 35^\circ$. The real atmosphere curves less than the plane-parallel formula
predicts at large $z$, so a **mapping function** is used in practice; the
demonstration uses the planar form and states it.

### 3.2 Delay to apparent displacement

With $\lambda = 5.6565\ \mathrm{cm}$,

| $\Delta L$ | $\Delta\phi$ (rad) | fringes | apparent LOS displacement |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 mm | 0.2222 | 0.0354 | 0.100 cm |
| 5 mm | 1.1108 | 0.1768 | 0.500 cm |
| 10 mm | 2.2216 | 0.3536 | 1.000 cm |
| 20 mm | 4.4432 | 0.7072 | 2.000 cm |

The conversion is one-to-one: **1 cm of differential delay is 1 cm of apparent
line-of-sight displacement.** There is no interferometric operation that
distinguishes them at a point. Separation requires information from outside the
pair: a second geometry, a time series, or an independent estimate of the delay.

### 3.3 The stratified component

Pressure falls with height, so the dry delay falls with height, so the delay is a
smooth function of topography:

$$\Delta L(\text{topography}) = c_1 h + c_2 h^{2} + \cdots .$$

The demonstration uses $c_1 = 3.00\ \mathrm{mm\,km^{-1}}$ and a curvature term,
$c_2 = -0.25\ \mathrm{mm\,km^{-2}}$:

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| DEM range across the profile | 228 – 816 m |
| screen range | 0.65 – 1.95 mm |
| induced apparent displacement, peak to peak | 0.130 cm |
| linear regression slope recovered | 2.3077 mm/km |
| residual rms before correction | 0.3237 mm |
| residual rms after linear correction | 0.0150 mm |

Two honest observations. The recovered slope (2.3077) is **not** the injected one
(3.00) because the curvature term biases a straight-line fit over a
$0.6\ \mathrm{km}$ height range — the fit is absorbing the quadratic term into
its slope. And the residual after the fit is not zero: a linear correction
removes what is linear, and the remainder is a model error that no amount of
extra data will remove, because it is a property of the adopted functional form.
The remedy is either a physically better relation (the delay is nearly linear in
surface pressure) or an independent measurement of the delay, never more pixels.

### 3.4 The turbulent component

Water vapour varies on scales of a kilometre and shorter, producing a random
screen. Its natural description is the second-order structure function

$$D(r) = \left\langle \left[x(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}_0)
- x(\mathbf{r}_0)\right]^{2} \right\rangle ,$$

since a mean delay is unobservable in a difference. For a screen whose
one-dimensional spectral density goes as $S(k)\propto k^{-\beta}$ over a band,

$$D(r)\propto r^{\beta-1} \ \ (\beta<3),
\qquad D(r)\propto r^{2} \ \ (\beta\ge 3),$$

the second being the smooth (Taylor) limit where the field is differentiable.
The demonstration generates band-limited screens and measures the exponent:

| $\beta$ | measured exponent, $r=25$–$400$ | $\min(\beta-1,2)$ | exponent at $r=800$–$4000$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.67 | 0.710 | 0.67 | $+0.529$ |
| 2.00 | 1.015 | 1.00 | $+0.503$ |
| 3.67 | 1.930 | 2.00 | $+1.277$ |
| 4.00 | 1.962 | 2.00 | $+1.244$ |

The scaling range exists only where $k_{\min}r \ll 1$ and $k_{\max}r \gg 1$;
above the largest scale in the screen the exponent collapses toward zero because
$D$ saturates at twice the field variance. The demonstration needed a field of
$131\,072$ samples spanning a $500$-fold band of wavenumbers to recover the
predicted exponents to $0.06$.

Two conclusions that matter downstream. First, $\beta = 5/3$ — the
one-dimensional spectrum of a fully developed turbulent screen — is the case with
a genuine power law, while $\beta>3$ gives a smooth $r^{2}$ screen with no
turbulent exponent at all in the measured range. Second, a fitted exponent must
be reported with its range of separations, its saturation check and the extent of
the data: a scene only a few times larger than its smallest structure cannot
measure $\beta$, and an exponent quoted without these is not a measurement.

### 3.5 The orbital ramp

Errors in the orbit and the baseline add a phase whose leading terms are linear
and quadratic in range and azimuth. Empirically it is removed by fitting a
low-order polynomial in the **quiet** part of the scene. The demonstration builds
a quadratic ramp ($8.382\ \mathrm{cm}$ span) and a $1.799\ \mathrm{cm}$ deformation
signal on a $3.81\ \mathrm{km}$ profile, then compares corrections:

| correction | residual rms (cm) | recovered signal peak (cm) |
|---|---|---|
| none | 2.5062 | 1.2713 |
| linear, quiet pixels ($48/128$) | 0.4530 | 0.9350 |
| quadratic, quiet pixels | 0.6503 | **1.7988** |
| quadratic, all pixels | 0.4392 | 1.0819 |

The signal to recover is $1.799\ \mathrm{cm}$. A quadratic fitted to the quiet
pixels recovers it essentially exactly; the same quadratic fitted to *all* pixels
absorbs $40\%$ of it, and a linear correction leaves a $0.45\ \mathrm{cm}$ residual
of the same order as the signal itself. The residual rms is not the right
criterion here — the "quadratic, all pixels" row has the smallest residual rms and
the worst answer. **The mask, not the polynomial order, decides whether the
correction is honest**, and the mask has to be justified by showing that the
signal does not extend into it.

### 3.6 What stacking does, and does not, average away

Consider $N$ acquisitions each carrying a screen $s_i$. A pair $(i,j)$ is biased
by $s_i - s_j$, so a screen common to all acquisitions cancels in every
difference, while a screen that persists over part of the record appears in every
difference that straddles its lifetime. Writing a fraction $\rho$ of the screen
variance as common to all epochs, the demonstration measures the residual of the
epoch mean:

| common fraction $\rho$ | residual of the epoch mean (mm) |
|---|---|
| 0.0 | 1.858 |
| 0.3 | 4.649 |
| 0.6 | 6.447 |
| 0.9 | 7.368 |

With independent screens the mean has standard error
$\sigma/\sqrt{N} = 10/\sqrt{20} = 2.236\ \mathrm{mm}$, and the measured 1.858 mm is
consistent with it. As the screens become correlated across epochs the mean stops
shrinking: at $\rho = 0.9$ it is 7.368 mm, four times worse. **Stacking reduces
*independent* screens; it cannot reduce a screen that is shared by the epochs it
stacks**, and the degree of sharing is a property of the weather, not of the
processing.

Finally, the cost of a single bad epoch. In a 40-epoch, 3-year record with a true
rate of $10.00\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$, one uncorrected $20\ \mathrm{mm}$ screen in
one epoch biases the fitted rate by

$$+0.0916\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}} \quad (+0.92\%),$$

which is fifty times smaller than the screen itself but still comparable to the
target accuracy of many studies. The bias scales with the screen and with the
epoch's leverage (its distance from the record centre), so per-epoch residual
screening belongs in every time-series chain.

## 4. Worked example — a delay budget for a 20 mm error

*All values SYNTHETIC.* An interferogram over a 1.5 km relief has a suspected
$20\ \mathrm{mm}$ differential delay.

1. **Phase.** $\Delta\phi = 4\pi\times0.020/0.056565 = 4.4432\ \mathrm{rad}$.
2. **Fringes.** $4.4432/2\pi = 0.7072$ fringe.
3. **Apparent displacement.** $20\ \mathrm{mm}$ along the line of sight. If the
   motion were assumed vertical, $d_v = 0.020/\cos35^\circ = 24.4\ \mathrm{mm}$;
   the assumed direction inflates the error by $22\%$.
4. **Stratified hypothesis.** With the demonstration's relation, a $20\ \mathrm{mm}$
   screen would require a height range of about
   $20/3.0 = 6.7\ \mathrm{km}$ if the coefficient were $3.0\ \mathrm{mm\,km^{-1}}$
   — larger than the scene, so the stratified term cannot explain it.
5. **Turbulent hypothesis.** The screen would have to vary by $20\ \mathrm{mm}$
   across the scene. With a structure function exponent of $0.71$ (the
   $\beta = 5/3$ screen) and a $1\ \mathrm{mm}$ difference over $1\ \mathrm{km}$,
   $D(r)$ grows as $r^{0.71}$, so a $20\ \mathrm{mm}$ difference needs
   $(20)^{1/0.71} = 68$ times the $1\ \mathrm{km}$ scale — i.e. tens of
   kilometres, larger than the scene. The turbulent term is therefore also
   implausible *across this scene*, although a single screen of $20\ \mathrm{mm}$
   amplitude is entirely plausible.
6. **Conclusion.** The most likely explanation is a screen that is nearly uniform
   across the scene (a stratified or common-mode delay), which is exactly the
   component that stacking removes. The honest next step is a stack, not a
   spatial correction.

This example shows the value of the sizes: even without data, the magnitude and
the spatial structure of each candidate can be checked against the scene scale.

## 5. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Plane-parallel mapping** $1/\cos z$ for the delay. It fails at large zenith
   angles; a mapping function with the Earth's curvature is required beyond about
   $70^\circ$-equivalent viewing geometry.
2. **The stratified relation is a function of elevation alone.** It ignores
   lateral pressure gradients, which are real and are the reason an external
   field usually beats a regression.
3. **The quiet pixels are quiet.** Section 3.5 shows how much signal a wrong mask
   absorbs; a mask must be justified quantitatively, usually by showing the
   signal does not extend into it in other epochs.
4. **The ramp is a low-order polynomial.** A step or a curved orbit error beyond
   the fitted order leaks into the signal.
5. **Screens are stationary** over the scene for the structure-function
   interpretation to apply; a front crossing the scene violates it.
6. **The demonstration's numbers are teaching values.** The coefficient
   $3.0\ \mathrm{mm\,km^{-1}}$, the $N_s = 315\ \mathrm{ppm}$ profile, the screen
   amplitudes and the ramp span are chosen so the arithmetic can be followed;
   they are not measured atmospheric statistics.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Treating a delay as a displacement.** In one pair they are the same number.
2. **Reporting a regression slope as the atmospheric coefficient.** Curvature and
   a limited height range bias it, as the demonstration's 2.3077 against 3.00 mm/km
   shows.
3. **Judging a correction by its residual rms.** The row with the smallest
   residual rms had the worst signal recovery.
4. **Fitting a ramp through the signal.** The signal is then partly inside the
   model.
5. **Assuming stacking removes the atmosphere.** It removes the independent part
   only.
6. **Quoting a turbulent exponent without its separation range.** The saturation
   scale and the field extent set what can be measured.
7. **Ignoring one bad epoch.** A single 20 mm screen moved a 3-year rate by
   0.09 mm/yr.
