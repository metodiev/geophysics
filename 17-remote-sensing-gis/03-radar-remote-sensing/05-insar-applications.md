# InSAR for Earthquakes, Volcanoes, Subsidence and Landslides

> All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**. The faults, slips,
> depths, source volumes, subsidence rates and landslide geometries here are
> teaching values generated by the demonstration. No real earthquake, volcano,
> subsidence field or landslide is modelled, and no measured displacement is
> quoted.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **State** the antiplane strike-slip model, **verify** its consequences
   numerically (far-field offset, peak strain, quarter-offset depth, linearity),
   and **identify** the assumptions that make it a model rather than a fact.
2. **Project** a three-component surface displacement onto the line of sight, and
   **quantify** the resulting insensitivity to north–south motion.
3. **Solve** the two-geometry decomposition for east and vertical motion, and
   **demonstrate** where a neglected north component goes.
4. **Compute** the per-pixel aliasing budget from the peak strain of a fault, and
   **decide** whether a given slip and locking depth can be unwrapped.
5. **Convert** a smooth uplift field into a model volume, and **state** why the
   volume is not a measurement but a consequence of the assumed source shape.
6. **Fit** a subsidence time series with a trend and a seasonal term, and
   **quantify** the bias from omitting the seasonal term.
7. **Decide**, for a landslide of a stated rate and width, whether a repeat-pass
   interferogram can be unwrapped at all.

## 1. Intuition: four targets, four different limits

The four application families in this lesson are distinguished not by their
physics but by **which limit binds**:

| target | signal size | binding limit |
|---|---|---|
| earthquake | decimetres to metres | the gradient: fringes crowd at the fault trace and alias |
| volcano | centimetres | the source trade-off: many sources fit one uplift field |
| subsidence | millimetres per year | the time series: one fringe is 2.83 cm, so the signal lives in the trend |
| landslide | centimetres to metres, fast | decorrelation and aliasing in the moving mass |

Reading the table the other way is the diagnostic skill: measure the signal size
and the spatial gradient, compare both with the ambiguity and the coherence, and
the applicable limit announces itself. Each subsection below does that
arithmetic with a synthetic example.

## 2. Definitions

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| $b$ | fault slip (offset across the fault) | m |
| $d$ | locking depth of a fault | m |
| $\hat{u}$ | unit vector along the line of sight, ground to satellite | — |
| $\alpha$ | satellite heading, from north | rad |
| $\theta$ | local incidence angle | rad |
| $A$, $\sigma$ | amplitude and radius of a Gaussian uplift | m, m |

The line-of-sight displacement of a surface point moving by $\mathbf{d}$ is

$$d_{\text{LOS}} = \hat{u}\cdot\mathbf{d},
\qquad
\hat{u} = \left(\sin\theta\cos\alpha,\ -\sin\theta\sin\alpha,\ \cos\theta\right)$$

in the east–north–up frame, for a right-looking radar. The sign convention
matters only for interpretation; the magnitude of the projection does not depend
on it.

## 3. Derivations and models

### 3.1 The strike-slip fault

For a long strike-slip fault in a homogeneous elastic half-space with uniform
slip $b$ below a locking depth $d$, the fault-parallel surface displacement is

$$
\boxed{\;u(x) = \frac{b}{\pi}\arctan\!\left(\frac{x}{d}\right),\;}
$$

with $x$ the distance from the trace, measured perpendicular to the fault. This
is a **stated model**, not a derived law: it assumes two-dimensional antiplane
geometry, elastic homogeneity, isotropic friction below $d$, and slip uniform
with depth. The demonstration verifies its consequences rather than its origin:

| property | closed form | measured |
|---|---|---|
| far-field limit | $\pm b/2$ | $\mp0.957621$ m at $x = \mp1$ Mm, i.e. 95.76% of $b/2$ |
| offset over $\pm5d$ | $2(b/\pi)\arctan 5$ | $+1.7487$ m $= 87.4\%$ of $b$ |
| peak surface strain at the trace | $b/(\pi d) = 1.591549\times10^{-4}$ | $1.591536\times10^{-4}$ (ratio 0.999992) |
| displacement at $x = d$ | $b/4$ | $0.500000$ m $= 25.0\%$ of $b$ |
| linearity in $b$ | factor 2 | 2.000000 |

The fourth row is the one interpreters use: because $u(d) = b/4$, the locking
depth is the distance at which a quarter of the far-field offset has accumulated.
Depth is therefore measurable from the *shape*, which is why it is reported at all
— and why a wrong assumption about how slip varies with depth biases it.

Note what is **not** here: no surface rupture. For a buried dislocation the trace
is continuous, the offset accumulates over a zone of width a few $d$, and the
interferogram has no data gap at $x=0$.

### 3.2 Projection onto the line of sight

With heading $\alpha = 8^\circ$ and incidence $\theta = 35^\circ$ the
demonstration's look vector is

$$\hat{u} = (+0.5680,\ -0.0798,\ +0.8192)$$

in (east, north, up). The decisive number is the ratio

$$\frac{\left|\hat{u}_{\text{east}}\right|}
{\left|\hat{u}_{\text{north}}\right|} = \frac{0.5680}{0.0798} = 7.12.$$

A near-polar orbit is **seven times less sensitive to north–south motion than to
east–west**. For a strike-slip fault, whose motion is entirely along strike, that
is the worst case, and it is why InSAR slip estimates for strike-slip events are
much weaker than for dip-slip events. The demonstration projects the same $2\
\mathrm{m}$ slip both ways:

| fault strike | projection coefficient | peak LOS displacement | fringes | largest LOS change per 20 m pixel |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| north–south | $-0.0798$ | 7.6443 cm | 5.406 | 0.2541 mm |
| east–west | $+0.5680$ | 54.3924 cm | 38.464 | 1.8080 mm |

against the $\lambda/4 = 14.1412$ mm per-pixel aliasing limit. Both are
unwrappable in this example, but the east–west case is seven times closer to the
limit — and the *inferred* slip differs by that same factor if the wrong
projection is assumed.

### 3.3 Two geometries, and where the north component goes

Two passes with different headings give two equations in three unknowns:

$$\begin{pmatrix} d^{a}_{\text{LOS}} \\ d^{d}_{\text{LOS}} \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} \hat{u}^{a}_{E} & \hat{u}^{a}_{N} & \hat{u}^{a}_{U} \\
\hat{u}^{d}_{E} & \hat{u}^{d}_{N} & \hat{u}^{d}_{U} \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} d_E \\ d_N \\ d_U \end{pmatrix}.$$

The demonstration uses heading $8^\circ$ / incidence $35^\circ$ (ascending) and
$188^\circ$ / $39^\circ$ (descending):

| component | ascending coefficient | descending coefficient |
|---|---|---|
| east | $+0.5680$ | $-0.6232$ |
| north | $-0.0798$ | $+0.0876$ |
| up | $+0.8192$ | $+0.7771$ |

Assuming $d_N = 0$ and solving the remaining $2\times2$ system recovers a
synthetic truth of $+2.00$ cm east and $-3.00$ cm up **exactly** — to
$0.0000\ \mathrm{mm}$ — because the system is square. Now give the same ground
$10\ \mathrm{mm}$ of northward motion and keep the assumption:

| recovered | value | error |
|---|---|---|
| east | $+1.8595$ cm | $+1.41$ mm |
| up | $-3.0000$ cm | $0.00$ mm |

The north component leaks **entirely into the east estimate** and leaves the
vertical estimate untouched. That is not luck: both passes share the same angle
to north, so the north coefficient is proportional to the east coefficient with
the *same* ratio ($\tan 8^\circ$) in both equations. The two components are not
separable by geometry alone, and the assumption decides which one absorbs the
signal. For a mostly strike-slip earthquake the omitted $d_N$ is the *largest*
term, so the east–west estimate is the least trustworthy number in the
decomposition.

### 3.4 The gradient budget

Unwrapping fails where the phase changes by more than $\pi$ between adjacent
pixels, i.e. where the LOS displacement changes by more than

$$\frac{\lambda}{4} = \frac{5.6565}{4} = 1.4141\ \mathrm{cm} = 14.141\ \mathrm{mm}
\ \text{per pixel}.$$

For a strike-slip fault the surface strain peaks at the trace with
$\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}x = b/(\pi d)$, so the worst-case change per pixel of
size $\Delta x$ is

$$\Delta d_{\text{LOS}} = \hat{u}\cdot\hat{e}\,\frac{b}{\pi d}\Delta x .$$

*Numerical check.* East–west case, $b = 2$ m, $d = 4$ km, $\Delta x = 20$ m:
$0.5680 \times 1.591549\times10^{-4} \times 20 = 1.8080$ mm, i.e. $13\%$ of the
limit. Halving the locking depth doubles it; a $0.8$ m slip on a $1$ km-depth
fault gives $0.5680\times0.8/(\pi\times1000)\times20 = 2.89$ mm per pixel — still
fine. The limit binds for shallow, fast ruptures and for mining-induced
subsidence, not for typical tectonic events at this wavelength.

### 3.5 Volcanoes: the volume is a model, not a measurement

For a Gaussian uplift $A\exp(-r^{2}/2\sigma^{2})$ the integral is exact:

$$\Delta V = \int_0^\infty 2\pi r\,A e^{-r^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}r
= 2\pi\sigma^{2}A .$$

*Numerical check.* $A = 0.060$ m, $\sigma = 2000$ m:
$\Delta V = 2\pi\times4\times10^{6}\times0.06 = 1.5080\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m^{3}}
= 1.5080$ Mm³. The radius at a fraction $f$ of the peak is
$r_f = \sigma\sqrt{-2\ln f}$: at $f = 0.5$, $r = 2355$ m; at $f = 0.25$,
$r = 3330$ m; at $f = 0.1$, $r = 4293$ m. On the line of sight at
$\theta = 35^\circ$ the peak signal is $4.915$ cm $= 3.476$ fringes.

**The honest caveat.** The volume is exact *for the Gaussian*, which is an
assumed shape. The surface displacement of a pressurised source depends on its
depth and volume through a product that the surface field constrains only in
combination: a shallow small source and a deep large one can produce nearly the
same surface pattern, with inferred volumes differing by a large factor. InSAR
constrains the *shape* $(\sigma)$ well and the *amplitude* well, and then the
volume follows from the assumed geometry — which is why a volcanic volume change
is always reported with "assuming a point source at depth $z$". Adding a second
observable (a GNSS station inside the deformation field, or a second look
geometry) breaks the trade-off; a single fringe pattern does not.

### 3.6 Subsidence: the signal lives in the time series

A subsiding basin is well modelled by a rate plus a seasonal term,
$d(t) = v t + A\sin(2\pi(t-t_0))$. The demonstration uses
$v = -14\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ and $A = 6\ \mathrm{mm}$ over 3 years with 40
dates:

| fit | rate (mm/yr) | bias |
|---|---|---|
| straight line only | $-13.0633$ | $+0.9367$ |
| trend + annual sine/cosine + constant | $-14.000000$ | $1.07\times10^{-14}$ |

The omitted seasonal term biases the rate by $0.94\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$ — a
$6.7\%$ error, and larger than the trend of many real basins. The full fit
recovers the amplitude ($6.0000$ mm) and the phase ($-2.5133$ rad) exactly,
because 40 dates over 3 years ($13.3$ dates per cycle) resolve the seasonal term.

The scale of the problem: over the record the subsidence accumulates
$42\ \mathrm{mm} = 148.5\%$ of a single fringe. The *total* signal is barely
one and a half fringes, so no individual interferogram shows the trend at all.
**This is the class of problem that motivates the whole network of
[lesson 03](03-insar-time-series.md)**: the rate is a property of the time
series, not of any pair.

### 3.7 Landslides: when the answer is "not with this method"

For a moving mass whose displacement varies across a width $W$ at a rate $R$, the
gradient over one year of accumulation is $G = R/W$. Comparing with the
per-pixel limit converted to millimetres per metre of ground,
$\lambda/4/\Delta x = 14.141/20 = 0.7071\ \mathrm{mm\,m^{-1}}$:

| rate (mm/yr) | width (m) | gradient after 1 yr (mm/m) | unwrappable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 200 | 0.2500 | yes |
| 50 | 100 | 0.5000 | yes |
| 200 | 100 | 2.0000 | **no (aliased)** |
| 500 | 50 | 10.0000 | **no (aliased)** |

A fast, narrow landslide cannot be unwrapped at this wavelength and pixel size,
no matter how the network is built; the remedies are a shorter revisit (smaller
accumulated motion between epochs), a longer wavelength, a smaller pixel, or a
different method entirely (offset tracking on the amplitude, or ground
instrumentation). Deciding this *before* processing is the point of the
calculation, and the demonstration prints the verdict explicitly.

## 4. Worked example — reading a fringe pattern backwards

*All values SYNTHETIC.* An interferogram over a strike-slip fault shows 5.4
fringes of peak displacement, and the fringes are symmetric about a straight
trace. The regional strike is north–south; the look geometry is
$\alpha = 8^\circ$, $\theta = 35^\circ$.

1. **LOS displacement.** $5.406$ fringes $\times 2.8282\ \mathrm{cm}
   = 15.29\ \mathrm{cm}$ along the line of sight.
2. **Projection.** For an N–S fault the projection coefficient is
   $\hat{u}_N = -0.0798$, so the inferred fault-parallel offset is
   $u = 15.29/0.0798 = 191.6\ \mathrm{cm} \approx 1.92\ \mathrm{m}$.
3. **Check against the model.** With $b = 2.0$ m the model gives a peak LOS of
   $7.6443$ cm, not $15.29$ cm. The observation is **twice** the model at the same
   slip — so either the slip is $\sim4$ m, or the geometry assumption
   (N–S strike, pure strike-slip) is wrong.
4. **The alternative.** If the fault were actually east–west (a mis-set regional
   strike), the projection coefficient would be $+0.5680$, and the same
   $15.29\ \mathrm{cm}$ would imply only $u = 0.269$ m of slip. The *same data*
   admit a $15\times$ range of slip, purely through the assumed projection.
5. **Resolution.** Use the second geometry, or an independent GNSS measurement of
   the fault-parallel motion. This is the arithmetic behind the rule that InSAR
   alone does not determine a strike-slip source.

## 5. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Elastic, homogeneous, isotropic half-space** for the fault model. Layering,
   topography, pore pressure and viscoelastic relaxation all bias the inferred
   slip and depth.
2. **Two-dimensional antiplane geometry.** A finite fault has along-strike
   variation and a decomposition-dependent near-field pattern that no 2-D model
   reproduces.
3. **The look vector is known.** An error in heading or incidence propagates
   linearly into the inferred displacement through the projection.
4. **The motion is assumed to have no component along the unmeasured direction.**
   Section 3.3 measures that this assumption can carry the whole signal.
5. **Source shape assumed** for the volume conversion: the surface field alone
   does not determine depth and volume separately.
6. **Linear superposition** of deformation, atmosphere and orbit error — a
   statement about the *processing*, not the physics.
7. **No aliasing** anywhere in the scene. Section 3.4 and 3.7 show the criterion
   and how easily it is violated.
8. **Teaching values throughout.** The slips, depths, rates, volumes and the
   $3.0\ \mathrm{mm\,km^{-1}}$-class atmospheric coefficient of lesson
   [04](04-insar-atmospheric-correction.md) are chosen for arithmetic clarity and
   are not measurements.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Quoting a slip without the projection.** A factor of seven lives in the
   geometry.
2. **Trusting the east–west component of a decomposition.** It absorbs the
   unmeasured north–south motion exactly.
3. **Reporting a source volume as a measurement.** It is a model output.
4. **Fitting a rate without a seasonal term.** A $0.94\ \mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}$
   bias appeared in a demonstration with a 6 mm season.
5. **Processing a landslide without the gradient budget.** Fast or narrow motion
   aliases regardless of the network.
6. **Reading a 2-D elastic model as the Earth.** Layering and finite-fault
   effects are first-order.
7. **Forgetting the trace.** Even a buried dislocation has a decorrelated near
   field in the presence of surface rupture, and no interpolation invents data.
