# The Electromagnetic Spectrum and Sensing Windows

Remote sensing begins with a measurement that never touches the thing it
measures. Every instrument in this folder — a camera, a thermal scanner, a radar
— records electromagnetic radiation that has travelled from a surface, through
an atmosphere, into an aperture. The wavelength of that radiation decides what
the surface does to it, what the atmosphere does to it, and which quantity the
detector actually converts into a number. This lesson builds the wavelength
bookkeeping that every later lesson assumes.

The organising question is deliberately narrow: **for a given target property,
which wavelengths can carry information about it to a sensor above the
atmosphere?** The answer is a set of *windows* — intervals where the atmosphere
is substantially transparent — and each window sits next to an interval where
the atmosphere is opaque. A sensing band is therefore never chosen because it is
physically interesting alone; it is chosen because it is both diagnostic of the
target and reachable through the air.

Two facts from this lesson recur constantly in the rest of the folder. First,
the Sun and the Earth emit at almost disjoint wavelengths, so solar-reflected
and thermal-emitted sensing are physically separate problems with separate
radiometry. Second, atmospheric absorption is a *spectral* process with narrow
features, so the spectrum is not a continuum with a gentle envelope but a comb of
windows and blocks — and a small change in band placement can move a measurement
from a transparent interval to an opaque one.

All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**. The constants used here
are exact SI values; every spectrum quoted as a teaching model is generated by
the demonstration in `code/`, and no real sensor or mission is named.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Convert** between wavelength, frequency and photon energy, and **state** the
   band each of a set of representative wavelengths belongs to.
2. **Derive** Wien's displacement law from the Planck function and **compute** the
   peak-emission wavelength for a given temperature.
3. **Compute** the total emitted exitance with the Stefan-Boltzmann law and
   **explain** why the solar-reflected and thermal-emitted regimes occupy
   different wavelength ranges.
4. **Explain** why a sensing window exists, and **quantify** the transmittance of
   a synthetic atmosphere through Beer-Lambert absorption.
5. **Quantify** the wavelength dependence of Rayleigh scattering and **state** the
   consequence for blue skylight and for the visible-versus-infrared contrast.
6. **Decide**, for a stated target property, whether a candidate wavelength is
   usable, by combining the Planck curve of the source, the transmittance of the
   atmosphere, and the energy available at the detector.
7. **State** the assumptions under which a blackbody model is adequate and the
   conditions under which it fails.

## 1. Intuition: the atmosphere decides what you can see

Imagine standing on a hill at night and looking at a town. You see it because
visible light passes through the air. Now imagine looking at the same town with
a detector tuned to a wavelength where the air is opaque — say a wavelength on
the flank of a strong absorption feature. The town is still emitting; the
photons still leave the roofs; but a few hundred metres above your head they are
absorbed and re-emitted in a random direction. Your detector sees the absorbing
layer, not the town.

That is the entire content of a "sensing window". The surface emits and reflects
across the whole spectrum, but only in the windows does a usable fraction of that
radiation reach the top of the atmosphere. Two consequences follow immediately:

- **The windows are fixed by the atmosphere, not by the instrument.** Improving
  the detector does not open a window.
- **The windows are spectral features, not bands of convenience.** Their edges
  are set by molecular absorption lines, so their widths and positions vary with
  the amounts of the absorbing gases present.

The second organising fact is the enormous temperature gap between the two
sources a surface sensor sees. The Sun's photosphere is of order $5800\
\mathrm{K}$; the Earth's surface is of order $290\ \mathrm{K}$. By Wien's law
(Section 3.2) those two temperatures place their peak emissions a factor of
about twenty apart in wavelength. That single ratio is why the subject splits
cleanly into *optical* remote sensing (reflected sunlight) and *thermal* remote
sensing (emitted terrestrial radiation), a split this folder follows in module
02.

## 2. Precise definitions and units

### 2.1 The spectral variables

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| wavelength | $\lambda$ | m (quoted in µm) | $1\ \mathrm{\mu m} = 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m}$ |
| frequency | $\nu$ | Hz $= \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ | $\nu = c/\lambda$ |
| wavenumber | $\tilde{\nu}$ | $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ (quoted cm⁻¹) | $\tilde{\nu} = 1/\lambda$ |
| photon energy | $E$ | J (quoted eV) | $E = h\nu = hc/\lambda$ |
| spectral radiance | $B_\lambda$ | $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,m^{-1}}$ | radiance per unit wavelength |
| exitance | $M$ | $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ | power per unit area leaving a surface |
| irradiance | $E_e$ | $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ | power per unit area arriving |

Radiance is the quantity that is *conserved* along a ray in a non-absorbing,
non-emitting medium, which is exactly why a sensor measures radiance rather than
power. Irradiance is what a surface receives; exitance is what it sends away.

### 2.2 Blackbody and the Planck function

A **blackbody** is an ideal absorber-emitter: it absorbs all incident radiation
and, in thermal equilibrium, emits the maximum possible radiance at every
wavelength for its temperature. Its spectral radiance is the Planck function

$$
B_\lambda(T) = \frac{2hc^{2}}{\lambda^{5}}\,
\frac{1}{\exp\!\left(\dfrac{hc}{\lambda k_B T}\right) - 1},
\qquad \left[\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,m^{-1}}\right].
$$

A real surface emits a fraction $\varepsilon(\lambda)$ of this, its **emissivity**:
$L_\lambda = \varepsilon(\lambda)\,B_\lambda(T)$. Emissivity is the subject of
[17-01-02](02-reflectance-and-emissivity.md); here we fix $\varepsilon = 1$ so that
only the temperature and the wavelength vary.

### 2.3 Standard constants

The whole lesson is built from six exact or standard constants.

| Constant | Symbol | Value | Unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Planck constant | $h$ | $6.62607015\times10^{-34}$ | J s |
| speed of light | $c$ | $2.99792458\times10^{8}$ | m s⁻¹ |
| Boltzmann constant | $k_B$ | $1.380649\times10^{-23}$ | J K⁻¹ |
| Stefan-Boltzmann constant | $\sigma$ | $5.670374419\times10^{-8}$ | W m⁻² K⁻⁴ |
| Wien displacement constant | $b$ | $2.897771955\times10^{-3}$ | m K |
| solar constant (at 1 au) | $S$ | $1361$ | W m⁻² |

The first three define the Planck function; $\sigma$ and $b$ are derived from
them in Section 3, and the demonstration in Section 8 prints every number below
from these values.

## 3. Derivations

### 3.1 Photon energy and the reason some bands are "quantum"

$$E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}.$$

Since $hc = 1.9864459\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{J\,m}$, a compact working form is

$$E\,[\mathrm{eV}] = \frac{1.2398419}{\lambda\,[\mathrm{\mu m}]},$$

which is the *only* conversion you need for the band table. It explains why the
spectrum is divided where it is: a photon at $0.55\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ carries
$2.25\ \mathrm{eV}$, enough to lift an electron across a semiconductor band gap
and be counted by a silicon detector; a photon at $10\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ carries
$0.124\ \mathrm{eV}$, too little for that, so thermal detectors must measure
heating rather than photoelectric ejection. Detector physics, not taste, sets the
long-wavelength limit of the optical bands.

### 3.2 Wien's displacement law

The peak of $B_\lambda(T)$ is found by setting $\mathrm{d}B_\lambda/\mathrm{d}\lambda
= 0$. With $x = hc/(\lambda k_B T)$ the condition reduces to

$$x = 5\left(1 - e^{-x}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x \approx 4.965114,$$

so that $\lambda_{\max} T = hc/(x k_B) = b = 2.897771955\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\,K}$
exactly as tabulated. Hence

$$\boxed{\;\lambda_{\max} = \frac{b}{T}\;}\qquad
\text{or}\qquad \lambda_{\max}\,[\mathrm{\mu m}] = \frac{2897.771955}{T\,[\mathrm{K}]}.$$

*Numerical check (exact constants).* For the Sun's photosphere at
$T = 5772\ \mathrm{K}$, $\lambda_{\max} = 2.897771955\times10^{-3}/5772
= 5.02\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m} = 0.502\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ — in the visible. For the
Earth at $T = 288\ \mathrm{K}$, $\lambda_{\max}
= 2.897771955\times10^{-3}/288 = 1.0062\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m}
= 10.062\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ — in the thermal infrared. The ratio is
$10.062/0.502 \approx 20$, the factor mentioned in Section 1. The
demonstration prints both values and the intermediate temperatures
$1200\ \mathrm{K}\to2.415\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ and $220\ \mathrm{K}\to13.172\
\mathrm{\mu m}$.

### 3.3 Stefan-Boltzmann law

Integrating $B_\lambda$ over all wavelengths and over the outward hemisphere,

$$M = \int_0^\infty \!\!\int_{\rm hemisphere} B_\lambda(T)\cos\theta\,
\mathrm{d}\Omega\,\mathrm{d}\lambda = \sigma T^{4},\qquad
\sigma = \frac{2\pi^{5}k_B^{4}}{15h^{3}c^{2}}.$$

*Numerical check.* At $288\ \mathrm{K}$, $M = 5.670374419\times10^{-8}\times288^{4}
= 5.670374419\times10^{-8}\times6.87997\times10^{9} = 390.1\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$.
At $5772\ \mathrm{K}$ the same law gives $6.294\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$,
larger by a factor $1.613\times10^{5}$.

### 3.4 Why the Earth is not simply at the radiating temperature

A planet intercepts sunlight over a disc of area $\pi R^{2}$ but radiates from a
sphere of area $4\pi R^{2}$, so the absorbed flux per unit surface area is

$$F_{\rm abs} = \frac{S(1-A)}{4},$$

with $A$ the planetary **albedo** (the fraction reflected back to space). Setting
$F_{\rm abs} = \sigma T_{\rm eff}^{4}$ gives the effective radiating temperature

$$T_{\rm eff} = \left[\frac{S(1-A)}{4\sigma}\right]^{1/4}.$$

*Numerical check.* With $A = 0.30$ (a SYNTHETIC teaching value),
$F_{\rm abs} = 1361\times0.70/4 = 238.2\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$,
$T_{\rm eff} = (238.2/5.670374419\times10^{-8})^{1/4}
= (4.2007\times10^{9})^{1/4} = 254.6\ \mathrm{K}$. The observed surface is
$288\ \mathrm{K}$, so the surface must emit at $390.1\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ while
only $238.2\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ leaves the top of the atmosphere. The
$33.4\ \mathrm{K}$ difference is the greenhouse effect, and it is the physical
reason a thermal sensor's radiance is *not* simply $B_\lambda(T_{\rm surface})$:
the atmosphere both emits downward and absorbs upward. That is the subject of
[17-01-03](03-atmospheric-effects.md).

### 3.5 Beer-Lambert absorption and the definition of a window

Let a beam of spectral radiance $L_\lambda$ cross a layer with absorption
coefficient $\kappa_\lambda$ [m⁻¹] and thickness $\mathrm{d}z$. In the absence of
scattering and emission,

$$\mathrm{d}L_\lambda = -\kappa_\lambda L_\lambda\,\mathrm{d}z
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
L_\lambda(s) = L_\lambda(0)\,e^{-\tau_\lambda},\qquad
\tau_\lambda = \int_0^{s}\kappa_\lambda\,\mathrm{d}z,$$

so the **transmittance** is $T_\lambda = e^{-\tau_\lambda}$ and
$\tau_\lambda$ is the **optical depth** (dimensionless). A **sensing window** is
a wavelength interval in which $T_\lambda$ is close enough to one that a surface
signal survives the round trip, and the surrounding intervals are the blocks,
where $\tau_\lambda \gtrsim 1$ and the atmosphere is effectively opaque.

The demonstration uses a **synthetic** atmosphere whose optical depth is a sum of
Gaussian features,

$$\tau_\lambda = \sum_i \tau_i \exp\!\left[-\left(\frac{\lambda - \lambda_i}{w_i}\right)^{2}\right]
+ \tau_{\rm cont},$$

with ten chosen features ($\tau_{\rm cont} = 0.02$). Its mean transmittance over
$0.30$–$16\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ is $0.576$, and the window means are:

| Nominal window | Range (µm) | Mean transmittance | Fraction with $\tau > 1$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| visible | 0.40–0.70 | 0.770 | 0.053 |
| near-infrared | 0.75–1.30 | 0.779 | 0.128 |
| shortwave-infrared | 1.55–1.75 | 0.800 | 0.052 |
| midwave-infrared | 3.50–4.10 | 0.830 | 0.083 |
| thermal-infrared | 8.00–13.00 | 0.437 | 0.350 |

Note the last column: even the visible "window" is 5% blocked, and the
thermal-infrared window is 35% blocked because the synthetic ozone feature at
$9.60\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ eats into it. A window is never perfectly open; it is
merely open enough.

### 3.6 Rayleigh scattering and its $\lambda^{-4}$ dependence

For scattering by particles much smaller than the wavelength (molecules), the
scattering cross-section per molecule is

$$\sigma_{\rm sca} = \frac{8\pi^{3}}{3}\frac{(n^{2}-1)^{2}}{N^{2}\lambda^{4}},$$

so the scattered fraction scales as $\lambda^{-4}$. Taking $0.55\ \mathrm{\mu m}$
as reference, the demonstration's relative weights are $1.00$ at
$0.55\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, $9.15\times10^{-2}$ at $1.0\ \mathrm{\mu m}$,
$3.96\times10^{-4}$ at $3.90\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ and $9.15\times10^{-6}$ at
$10\ \mathrm{\mu m}$. Over the visible band, blue light at $0.45\ \mathrm{\mu m}$
scatters about $4.4$ times more strongly than red at $0.65\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ — the
same law that makes the sky blue and the setting Sun red. For remote sensing the
consequence is practical: the shorter the wavelength, the larger the
atmospheric *scattering* additive term, even where the *absorption* window is
clean.

## 4. Physical interpretation

- **Two sources, two regimes.** Solar-reflected sensing works between roughly
  $0.4$ and $3\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ because that is where the Sun is bright and the
  atmosphere has windows; thermal sensing works between roughly $8$ and
  $14\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ because that is where the Earth is brightest. The
  $3$–$5\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ interval is the ambiguous overlap where both matter,
  which is why it is treated as a separate case in
  [17-02-03](../02-optical-and-thermal-remote-sensing/03-thermal-infrared.md).
- **Transmittance multiplies.** A round trip through the atmosphere applies
  $T_\lambda$ twice for reflected sunlight (down and up) and once for emitted
  thermal radiance that originates at the surface. Small transmittance errors
  therefore hurt reflected bands more.
- **Photon energy sets the detector.** A $0.124\ \mathrm{eV}$ thermal photon
  cannot eject an electron in silicon, so thermal bands use bolometers and
  photon-detection language does not transfer.
- **Windows are quantitative, not qualitative.** "The atmosphere is transparent
  in the visible" is replaced by a number: transmittance $0.770$ in this
  synthetic model, which is a 23% loss before any surface interaction is
  considered.

## 5. Assumptions and regime of validity

The blackbody development of Sections 3.2–3.4 is valid under these assumptions,
each of which is violated somewhere in this folder:

1. **Thermodynamic equilibrium.** The source is characterised by a single
   temperature. The Sun's photosphere is close to this; a rock heated on one
   face and not the other is not.
2. **Opacity and emissivity equal unity.** A real surface has
   $\varepsilon(\lambda) < 1$ and generally varies with wavelength. Setting
   $\varepsilon = 1$ is the idealisation relaxed in
   [17-01-02](02-reflectance-and-emissivity.md).
3. **No atmosphere.** Sections 3.2–3.4 describe a surface at the top of the
   atmosphere. Everything the sensor sees passes through air; Section 3.5 and
   [17-01-03](03-atmospheric-effects.md) supply the correction.
4. **No scattering for the Beer-Lambert law.** Section 3.5 treats absorption
   only. Adding scattering requires the full radiative-transfer equation, in
   which the source function is no longer zero.
5. **Plane-parallel, horizontally uniform atmosphere.** Real atmospheres vary,
   and clouds break the assumption entirely.
6. **The synthetic absorption model's features are chosen teaching values.**
   Real absorption line positions, strengths and pressure broadening differ;
   consequently the window means in Section 3.5 are properties of *this model*
   only and must not be quoted as atmospheric data.

## 6. Units and dimensional analysis

Check that every quantity carries the unit it should before trusting a number.

| Expression | Unit arithmetic | Result |
|---|---|---|
| $\nu = c/\lambda$ | $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} / \mathrm{m}$ | $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ |
| $E = hc/\lambda$ | $\mathrm{J\,s}\cdot\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}/\mathrm{m}$ | $\mathrm{J}$ |
| $\lambda_{\max} = b/T$ | $\mathrm{m\,K}/\mathrm{K}$ | $\mathrm{m}$ |
| $\sigma T^{4}$ | $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}}\cdot\mathrm{K^{4}}$ | $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ |
| $\tau = \int \kappa\,\mathrm{d}z$ | $\mathrm{m^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{m}$ | dimensionless |
| $B_\lambda$ | $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}\,m^{-1}}$ | as declared |

Two traps are worth naming. First, $B_\lambda$ and $B_\nu$ have *different*
units and different peak wavelengths (this is why Wien's law is stated per unit
wavelength, and it is not the same constant for the per-frequency form). Second,
a wavenumber in $\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ is the reciprocal of a wavelength in cm, not
in µm; mixing the two shifts every band by $10^{4}$.

## 7. Worked examples

### Example 1 — the thermal band of a hot surface

A SYNTHETIC lava-like surface is at $1200\ \mathrm{K}$. Where does it emit most
strongly, what total exitance does it radiate, and how many photons per second
does a $1\ \mathrm{m^{2}}$ patch emit at $2.415\ \mathrm{\mu m}$?

1. $\lambda_{\max} = 2.897771955\times10^{-3}/1200 = 2.415\times10^{-6}\
   \mathrm{m} = 2.415\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ (matches the demonstration's table).
2. $M = \sigma T^{4} = 5.670374419\times10^{-8}\times1200^{4}
   = 1.176\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$.
3. Per photon, $E = hc/\lambda
   = 1.9864459\times10^{-25}/2.415\times10^{-6}
   = 8.225\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{J}$. The *total* photon emission rate is
   $M/E = 1.176\times10^{5}/8.225\times10^{-20}
   = 1.43\times10^{24}\ \mathrm{photons\,s^{-1}\,m^{-2}}$.

The point of step 3 is that "how bright" and "how many photons" are different
questions once the photon energy changes by $20\times$ between bands.

### Example 2 — is there enough energy in the band?

Using the demonstration's synthetic atmosphere: is a $1.65\ \mathrm{\mu m}$
surface signal stronger or weaker than a $0.55\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ one, for the same
reflected surface radiance?

1. Window-mean transmittances: $T_{1.65} = 0.800$, $T_{0.55} = 0.770$ — the SWIR
   window is marginally *cleaner* in this synthetic model. (These are means over
   the whole window; the transmittance at the single wavelength $1.65\
   \mathrm{\mu m}$ is $0.914$.)
2. Rayleigh weight: $1.23\times10^{-2}$ at $1.65\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ against
   $1.00$ at $0.55\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, so the additive scattered component is about
   $80\times$ smaller in the SWIR.
3. Solar radiance available: $4.53\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,sr^{-1}}$
   in the $1.55$–$1.75\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ window against
   $5.58\times10^{6}$ in the visible — about $8\%$ as much.

So the SWIR band is cleaner but dimmer. Whether it is *usable* depends on the
detector noise, which is the trade quantified in
[17-01-04](04-resolution-concepts.md).

### Example 3 — reading a spectrum against the windows

Consider three notional reflectance features at $\lambda = 0.66$, $1.40$ and
$2.20\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ (all chosen for the exercise; no real mineral is named).

| $\lambda$ (µm) | Synthetic $T_\lambda$ | Optical depth | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.66 | 0.773 | 0.257 | inside the visible window; usable |
| 1.40 | 0.035 | 3.351 | on the synthetic water-2 feature; essentially blocked |
| 2.20 | 0.972 | 0.029 | between the water-3 and water-4 features; usable |
| 2.70 | 0.015 | 4.220 | on the synthetic water-4 feature; blocked |

The transmittance values come from evaluating the model at those wavelengths
(demonstration section 9). The lesson is blunt: a *diagnostic* absorption feature
at $1.40\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ is useless from above the atmosphere if the atmosphere
absorbs it first. Real band selection is a search for wavelengths that are
simultaneously diagnostic **and** transmitted.

## 10. Common pitfalls

1. **Quoting a window without a number.** "The atmosphere is transparent in the
   infrared" is false in this synthetic model for 35% of the $8$–$13\
   \mathrm{\mu m}$ window.
2. **Confusing $B_\lambda$ with $B_\nu$.** Their units differ by
   $\lambda^{2}/c$, and Wien's constant is not interchangeable between them.
3. **Assuming the surface temperature equals the colour temperature.** With
   $\varepsilon < 1$ and an atmosphere in between, a brightness temperature is
   not a surface temperature.
4. **Forgetting the round trip.** Reflected solar radiation is attenuated twice;
   emitted thermal radiation from the surface once.
5. **Treating this model's features as measured data.** They are chosen
   Gaussian teaching features, and their centres are parameters of the demo.
6. **Ignoring scattering when the window is absorption-clean.** Rayleigh
   scattering at $0.45\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ is about $4.4\times$ that at
   $0.65\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, and it adds signal that is not from the target.

## 11. Real-world applications

- **Band selection.** Every optical instrument's band list is a compromise
  between diagnostic value and atmospheric transmission, and the reasoning here
  is the reasoning behind that list.
- **Thermal mapping.** Because the Earth emits near $10\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, night
  imaging is possible with emitted radiation alone; solar-reflected sensing is
  impossible at night.
- **Hot-target detection.** A $1200\ \mathrm{K}$ surface emits $1.176\times10^{5}\
  \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$, about $300\times$ the Earth's exitance, which is why
  volcanic and fire thermal anomalies are detectable in the midwave infrared.
- **Atmospheric correction.** Knowing which window a band sits in tells you
  whether the dominant error is absorption (thermal bands) or scattering
  (visible bands).
- **GIS and data fusion.** Because solar and thermal bands measure different
  physical quantities, a GIS layer stack mixing them must document the physical
  quantity per layer, not just the band index — see
  [17-05-01](../05-gis-fundamentals/01-gis-concepts.md).
