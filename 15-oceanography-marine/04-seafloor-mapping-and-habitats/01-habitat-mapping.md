# Benthic Habitat Mapping and Classification

> **All models and data in this module are SYNTHETIC.** The seabed parameters,
> terrain surface, class features and classifier results below are properties of
> teaching models generated by the demonstration. No real survey, seabed, habitat
> map or ground-truth station is referenced, and no number is quoted from a
> published measurement.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Compute** the acoustic reflection coefficient of a seabed from its density
   and sound speed, and **convert** an impedance contrast into a decibel level.
2. **Compute** the Rayleigh roughness parameter $2k\sigma\cos\theta$ and **state**
   whether a seabed is in the coherent or the diffuse scattering regime.
3. **Predict** and **measure** the angular response of backscatter for a smooth,
   high-impedance seabed and for a fully rough one, and **explain** why the shape,
   not a single number, is the classifier.
4. **Quantify** how grid spacing biases a terrain attribute, and **state** the
   consequence for any habitat class defined by a slope threshold.
5. **Compute** a confusion matrix, per-class recall and precision, overall
   accuracy and Cohen's kappa, and **explain** why accuracy alone is misleading
   with unequal class priors.
6. **Demonstrate** with numbers that the number of clusters cannot be chosen by
   accuracy, and **state** what does choose it.
7. **State** the two ways a habitat map's reported accuracy is optimistic, and
   **name** the validation design that avoids each.

## 1. Intuition: a habitat map is a model, not a photograph

A benthic habitat map looks like a photograph of the seafloor with labels on it.
It is not. It is the output of three separate models stacked on each other: an
**acoustic model** that turns received intensity into a seabed property, a
**terrain model** that turns a gridded surface into attributes such as slope and
rugosity, and a **classification model** that turns those attributes into class
labels. Each has parameters, each is fitted or chosen, and each can fail
silently.

The physical measurement is simple to state. A sonar sends a pulse, the seabed
returns part of it, and the level and the angular dependence of the return depend
on two things: the **impedance contrast** at the water–sediment interface, and the
**roughness** of the interface relative to the acoustic wavelength. Soft mud is a
weak reflector with almost no roughness; rock is a strong reflector with
centimetre-to-metre roughness. At 100 kHz the wavelength is about 1.5 cm, so a
0.1 m rough rock surface is not slightly rough — it is *violently* rough, and the
coherent reflection model that works for mud does not apply to it at all. §3.1–3.2
make that quantitative.

The terrain side has its own trap. Slope is a derivative of a gridded surface, and
the grid is a choice. A 200 m grid on a 50 m high, 200 m wide mound reports a
maximum slope of $6.72^\circ$ where a 5 m grid reports $8.62^\circ$ — a 22%
underestimate that arrives with no error message. Any habitat class defined as
"slope > 7°" changes its area when the grid is changed. This is the marine form
of the modifiable areal unit problem, and §3.3 measures it.

Finally, the accuracy of the resulting map: the standard summary statistic,
overall accuracy, is inflated by unequal class priors — in the demonstration a
classifier that answers "mud" for every sample scores 0.537 accuracy, while
having no skill at all. Kappa and per-class recall are what expose that, and
§3.4 prints all of them.

## 2. Precise definitions

**Grazing angle** $\theta$: the angle between the incident ray and the seabed
plane, $0^\circ$ along the interface, $90^\circ$ at normal incidence. Confusing
grazing angle with incidence angle from the normal inverts every angular
statement, and it is the commonest error in reading backscatter literature.

**Impedance** $Z=\rho c$, unit $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$ (the "rayl").

**Reflection coefficient** $R$: the ratio of reflected to incident pressure
amplitude. At normal incidence in a fluid–fluid interface,
$R=(Z_2-Z_1)/(Z_2+Z_1)$.

**Backscatter strength** $\mathrm{BS}$: the decibel level of the backscattered
intensity per unit insonified area, relative to a unit reference,
$\mathrm{BS}=10\log_{10}(\text{scattering cross-section per unit area})$.

**Rayleigh roughness parameter**: $2k\sigma\cos\theta$ with $k=2\pi f/c$ the
acoustic wavenumber and $\sigma$ the rms height of the interface. Values much
below 1 mean the interface is smooth enough for coherent reflection; values much
above 1 mean the return is dominated by scattering, and the coherent model is
invalid rather than merely inaccurate.

**Lambertian scattering**: the end-member model in which the backscattered
intensity is proportional to $\sin^2\theta$ with a proportionality constant
$\mu_L$. It is the large-roughness limit used in practice.

**Terrain attribute**: any quantity computed from the gridded surface — slope,
aspect, curvature, rugosity, and the **bathymetric position index** (BPI, the
elevation of a cell relative to the mean of an annulus around it).

**Confusion matrix**: a table of predicted class against true class for a
validation set. From it: **recall** (fraction of a true class that is found),
**precision** (fraction of a predicted class that is correct), **overall
accuracy** (trace divided by total) and **kappa**
($(p_o-p_e)/(1-p_e)$, the agreement above chance).

## 3. Derivations and measurements

### 3.1 Impedance contrast

For a fluid over a fluid, the plane-wave reflection coefficient at grazing angle
$\theta$ is

$$R=\frac{\rho_2q_1-\rho_1q_2}{\rho_2q_1+\rho_1q_2},\qquad
q_1=\frac{\sin\theta}{c_1},\qquad
q_2=\sqrt{\frac{1}{c_2^{2}}-\frac{\cos^{2}\theta}{c_1^{2}}} ,$$

which reduces to the impedance formula at $\theta=90^\circ$ (normal incidence).
When the square root is imaginary the wave is evanescent in the lower medium and
$|R|=1$: because seawater is almost always slower than the seabed, **real
seafloors have a critical grazing angle and are perfect reflectors at grazing
incidence**.

With water $\rho_1=1030\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$,
$c_1=1500\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ ($Z_1=1.545\times10^{6}$), the demonstration's two
model seabeds give

| seabed | $\rho_2$ | $c_2$ | $Z_2$ | $\lvert$R(90°)$\rvert$ | level |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| soft mud | 1500 | 1520 | $2.280\times10^{6}$ | 0.1922 | $-14.33$ dB |
| rock | 2600 | 3200 | $8.320\times10^{6}$ | 0.6868 | $-3.26$ dB |

A factor of 11 in reflected *power* separates them, and that is the whole basis
of using backscatter as a sediment property.

### 3.2 Roughness, and where the coherent model stops applying

The coherence loss from a rough interface is
$\exp[-(2k\sigma\cos\theta)^{2}]$, i.e. a loss in decibels of
$10\log_{10}\{\exp[-(2k\sigma\cos\theta)^2]\}$. At 100 kHz,
$k=2\pi f/c=418.9\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$ (wavelength 1.50 cm). The demonstration's
table:

| $\theta$ | $2k\sigma\cos\theta$ (mud, $\sigma=2$ mm) | loss (dB) | rock ($\sigma=0.1$ m) | loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20° | 1.57 | $-10.8$ | 78.72 | n/a |
| 30° | 1.45 | $-9.1$ | 72.55 | n/a |
| 45° | 1.18 | $-6.1$ | 59.24 | n/a |
| 60° | 0.84 | $-3.0$ | 41.89 | n/a |
| 90° | 0.00 | $-0.0$ | 0.00 | $-0.0$ |

Two conclusions, both of which change how the data are used:

1. **Mud is in the intermediate regime**: the coherence loss is $3$–$11$ dB and
   varies strongly with the grazing angle, so the mud's angular response carries
   information about its roughness.
2. **A 0.1 m rough rock at 100 kHz is two orders of magnitude beyond the coherent
   regime**, so no coherent-reflection calculation describes it. Only at normal
   incidence (where $\cos\theta=0$) does the coherent model return a finite
   value, and it returns the impedance level $-3.26$ dB. The demonstration prints
   "n/a" rather than a meaningless $-27000$ dB, and that refusal is the correct
   scientific output: **the model's regime of validity is part of the answer.**

### 3.3 Angular response

The measured coherent BS for the mud is $-22.9$, $-22.6$, $-20.1$, $-17.3$ and
$-14.3$ dB at $20^\circ$, $30^\circ$, $45^\circ$, $60^\circ$ and $90^\circ$
grazing; the Lambertian end-member with $\mu_L=0.02$ gives $-26.3$, $-23.0$,
$-20.0$, $-18.2$ and $-17.0$ dB. Over $20^\circ$–$60^\circ$ the mud's coherent
curve rises at $+0.142\ \mathrm{dB\,deg^{-1}}$ and the Lambertian at
$+0.202\ \mathrm{dB\,deg^{-1}}$.

Both brighten toward normal incidence, so the *sign* of the slope is not a
discriminator between these two end-members; the **level** and the **curvature**
are. Three features of the curve are used in practice:

- the level near normal incidence, which for a coherent seabed is $20\log_{10}|R|$
  and therefore an impedance measurement;
- the difference between the observed curve and $20\log_{10}|R|$, which is the
  coherence loss and therefore a roughness measurement;
- the shape in the middle angles, compared with a $\sin^2\theta$ law, which says
  how far the seabed is from the fully diffuse limit.

The demonstration's own numbers are a warning about method: the mud's coherent
level at $90^\circ$ ($-14.3$ dB) equals its impedance level exactly, while the
Lambertian model's $90^\circ$ value is $-17.0$ dB. **A single backscatter value
is therefore consistent with both a smooth strong reflector and a rough weak one
— it takes the curve to separate them.**

### 3.4 Grid resolution and terrain attributes

For a Gaussian mound $h(r)=A\exp[-r^2/(2s^2)]$ the analytic maximum slope is

$$\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\mathrm{d}r}\right|_{\max}=\frac{A}{s}e^{-1/2}
=\frac{50}{200}\times0.6065=0.1516\quad\Rightarrow\quad8.62^\circ .$$

Measuring on a grid of spacing $\Delta x$ with central differences:

| $\Delta x$ (m) | samples across the mound | measured max slope | recovered |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 240 | $8.62^\circ$ | 100.0% |
| 10 | 120 | $8.62^\circ$ | 99.9% |
| 25 | 48 | $8.58^\circ$ | 99.5% |
| 50 | 24 | $8.45^\circ$ | 98.0% |
| 100 | 12 | $7.94^\circ$ | 92.1% |
| 200 | 6 | $6.72^\circ$ | 77.9% |

The bias is negligible until the feature is sampled by a few tens of cells and
then grows fast: at 6 samples across the mound a quarter of the true slope is
gone. **Every slope-threshold habitat class inherits that bias**, and because the
bias is systematic rather than random it does not average out over a map — it
converts a shoreline of a class into a different shoreline.

### 3.5 Classification, accuracy and kappa

The demonstration builds 660 synthetic samples in two features (slope in degrees,
backscatter in dB) from three classes with deliberately overlapping
distributions and unequal sizes: mud 400, sand 200, rock 60. A nearest-centroid
classifier trained on 40 samples gives, on the remaining 620:

| truth \ prediction | mud | sand | rock | recall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| mud | 317 | 57 | 0 | 0.848 |
| sand | 16 | 165 | 7 | 0.878 |
| rock | 0 | 21 | 37 | 0.638 |
| precision | 0.952 | 0.679 | 0.841 | |

Overall accuracy $0.837$, $\kappa=0.704$. The majority-class baseline
("everything is mud") scores $0.537$ accuracy with $\kappa\approx0$. The rare
class is the poorly recovered one: rock recall is $0.638$ against mud's $0.848$,
and the sand precision is only $0.679$ because rock is confused into it.

**Kappa is the honest headline** because it subtracts the agreement a random
classifier with the same marginals would produce; it is still not a substitute
for the per-class recalls, since a kappa of 0.704 can hide a rare class that is
almost never found — as here.

### 3.6 What the unsupervised route cannot supply

k-means with three clusters reproduces the supervised partition almost exactly on
this data set: accuracy $0.839$ against $0.837$, $\kappa=0.710$ against $0.704$.
Accuracy therefore does not choose between them. What k-means cannot supply is
the mapping from cluster index to habitat name; the demonstration used the truth
labels as an oracle to name the clusters. Without ground truth that mapping is
unknown — and so is the number of clusters:

| clusters | accuracy with an oracle mapping |
|---|---|
| 2 | 0.812 |
| 3 | 0.802 |
| 4 | 0.874 |
| 5 | 0.879 |

Accuracy *rises* with the number of clusters, because with an oracle mapping each
extra cluster can only subdivide a class and become purer. **Accuracy cannot
validate the number of habitat classes.** That number comes from the management
question (how many units will be treated differently?), and the validation is the
ground-truth mapping.

### 3.7 Sample size, and the two optimistic biases

The learning curve, averaged over 30 draws:

| training samples | mean accuracy | mean kappa | mean rock recall |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 0.803 | 0.603 | 0.655 |
| 6 | 0.787 | 0.599 | 0.671 |
| 12 | 0.807 | 0.652 | 0.686 |
| 24 | 0.833 | 0.694 | 0.712 |
| 48 | 0.848 | 0.721 | 0.779 |
| 96 | 0.846 | 0.718 | 0.785 |

The overall accuracy moves only seven points across a 30-fold change in training
size, while the rare-class recall moves twelve points and kappa nineteen.
**Sample size buys rare-class performance, not headline accuracy**, and a study
that reports only overall accuracy will not notice that its rarest and often
most important habitat is unvalidated.

Two biases make these numbers optimistic relative to a real map:

1. **Spatial autocorrelation.** The demonstration's samples are independent by
   construction. Real ground-truth sites are clustered, so a validation set drawn
   at random from them shares information with the training set, and the reported
   accuracy is higher than the map's true accuracy. The fix is **spatially
   blocked cross-validation**: hold out whole regions, not random points.
2. **Ground-truth error.** The labels themselves come from video, grabs or
   acoustics with their own error rates. If the labels are 90% correct, the map's
   measured accuracy has a ceiling well below 100%, and differences of a few
   points between classifiers are noise.

## 4. Physical interpretation

- **Two properties are being measured, and they are not independent.** Impedance
  and roughness both change BS, and they can trade off: a rough soft seabed and a
  smooth hard one can return the same level at one angle.
- **The angular curve is the measurement.** That is why multibeam systems record
  per-beam backscatter and why the angular-response curve is a classification
  input in its own right.
- **The regime of validity is a result.** Reporting "n/a" for the rough rock at
  oblique angles is not a failure of the calculation; it is the finding that the
  coherent model does not apply.
- **A map has a resolution, and so does every attribute in it.** Slope, rugosity
  and BPI are all resolution dependent; a class boundary drawn at a fixed
  threshold moves with the grid.
- **Accuracy is a property of a validation design, not of a map.** The same map
  has different accuracies under different sampling schemes, and the honest
  number is the one from the design that matches how the map will be used.

## 5. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Fluid–fluid interfaces.** Sediment is treated as a fluid for the reflection
   coefficient; this fails for hard rock at non-normal incidence, where shear
   waves carry part of the energy and the elastic (Zoeppritz) problem applies.
2. **Small roughness for the coherence model**, $2k\sigma\cos\theta\lesssim1$.
   Beyond it the model has no meaning, as §3.2 shows at 100 kHz.
3. **Single scattering, no multiple reflections, no volume scattering.** Sandy
   sediments with a rough *subsurface* scatter from within the layer, which the
   surface model cannot represent.
4. **Flat seabed over the insonified footprint.** A sloping seabed changes the
   local grazing angle and therefore the measured BS; slope and BS are physically
   coupled, which is why using both as independent classifier features is a model
   choice.
5. **Lambertian $\mu_L$ constant.** In reality $\mu_L$ varies with substrate and
   with frequency. The value 0.02 here is a teaching constant, not a measurement.
6. **Terrain attributes are functions of the grid.** Slope is not a property of
   the seabed; it is a property of the surface model and the grid spacing.
7. **Class independence and Gaussian features for the classifier.** Real features
   are correlated and heavy-tailed; the classifier's accuracy on real data will
   differ from anything measured here.
8. **Independent ground truth.** The demonstration's confusions are properties of
   synthetic Gaussians. Real confusion arises from mixed substrates, transition
   zones and label error, none of which are represented.

## 6. Units and dimensional analysis

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| density | $\rho$ | $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ | |
| sound speed | $c$ | $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ | |
| impedance | $Z=\rho c$ | $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$ | |
| wavenumber | $k=2\pi f/c$ | $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ | $f$ in Hz |
| roughness | $\sigma$ | m | compared with $1/k$ |
| backscatter | $\mathrm{BS}$ | dB | $10\log_{10}$ of an intensity ratio |
| grazing angle | $\theta$ | deg or rad | never mixed |
| slope | — | deg or % | $\tan$ vs. angle |
| accuracy, kappa | — | dimensionless | 0–1 |

The dimensional trap in this subject is the decibel. A **factor of two in
amplitude is 6 dB**, and $R^2$ (a power ratio) is $20\log_{10}|R|$ for the
pressure coefficient. Writing $10\log_{10}|R|$ instead of $20\log_{10}|R|$ halves
every contrast, which is the same mistake as confusing amplitude and power.

## 7. Worked examples

All numbers are printed by `code/habitat_mapping_demo.py`.

### Example 1 — impedance contrast of two seabeds

$$R_{\text{mud}}=\frac{2.280-1.545}{2.280+1.545}=0.1922 \Rightarrow
20\log_{10}0.1922=-14.33\ \mathrm{dB},$$
$$R_{\text{rock}}=\frac{8.320-1.545}{8.320+1.545}=0.6868 \Rightarrow
-3.26\ \mathrm{dB}.$$

The power ratio is $0.6868^2/0.1922^2=12.8$, i.e. 11.1 dB.

### Example 2 — the roughness parameter and the coherence loss

At 100 kHz, $k=418.9\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$. For mud, $\sigma=2\ \mathrm{mm}$ at
$20^\circ$ grazing: $2k\sigma\cos\theta=2\times418.9\times0.002\times0.9397=1.574$,
and the loss is $10\log_{10}\exp(-1.574^2)=-10.8\ \mathrm{dB}$. For rock,
$\sigma=0.1$ m: $2k\sigma\cos\theta=78.7$ and the loss is $\sim-27000$ dB, which is
why the demonstration prints n/a: the model has left its range.

### Example 3 — the coherent backscatter curve of mud

The two effects run in opposite directions. At $20^\circ$ grazing,
$|R|=0.2460$ ($-12.18$ dB — *higher* than at normal incidence, because the
grazing angle is approaching the critical angle) and the coherence loss is
$-10.77$ dB, so $\mathrm{BS}=-12.18-10.77=-22.95$ dB. At $60^\circ$,
$|R|=0.1943$ ($-14.23$ dB) and the loss is $-3.05$ dB, giving $-17.28$ dB. At
$90^\circ$ the loss vanishes and $\mathrm{BS}=-14.33$ dB, exactly the impedance
level. The measured whole-curve slope over $20^\circ$–$60^\circ$ is
$+0.142\ \mathrm{dB\,deg^{-1}}$, and it is the *net* of two competing angle
dependencies — which is why the angular curve has to be computed rather than
guessed from a table of impedances.

### Example 4 — resolution bias

The analytic maximum slope of the mound is $8.62^\circ$. On a 100 m grid the
measured maximum is $7.94^\circ$ (92.1% of the truth); on a 200 m grid,
$6.72^\circ$ (77.9%). A class threshold at $7^\circ$ would classify the summit
region as "steep" on the 100 m grid and largely as "not steep" on the 200 m grid,
for the same seabed.

### Example 5 — accuracy, kappa and the rare class

With 40 training samples: overall accuracy $0.837$, $\kappa=0.704$, mud recall
$0.848$, sand recall $0.878$, rock recall $0.638$; sand precision $0.679$. The
majority-class baseline is $0.537$ accuracy with $\kappa\approx0$. Reporting
"84% accurate" without kappa and without the per-class recalls would hide both
the baseline and the missing rocks.

## 10. Real-world applications

- **Marine spatial planning.** Habitat maps define the units that are protected,
  licensed or surveyed further, so their class boundaries are regulatory.
- **Biodiversity and monitoring.** Habitat class is the stratification variable
  for biological sampling; a misclassified map biases every subsequent estimate.
- **Fisheries and benthic impact assessment.** Gear–habitat overlap is computed
  from these maps.
- **Engineering and cable routing.** Slope and substrate classes from the same
  attributes feed route selection
  ([15-02-02](../02-marine-geophysics-methods/02-multibeam-bathymetry.md)).
- **Gas hydrates and mineral prospecting.** Backscatter anomalies are one of the
  reconnaissance signals ([15-04-02](02-gas-hydrates.md),
  [15-04-03](03-marine-minerals.md)).

## Common pitfalls
1. **Confusing grazing angle with incidence angle from the normal.** Every
   angular statement inverts.
2. **Using a coherent-reflection model on a rough seabed.** At 100 kHz a
   decimetre-rough rock is far outside it; the answer is "the model does not
   apply", not a small number.
3. **Reporting overall accuracy as *the* accuracy.** With unequal priors the
   majority-class baseline can exceed 50%, and kappa is the minimum honest
   statistic.
4. **Hiding the rare class.** Rock recall 0.638 is invisible in an accuracy of
   0.837.
5. **Choosing the number of clusters by accuracy.** With an oracle mapping more
   clusters always score higher.
6. **Ignoring the grid when quoting slope.** A 22% error appeared between 5 m and
   200 m grids on the same mound.
7. **Validating with randomly chosen ground-truth stations.** Spatial
   autocorrelation makes the reported accuracy optimistic; block the validation.
8. **Treating slope and backscatter as independent evidence.** They are physically
   coupled through the local grazing angle.
