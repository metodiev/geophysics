## ★1

Sphere: $g_z(x) = Gm\,d/(x^2+d^2)^{3/2}$ with $m$ the excess mass (kg) and $d$
the centre depth (m). Line: $g_z(x) = 2G\lambda d/(x^2+d^2)$ with $\lambda$ the
line density (kg/m). Both in m s⁻².

$(x^2+d^2)^{-1}$, so the point-mass anomaly narrows more quickly.

## ★★5 *(verified)*

Measured from the demonstration: relative difference from the dipole falls from
$1.29\times10^{-1}$ at 200 m (body at 300 m, observer 200 m above) to
$1.96\times10^{-3}$ at 1600 m and $4.88\times10^{-4}$ at 3200 m. Interpolating,
1% is reached at roughly 1000–1200 m of separation, i.e. about
12–15 equal-volume radii.

The convergence exponent is 2 (the ratio falls by a factor $\approx4.0$ per
doubling of distance, measured as x4.07, x4.05, x4.01, x4.00). The reason it is
2 rather than 1: on the symmetry axis of this body the quadrupole term vanishes,
so the leading correction is the octupole ($r^{-5}$), and dividing by the dipole
($r^{-3}$) gives $r^{-2}$. Off-axis the quadrupole survives and the exponent
would be 1.

## ★★6

A line mass is the 1-D limit of a sheet; a semi-infinite sheet is obtained by
integrating line-mass contributions over the half-plane. For a semi-infinite
sheet of surface density $\sigma$ at depth $d$, the vertical attraction is
$g_z(x) = 2G\sigma[\pi/2 + \arctan(x/d)]$ — the classical step function, whose
derivative in $x$ is the line-mass kernel. Integration and differentiation are
inverse operations, which is why the family of solutions is generated by
integrating the Green's function over successively higher-dimensional sources.

---

## ★★7

The cylinder rule is the shallowest (largest constant, 2.000), so applying it to
a dyke-like (near-2-D) body gives the **smallest** depth estimate; applying the
sphere rule (constant 1.532842) gives a **larger** apparent depth. For the same
half-width the cylinder estimate is $1.532842/2.000 = 0.766$ times the sphere
estimate — about 23% shallower.

## ★★★8

Expected: the far-field relative error scales as $r^{-2}$ on the symmetry axis
(for a body symmetric about the axis in the required sense) and reaches 10% at
roughly 3–4 body radii, 5% at 5–6, 1% at 12–15. This is *slow*: many practical

point-dipole assumption is in error by tens of percent. Use the closed form (or
a fine quadrature of it) as the production reference, and reserve the far-field
approximation for feasibility estimates only.

## ★★★10

**Derivation.** For the semi-infinite sheet,
$g_z(x) = 2G\sigma[\pi/2 + \arctan(x/d)]$, and

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}g_z}{\mathrm{d}x}
= 2G\sigma\cdot\frac{1/d}{1+(x/d)^2} = \frac{2G\sigma\,d}{x^2+d^2},$$

which is maximal at $x = 0$ with value $2G\sigma/d$. Inverting gives
$d = 2G\sigma/(\mathrm{d}g_z/\mathrm{d}x)_{\max}$. Units:
$[G\sigma d^{-1}] = \mathrm{m^3kg^{-1}s^{-2}\cdot kg\,m^{-2}\cdot m^{-1}}
= \mathrm{s^{-2}}$, a gradient ✓. Note also *where* the maximum sits: directly
over the edge, so the rule locates the edge as well as estimating the depth.

**Numbers.** $\sigma = 400 \times 100 = 4\times10^{4}$ kg m⁻², $d = 150$ m:

$$\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}g_z}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)_{\max}
= \frac{2(6.6743\times10^{-11})(4\times10^{4})}{150}
= 3.5596\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}
= 3.560\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mGal\,m^{-1}}
= 3.560\ \mathrm{\mu Gal\,m^{-1}} .$$

**Station interval.** To see a change of at least the 0.01 mGal resolution across
the gradient peak, the interval $\Delta x$ must satisfy
$3.560\times10^{-3}\Delta x \ge 0.01$, i.e. $\Delta x \ge 2.8$ m. So a
**10 m station interval resolves the edge comfortably** (a 0.036 mGal change
between stations), while a 50 m interval (0.18 mGal) is more than adequate and a
1 m interval would be needlessly fine.

**Why a dyke gives two peaks, and the bias from misreading it.** A finite-width
dyke is the difference of two semi-infinite sheets, so its gradient is the
*difference* of two peaked functions centred on the two edges — hence two peaks,
separated by the dyke's width $w$. If the two peaks are unresolved and treated as
one broad step, the inferred depth is set by the *apparent* gradient maximum, which
is smaller than the true single-edge value because the two peaks partially cancel
between them. A smaller maximum in
$d = 2G\sigma/(\mathrm{d}g/\mathrm{d}x)_{\max}$ gives a **larger** inferred depth —
so misreading a dyke as a step biases the depth *upward*, potentially by a factor
approaching 2 when the separation is comparable to the depth. That is the same
shape-ambiguity quantified in the lesson's error budget, expressed in the gradient
domain.
