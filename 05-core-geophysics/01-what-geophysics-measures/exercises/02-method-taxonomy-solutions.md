## ★1

| Method | Source | Field type | Domain | Property |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gravity | natural | potential | surface/air/borehole/satellite | density |
| Magnetics | natural | potential | surface/air/borehole/satellite | magnetisation, susceptibility |
| Magnetotellurics | natural | diffusive | surface (electric + magnetic) | conductivity |
| Reflection seismics | controlled | wave | surface (mostly) | elastic moduli, density |
| GPR | controlled | wave + loss | surface/borehole | permittivity, conductivity |
| DC resistivity | controlled | potential (steady current) | surface/borehole | resistivity |

Note that resistivity is a controlled-source *potential* method — the current
flow is steady, so it sits between the potentials and the waves.

## ★2

$\Delta x < \lambda_x/2$, so $\lambda_x^\text{min} = 2\Delta x$. Since
$k = 2\pi/\lambda$, the maximum unambiguous wavenumber is
$k_N = 2\pi/(2\Delta x) = \pi/\Delta x$ rad m⁻¹.

## ★3

Resolution is controlled by the **bandwidth** (5–90 Hz, so $B \approx 85$ Hz),
not the dominant frequency. The 30 Hz peak sets the *penetration* character
(attenuation scales with frequency) and the general look of the wavelet, but two
interfaces can be separated only if the wavelet is short, and that requires a
broad band. A pure 30 Hz tone has essentially infinite bandwidth-limited
resolution.

## ★4 *(verified)*

$v = 1800$ m/s.
$B = 75$ Hz: $\Delta z = 1800/(4\cdot75) = 6.0$ m.
$B = 155$ Hz: $\Delta z = 1800/(4\cdot155) = 2.9$ m.

The second source helps only if the earth transmits 160 Hz to the target depth.
Because attenuation grows with frequency (and roughly exponentially with
depth), the usable band usually narrows with depth, so the theoretical gain is
often not realised. The honest statement is: better resolution requires the
high frequencies to *arrive*.

## ★5 *(verified)*

Aperture $L = 800$ m, $\Delta x = 4$ m. Shortest wavelength
$\lambda_\text{min} = 2\Delta x = 8$ m. Longest
$\lambda_\text{max} \approx L = 800$ m. Ratio $= 800/8 = 100$, i.e. two decades
of wavenumber.

A 12 m target has $\lambda = 12$ m, which is above $\lambda_\text{min} = 8$ m,
so it is in band and the survey is adequate *in the sampling sense*. Whether it
is detectable is a separate question (contrast and noise).

## ★6

*Source viewpoint:* natural MT sources are wideband, low-frequency signals
(roughly 10⁻⁴–10² Hz) generated by ionospheric and magnetospheric currents;
controlled sources cannot generate useful power at those low frequencies. In the
diffusive regime, low frequency means large skin depth, so MT reaches deep.

*Physics viewpoint:* depth reach is $\delta = \sqrt{2/\mu\sigma\omega}$, i.e.
governed by the *period*, not the amplitude. Even a very powerful
high-frequency transmitter cannot reach a depth much greater than its skin
depth.

**Physics decides.** The source determines the *available band*, but the skin
depth law determines what that band converts into in terms of depth.

## ★7

Aperture alone bounds the horizontal location uncertainty to roughly
$\pm\frac12\cdot$(aperture)/1 in the best case of a well-conditioned array, but
the controlling ratio is depth/aperture. Here $2\,\mathrm{km}/0.2\,\mathrm{km}
= 10$, an unfavourable ratio: horizontal location is poorly constrained and the
depth is far worse. A reflection survey with a 200 m aperture at 2 km depth
would likewise be limited, but for waves the resolution is set by the bandwidth
and the migration aperture, and a surface spread of hundreds of metres at 2 km
depth gives a Fresnel zone of order $\sqrt{\lambda z} \approx
\sqrt{50\cdot2000} \approx 320$ m. Both methods are poor at that depth-to-
aperture ratio; the honest conclusion is that neither is adequate without a
larger aperture or additional constraints.

## ★★★8

A strong answer gives a table: target → contrast (with source or stated
synthetic value) → method → predicted amplitude → noise → CNR → geometry →
resolution → conclusion. Marks are earned for (i) stating the geometry
correctly (2-D versus 3-D kernels), (ii) listing geological noise separately
from instrumental, (iii) naming the single dominant uncertainty, and (iv) not
quoting an unsourced property value. A common error is to use a 3-D point-mass
formula for a landfill, which is a wide tabular body; the 2-D or 2.5-D form
changes the answer by an order of magnitude.

## ★★★9

Target spectrum: suppose wavelengths 20–400 m must be recovered. Then
$\Delta x \le 10$ m and $L \ge 400$ m; over 400 m that is 40 receivers.

*Halving the count at fixed aperture:* $\Delta x$ doubles to 20 m, so
$\lambda_\text{min}$ rises to 40 m; wavelengths of 20–40 m are aliased into
apparent long wavelengths and appear as false broad structure.

*Halving the aperture at fixed count:* $\Delta x$ halves to 5 m (better short
wavelengths), but $\lambda_\text{max}$ falls to 200 m; structure longer than
200 m is absorbed into the regional trend and is not recoverable. The
observable symptom is a systematic long-wavelength bias in the derived model.

Neither change can be repaired in processing, because processing cannot create
wavenumbers that were never sampled.
