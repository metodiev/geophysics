# Autocorrelation, Power Spectral Density and Wiener-Khinchin

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Define** the autocovariance, the autocorrelation coefficient and the two
   conventions for the sample autocorrelation (biased and unbiased).
2. **Derive** the Wiener-Khinchin theorem and **verify** it numerically to
   machine precision.
3. **Derive** Parseval's relation and use it to check a computed PSD against the
   record variance.
4. **Explain** why the periodogram is unbiased but statistically inconsistent,
   and **measure** its variance remaining of order the mean as the record
   lengthens.
5. **Quantify** the bias-variance trade-off of Welch averaging, and **state** the
   resolution cost of a short segment.
6. **Choose** an estimator for a stated frequency resolution and variance
   requirement.

All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**. The traces below are
generated by the accompanying demonstration; no real recording is used and no
number is quoted from a published measurement.

## 1. Intuition: the same information twice

A random signal can be described in two equivalent ways. In the **time domain**
you say how well a sample predicts the sample a lag $\tau$ later: that is the
autocorrelation. In the **frequency domain** you say how the variance is
distributed over frequency: that is the power spectral density (PSD). The
Wiener-Khinchin theorem says these two descriptions are a Fourier pair, so
neither adds information — but they carry *different statistical difficulties*.
The autocorrelation is a smooth, well-behaved estimator; the raw PSD estimate at
a single frequency does not improve with record length. Both facts come out of
one derivation, and this lesson is that derivation plus its measured
consequences.

## 2. Precise definitions

### 2.1 Autocovariance and autocorrelation

For a wide-sense stationary process with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^{2}$,

$$C(\tau) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[(X(t)-\mu)(X(t+\tau)-\mu)\right],
\qquad \rho(\tau) = \frac{C(\tau)}{\sigma^{2}},$$

so $\rho(0) = 1$ and $|\rho(\tau)| \le 1$. For a finite record of $N$ samples at
interval $\Delta t$ there are two standard sample estimators:

$$\hat C_{\text{biased}}(k) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1-k} x_n x_{n+k},
\qquad
\hat C_{\text{unbiased}}(k) = \frac{1}{N-k}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1-k} x_n x_{n+k}.$$

The unbiased one divides by the number of terms actually used. It is unbiased
but its variance grows with $k$ (few terms remain) and it is not guaranteed
positive-definite, which can produce negative PSD estimates. The **biased**
estimator divides by $N$ throughout: it is biased low by the factor
$(N-k)/N$, but it is positive-definite, its bias is negligible for $k \ll N$,
and it is the estimator that is *exactly* the Fourier transform of the
periodogram. We use the biased form throughout, and say so.

### 2.2 Power spectral density

For a continuous process defined on all time, the **two-sided** PSD is

$$S_{2}(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} C(\tau) e^{-i2\pi f\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,
\qquad |f| < \infty,$$

with the convention that $\int_{-1/(2\Delta t)}^{1/(2\Delta t)} S_2(f)\,
\mathrm{d}f = \sigma^{2}$. The **one-sided** density folds the negative
frequencies onto the positive axis, $S_1(f) = 2S_2(f)$ for $0<f<f_N$ and
$S_1(0) = S_2(0)$, so that $\int_0^{f_N} S_1(f)\,\mathrm{d}f = \sigma^{2}$ with
$f_N = 1/(2\Delta t)$ the Nyquist frequency. The scipy convention
`scaling="density"` returns one-sided density. Units: if the signal is in volts,
$S_1$ is in $\mathrm{V^{2}\,Hz^{-1}}$; for a seismogram in
$\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, it is in $\mathrm{(m\,s^{-1})^{2}\,Hz^{-1}}$.

### 2.3 The periodogram

The periodogram is the modulus-squared DFT scaled to a density:

$$\hat S(f_k) = \frac{2}{f_s N}\left|\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_n e^{-i2\pi k n/N}
\right|^{2}, \qquad k = 0,\dots,N/2,$$

with $f_s = 1/\Delta t$, and the factors $1$ rather than $2$ at $k=0$ and
$k=N/2$. It is the *only* estimator a single record offers without further
assumptions, which is why it is the starting point and why its defects matter.

## 3. Derivations

### 3.1 Wiener-Khinchin

The DFT of the record is $X_k = \sum_n x_n e^{-i2\pi kn/N}$ and
$|X_k|^{2} = \sum_m c_m e^{-i2\pi km/N}$ where $c_m = \sum_n x_n x_{n+m}$ is the
circular autocovariance sum. The biased sample autocovariance is
$\hat C(k) = c_k/N$ for $0 \le k \le N-1$, so

$$|X_k|^{2} = N \sum_{m} \hat C(m)\,e^{-i2\pi km/N}.$$

Dividing by $f_s N$ and folding the negative frequencies gives the periodogram:

$$\boxed{\;\hat S(f_k) = 2\Delta t \sum_{m}\hat C(m) e^{-i2\pi k m/N}
\quad\text{(one-sided, density)}\;}$$

so the periodogram and the biased autocovariance are *exactly* a Fourier pair.
They are not two estimators of the same quantity that happen to agree; they are
the same number computed two ways. The demonstration verifies this on an
8192-sample record and finds a maximum difference of $9.71\times10^{-17}$ — the
round-off floor of double precision.

### 3.2 Parseval

Setting $k=0$ in the transform relation and integrating over the positive
frequencies,

$$\int_0^{f_N}\hat S(f)\,\mathrm{d}f = \frac{1}{N}\sum_n x_n^{2}
\quad\text{(for zero-mean records)},$$

i.e. the integral of the one-sided PSD is the variance. In discrete form the
integral is a sum with $\mathrm{d}f = f_s/N$. The demonstration confirms it: a
white record has variance $0.9853$ and $\sum \hat S\,\mathrm{d}f = 0.9854$; an
AR(1) record has variance $5.3904$ and integral $5.3956$ against the exact
$\sigma_w^{2}/(1-\varphi^{2}) = 5.2632$. The residual is the estimator's
statistical scatter, not an error in the identity.

### 3.3 Why the periodogram is unbiased

For a Gaussian white record, $\operatorname{Re}X_k$ and $\operatorname{Im}X_k$
are independent zero-mean Gaussians of variance $N\sigma^{2}/2$, so
$|X_k|^{2}$ is exponential with mean $N\sigma^{2}$ and
$\mathbb{E}[\hat S] = 2\sigma^{2}/f_s$. Averaging over many realisations, the
estimator is unbiased at every frequency. The demonstration measures
$0.02103$, $0.02070$, $0.01899$, $0.02112$ for record lengths of $1024$ to
$65536$ samples against the exact level $2\sigma^{2}/f_s = 0.02000$ — no trend
with length.

### 3.4 Why the periodogram is inconsistent

The same calculation gives $\operatorname{Var}(|X_k|^{2}) = (N\sigma^{2})^{2}$,
so the standard deviation of the estimate equals its mean and does *not* fall as
$N$ grows:

$$\frac{\operatorname{std}(\hat S)}{\mathbb{E}[\hat S]} \;\to\; 1 .$$

The demonstration measures $0.909$, $1.095$, $1.021$ and $0.977$ for the same
four record lengths: the ratio stays near one. **A longer record does not make a
single-frequency periodogram value more reliable**; it only places more
frequency bins in the same band. This is the statistical *inconsistency* of the
periodogram, and it is the reason every practical spectrum is smoothed or
averaged.

## 4. Welch averaging and the bias-variance trade-off

Welch's method splits the record into $K$ segments, windows each, computes a
periodogram for each and averages. For independent segments the variance of the
average is $1/K$ of a single periodogram's:

$$\frac{\operatorname{std}(\hat S_{\text{Welch}})}{\mathbb{E}[\hat S]}
\approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{K_{\text{eff}}}},$$

where $K_{\text{eff}}$ counts effectively independent segments (overlapping
segments are correlated, so $K_{\text{eff}}$ is smaller than the nominal count).
The demonstration measures, for 200 realisations of white noise at 10 Hz,

| segments $K$ | measured std/mean | $1/\sqrt{K_{\text{eff}}}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.591 | 0.577 |
| 2 | 0.375 | 0.378 |
| 4 | 0.247 | 0.258 |
| 8 | 0.189 | 0.180 |
| 16 | 0.125 | 0.126 |

The agreement is quantitative. What is paid for this is **resolution**. Each
segment of $N_{\text{seg}}$ samples has a frequency resolution of order
$2f_s/N_{\text{seg}}$ for a Hann window. The demonstration resolves two tones at
9.5 and 10.5 Hz (separation $1.0\ \mathrm{Hz}$) as follows:

| segment length | nominal resolution $2f_s/N_{\text{seg}}$ | valley/peak ratio | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 128 | 1.562 Hz | 1.068 | merged |
| 256 | 0.781 Hz | 0.381 | resolved |
| 1024 | 0.195 Hz | 0.0001 | resolved |
| 4096 | 0.049 Hz | 0.00002 | resolved |

When the nominal resolution exceeds the tone separation the two peaks become one.
Averaging bought variance reduction by discarding resolution.

## 5. Worked example: choosing an estimator

A record is $T = 81.92\ \mathrm{s}$ at $f_s = 100\ \mathrm{Hz}$ ($N = 8192$,
$\mathrm{d}f = 0.0122\ \mathrm{Hz}$). A survey needs the PSD between 0.5 and
40 Hz with a standard error below 20% of the mean, and must resolve a
spectral feature $0.8\ \mathrm{Hz}$ wide.

1. From $\text{std}/\text{mean} \approx 1/\sqrt{K_{\text{eff}}}$, a standard
   error below 20% needs $K_{\text{eff}} \gtrsim 25$. With 50% overlap the
   effective count is roughly half the nominal overlap count, so about
   $K \approx 50$ segments are required.
2. With 50% overlap, $K \approx 2N/N_{\text{seg}} - 1$, so
   $N_{\text{seg}} \approx 2N/(K+1) = 2(8192)/51 \approx 321$ samples.
3. But the resolution requirement needs
   $2f_s/N_{\text{seg}} < 0.8\ \mathrm{Hz}$, i.e.
   $N_{\text{seg}} > 2(100)/0.8 = 250$ samples. This is satisfied.
4. **Conclusion:** $N_{\text{seg}} \approx 256$ with 50% overlap
   ($K \approx 63$ nominal, $\text{df} = 0.39\ \mathrm{Hz}$) meets both
   requirements. Had the feature been $0.3\ \mathrm{Hz}$ wide, the two
   requirements would conflict and the honest answer would be that one record
   cannot deliver both — the standard error must be relaxed, a longer record
   obtained, or a multitaper estimator used.

## 6. Worked example: the AR(1) autocorrelation

The AR(1) process of
[lesson 05-01](01-stochastic-processes.md) has the exact autocorrelation
$\rho_k = \varphi^{|k|}$. The demonstration forms the biased estimator on 400
realisations of 10 000 samples and measures

| lag $k$ | measured $\rho_k$ | exact $\varphi^{k}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | $0.5888 \pm 0.0192$ | 0.5905 |
| 10 | $0.3461 \pm 0.0279$ | 0.3487 |
| 20 | $0.1177 \pm 0.0329$ | 0.1216 |
| 30 | $0.0386 \pm 0.0313$ | 0.0424 |

The maximum deviation of the ensemble-mean estimate from the exact curve over
$k \le 39$ is $0.0046$, and the scatter grows with lag exactly as the shrinking
number of terms predicts. **Always report the scatter**: a single realisation at
lag 30 could be $0.04$ or $0.00$ and both are consistent with the model.

## 7. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Wide-sense stationarity over the record.** The Wiener-Khinchin pair is
   defined for a stationary process. A trend makes the periodogram diverge at
   zero frequency and the autocovariance estimate meaningless; detrend first.
2. **The record is longer than the correlation time.** All the estimator
   statistics above assume $N \gg \tau_{\text{int}}/\Delta t$. For $1/f$ noise
   this fails and the effective count is much smaller than $N$; see
   [03 Colour of Noise](03-noise-colour.md).
3. **The DFT is taken over the whole record with no taper.** The exact
   equivalence of Section 3.1 holds only for a rectangular window and the biased
   autocovariance. Apply a taper and the periodogram is no longer the transform
   of the sample autocovariance — it is the transform of a *smoothed* version,
   which is a feature (leakage control, see
   [02-03 Windowing](../02-fourier-and-spectral-analysis/03-windowing-advanced.md)),
   not a defect.
4. **Gaussianity for the exponential distribution.** The $\text{std}/\text{mean}
   \to 1$ result uses Gaussian samples. For heavy-tailed noise the ratio can
   exceed one, and the estimates are noisier still.
5. **One-sided density convention.** Mixing density and power spectra, or
   one-sided and two-sided, changes every number by a factor of two or by
   $\mathrm{d}f$. State the convention with every reported PSD.

## 8. Units and dimensional analysis

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| lag | $\tau$, $k$ | s, samples | |
| autocovariance | $C(\tau)$ | (signal unit)$^{2}$ | product of two signals |
| autocorrelation | $\rho$ | dimensionless | $C/\sigma^{2}$ |
| two-sided PSD | $S_2$ | (unit)$^{2}\,\mathrm{Hz^{-1}}$ | $C \times \mathrm{s}$ |
| one-sided PSD | $S_1$ | (unit)$^{2}\,\mathrm{Hz^{-1}}$ | same |
| frequency bin | $\mathrm{d}f$ | Hz | $f_s/N$ |

Integrating $S_1$ over $[0,f_N]$ must return the variance; if it returns twice
the variance the fold at zero frequency has been applied twice, and if it
returns half, the negative frequencies were never folded.

## 12. Common pitfalls

1. **Treating a periodogram peak as a measured spectral line.** A single bin is
   a statistic with 100% scatter; only an average over bins or realisations is a
   measurement.
2. **Reporting "the" PSD without its convention.** One-sided or two-sided,
   density or power, and the value of $\mathrm{d}f$ must accompany it.
3. **Averaging more to chase variance and never checking resolution.** Welch
   always reduces the scatter and always broadens the peaks; the two are the
   same knob.
4. **Using the unbiased autocovariance for a spectrum.** It can produce negative
   PSD values because it is not positive-definite.
5. **Forgetting the taper.** A rectangular window leaks power from strong
   spectral lines into the whole band; the measured level away from a line is
   then leakage, not noise.
