# Noise Reduction Strategies in Practice

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Define** the signal-to-noise ratio in a way that is measurable, and state
   its units and reference.
2. **Derive** the $\sqrt{N}$ stacking gain and **state** the coherence condition
   that makes it hold.
3. **Measure** the SNR gain of band-pass filtering against its waveform
   distortion, and **choose** a passband from that trade-off.
4. **Explain** why coherent noise survives stacking, and **design** a remedy.
5. **Derive** the Wiener filter from the mean-square error and **measure** what
   over-smoothing costs.
6. **Measure** the robustness of the mean, the median and a trimmed mean against
   outliers, and **report** the price of the robust estimator.

All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**. The signal is a Ricker
wavelet and the noise is generated by the accompanying demonstration; no real
recording is used and no number is quoted from a published measurement.

## 1. Intuition: four levers, not one

"Noise reduction" is a collection of mostly independent tools, and confusing
them wastes effort. There are four levers.

- **Frequency.** If the signal occupies a band the noise does not, filter. The
  cost is distortion whenever the bands overlap.
- **Repetition.** If the signal repeats and the noise does not, stack. The gain
  is the largest available, and it is the only one that improves with more data.
- **Amplitude statistics.** If the noise is heavy-tailed, robust estimators
  reject the tail. The cost is a slightly larger variance under Gaussian noise.
- **Signal knowledge.** If a model of the signal and of the noise exists, the
  optimal linear filter (Wiener) uses both. The cost is the model.

The demonstration measures each lever's gain, its cost, and the conditions under
which it fails. The recurring theme is that **every gain is paid for**, and the
payment must be stated.

## 2. Precise definitions

### 2.1 The measurable SNR

Two definitions are used, and they are not interchangeable.

**Rms-amplitude SNR.** With $s$ the true signal and $n$ the noise (the record
minus the true signal), define

$$\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{dB}} = 20\log_{10}\frac{\operatorname{rms}(s)}
{\operatorname{rms}(n)} .$$

It is 20 log because it is a ratio of amplitudes; it is 10 log of the power
ratio. In a synthetic experiment $n$ is known exactly. In the field it is not,
and an SNR statement must name the substitute: a quiet window, a pre-arrival
window, or a matched-filter output.

**Detection SNR.** The peak of a matched filter output divided by the standard
deviation of that output on signal-free data. This is the quantity that controls
detectability, and it is not the same as the rms-amplitude SNR.

### 2.2 Distortion

A filter cannot improve a signal without changing it. Define the **distortion**
of a linear filter with impulse response $h$ applied to the true signal $s$ as

$$D = \frac{\lVert h*s - s\rVert_2}{\lVert s\rVert_2},$$

i.e. how far the filtered signal departs from the true signal, in the
$\ell_2$ sense. Reporting an SNR improvement without $D$ is reporting half a
result.

## 3. Derivations

### 3.1 The stacking gain

Let $M$ records be $x_j = s + n_j$ with the *same* signal $s$ in each and noise
$n_j$ independent between records, zero mean, variance $\sigma_n^{2}$ (with $n_j$
interpreted as the noise waveform). The stack is

$$\bar x = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M} x_j = s + \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M} n_j,$$

whose noise part has variance

$$\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_j n_j\right)
= \frac{1}{M^{2}}\sum_j \sigma_n^{2}
= \frac{\sigma_n^{2}}{M}.$$

The signal is unchanged at $\operatorname{rms}(s)$, so

$$\boxed{\;\text{SNR gain} = \frac{\operatorname{rms}(s)/\sqrt{\sigma_n^{2}/M}}
{\operatorname{rms}(s)/\sqrt{\sigma_n^{2}}}
= \sqrt{M}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
10\log_{10}M\ \text{dB}.\;}$$

The whole derivation is one line, and the *whole* condition is the independence
of the noise between records. If the noise is correlated between records the
sum does not average down, and Section 5 measures what is left.

### 3.2 The Wiener filter

Choose the linear filter $h$ minimising the mean-square error
$\mathbb{E}[(\hat s - s)^{2}]$ with $\hat s = h * x$, $x = s + n$, $s$ and $n$
zero mean and independent. In the frequency domain the error is

$$\mathcal{E} = \int \left|H(f)X(f) - S(f)\right|^{2} \mathrm{d}f ,$$

with expectation $\int\left[|1-H|^{2}S_{ss} + |H|^{2}S_{nn}\right]\mathrm{d}f$
using independence. Differentiating with respect to $H$ and setting to zero,

$$\boxed{\;H(f) = \frac{S_{ss}(f)}{S_{ss}(f) + S_{nn}(f)} .\;}$$

$H \to 1$ where the signal dominates and $H \to 0$ where the noise dominates:
the filter is a frequency-dependent *weight*, and the information it needs is
exactly the two spectra. Setting $H = 0$ wherever the ratio is below a threshold
returns a fixed band-pass; the Wiener filter differs only in tapering smoothly
instead of truncating.

### 3.3 Why robust estimators cost a little

For Gaussian noise the sample mean is the minimum-variance estimator, so any
robust alternative must be worse *in variance*. The asymptotic variance of the
sample median is

$$\operatorname{Var}(\text{median}) \;\longrightarrow\;
\frac{1}{4n\,f(m)^{2}}
= \frac{\pi}{2n}\sigma^{2}
\quad\text{for Gaussian } f ,$$

against $\sigma^{2}/n$ for the mean. The ratio is exactly $\pi/2 \approx 1.571$,
so the median's standard deviation is $\sqrt{\pi/2} \approx 1.253$ times the
mean's. **The median costs 25% more scatter in exchange for bounded influence
from outliers**, and that is a good trade whenever outliers are possible.

## 4. Worked example: filtering versus distortion

The demonstration builds a 1024-sample record at $f_s = 250\ \mathrm{Hz}$
(4.096 s) containing a 20 Hz Ricker wavelet at $t = 0.8\ \mathrm{s}$, and
band-limited noise of rms $0.5$ times the signal rms, giving an input
$\mathrm{SNR} = +6.02\ \mathrm{dB}$. Four Butterworth band-passes (fourth order,
applied zero-phase) give:

| passband | output SNR | distortion $D$ | pulse FWHM |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5–100 Hz | $+6.14$ dB | 0.0345 | 40.0 ms |
| 5–40 Hz | $+10.40$ dB | 0.0836 | 40.0 ms |
| 10–30 Hz | $+11.91$ dB | 0.3313 | 56.0 ms |
| 18–22 Hz | $+13.56$ dB | 0.8599 | 280.0 ms |

Read the table as a trade, not as a ranking. The wide band removes almost no
noise and leaves the pulse intact ($D = 0.035$). Narrowing to 10–30 Hz buys
$+5.9\ \mathrm{dB}$ of SNR at $D = 0.33$; the 18–22 Hz band buys another
$1.7\ \mathrm{dB}$ but distorts the pulse beyond recognition — the FWHM grows
from 40 ms to 280 ms, a factor of seven. **The honest way to choose a passband is
to plot SNR against $D$ and pick on the knee**, and never to report the SNR alone.

## 5. Worked example: stacking, and where it stops working

The demonstration generates 100 records with the same signal and independent
noise. The measured SNR gain against $10\log_{10}M$ is

| $M$ | 1 | 2 | 4 | 10 | 25 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| measured gain (dB) | 0.00 | 2.92 | 5.94 | 9.72 | 13.84 | 20.41 |
| $10\log_{10}M$ (dB) | 0.00 | 3.01 | 6.02 | 10.00 | 13.98 | 20.00 |

The agreement is within 0.3 dB at every level: the $\sqrt{M}$ gain is real and
quantitative.

Now add a 50 Hz tone of the **same phase** to every record — a power-line
harmonic, the standard coherent-noise example. The measured gains become

| $M$ | 1 | 2 | 4 | 10 | 25 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| measured gain (dB) | 0.00 | 2.28 | 4.25 | 6.22 | 7.34 | 7.98 |

The gain saturates near 8 dB instead of climbing to 20 dB, because the coherent
part of the noise is reproduced exactly by the average — it is *signal* as far as
the stack is concerned. **Coherent noise must be removed before stacking (notch
filter, modelling, or an array), or the stack will converge to signal plus
coherent noise.** This is the single most common failure of a stacking
workflow, and it is the reason
[06-02 Beamforming](../06-polarisation-and-array-processing/02-beamforming.md)
distinguishes coherent from incoherent array gain.

## 6. Worked example: robustness, and its price

The demonstration takes the same 100 records and puts a single-sample spike 30×
the signal rms into 30 of them.

| estimator | $\max\lvert\text{stack}-s\rvert$ | rms error |
|---|---|---|
| mean | 0.0267 | 0.0042 |
| median | 0.0175 | 0.0036 |
| trimmed mean (10%) | 0.0135 | 0.0030 |
| mean of the clean records | 0.0114 | 0.0029 |

The clean-record row is the noise floor: what the best possible stack would give
with no outliers at all. The **mean is more than twice the floor**; the median and
the trimmed mean are within about 20% of it. The robust estimators are not free
— they carry the $\sqrt{\pi/2}$ variance penalty of Section 3.3 — but at this
outlier rate that penalty (a few tens of per cent in the rms) is far smaller than
the outlier damage in the mean. Report the rate at which you applied the robust
estimator, or the reader cannot judge the trade.

## 7. Worked example: the Wiener filter and over-smoothing

With the true signal and the true noise available (as they are in a synthetic
test), $H(f) = S_{ss}/(S_{ss} + c\,S_{nn})$ is applied for several values of the
scale $c$:

| noise scale $c$ | output SNR | residual bias | retained rms |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | $+11.02$ dB | 0.281 | 0.950 |
| 2.00 | $+10.58$ dB | 0.296 | 0.896 |
| 5.00 | $+8.80$ dB | 0.363 | 0.794 |
| 0.50 | $+10.86$ dB | 0.286 | 0.987 |

At the correct scale the Wiener filter reaches $+11.02\ \mathrm{dB}$ with a
residual bias of $0.281$ — a slightly lower SNR than the 10–30 Hz band
($+11.91\ \mathrm{dB}$) but a *smaller* bias ($0.281$ against $0.331$). Its real
advantage is that it needs no hand-chosen passband. Over-estimating the noise
($c > 1$) is the instructive failure: the output SNR **falls** and the retained
rms falls with it, because the filter now suppresses part of the signal. The
bias column is the price of that suppression. **A filter tuned on a test record
by maximising SNR alone will always over-smooth**, because the true signal is not
in the objective.

## 8. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **The signal is repeatable and aligned.** Stacking gains $\sqrt{M}$ only for
   a common signal. Misalignment of a fraction of a period destroys the gain at
   the signal's dominant frequency; align before stacking.
2. **The noise is stationary over the stack.** A noise level that changes between
   records calls for weighted stacking, not an unweighted mean.
3. **The signal and noise are independent** for the Wiener derivation. If the
   noise estimate was made from the record itself (as it often is), the
   independence fails and the filter is optimistic.
4. **The spectra are known.** The Wiener filter uses $S_{ss}$ and $S_{nn}$. With
   estimates, the filter is only as good as they are; errors here are the
   over-smoothing failure measured above.
5. **Outliers are a modelling decision.** Robust estimators assume the outliers
   are *not* signal. A genuine impulsive arrival will be suppressed by a median
   stack. Decide which you are looking at before you choose the estimator.

## 9. Units and dimensional analysis

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| signal rms | $\operatorname{rms}(s)$ | signal unit | |
| SNR | $\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{dB}}$ | dB | $20\log_{10}$ of a ratio |
| stacking gain | $\sqrt{M}$ | dimensionless | $10\log_{10}M$ in dB |
| distortion | $D$ | dimensionless | norm ratio |
| Wiener filter | $H(f)$ | dimensionless | ratio of two PSDs |

$H$ is dimensionless because it is a ratio of two densities. If a computed
$H$ carries a unit, the two spectra were scaled differently — a very common bug
when one is a one-sided density and the other a two-sided power spectrum.

## 13. Common pitfalls

1. **Reporting an SNR gain without a distortion measure.** The two are one
   result; a narrow filter always wins on SNR alone.
2. **Stacking coherent noise.** A power-line harmonic or a common-mode
   instrument artefact stacks as if it were signal.
3. **Maximising SNR on a test record.** The optimum will over-smooth, because
   the objective does not contain the true signal.
4. **Using a median stack on impulsive signal.** Robustness is not free: it
   rejects anything that looks like an outlier, including a real arrival.
5. **Mixing PSD conventions** when forming a Wiener filter or an SNR, producing
   a factor-of-two error that looks like a physics result.
