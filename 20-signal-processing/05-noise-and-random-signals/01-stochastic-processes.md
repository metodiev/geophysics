# Stochastic Processes, Stationarity and Ergodicity

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Define** a stochastic process as a family of random variables indexed by
   time, and distinguish the ensemble average from the time average.
2. **State** strict-sense and wide-sense stationarity as precise conditions, and
   **test** for them on a finite record.
3. **Derive** $\operatorname{Var}(\bar x) = \sigma^{2}/N_{\text{eff}}$ with
   $N_{\text{eff}} = T/\tau_{\text{int}}$, and **compute** $\tau_{\text{int}}$ for
   an AR(1) process in closed form.
4. **Measure** the scatter of a time average over an ensemble and show it
   matches $\sigma/\sqrt{N_{\text{eff}}}$, not $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$.
5. **Explain** erdogicity as an extra assumption beyond stationarity, and
   **construct** a process that is stationary but not ergodic in the mean.
6. **Diagnose** non-stationarity from a split-half test of mean and variance.

All models and data in this module are **SYNTHETIC**. The processes below are
generated by the accompanying demonstration; no real recording is used and no
number is quoted from a published measurement.

## 1. Intuition: two different averages

A deterministic signal has a value you can predict. A random signal does not:
at a fixed time $t$ you cannot say what the voltage is, only how it is
distributed. That single change forces a new vocabulary, and the vocabulary is
built on one idea — there are **two** ways to average a random signal.

- The **ensemble average** at time $t$: imagine running the same experiment
  many times and averaging the value at $t$ across the runs. This is an average
  *across realisations*.
- The **time average** of one realisation: average the single record over a long
  span of time. This is an average *along one realisation*.

Both are useful. They are not the same thing, and the whole of this lesson is
about the conditions under which they agree. A signal-processing practitioner
almost always measures the second and reports it as if it were the first — that
step is an assumption, and it is checkable only against the physics that
generated the record.

## 2. Precise definitions

### 2.1 The process and its moments

A **stochastic process** $X(t)$ is a family of random variables indexed by time.
Fix one realisation and you obtain a **sample function** or **record**
$x(t)$. Fix $t$ and $X(t)$ is a random variable.

The first two moments are

$$\mu(t) = \mathbb{E}[X(t)], \qquad
C(t_1,t_2) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(X(t_1)-\mu(t_1)\right)
\left(X(t_2)-\mu(t_2)\right)\right].$$

For a signal at a single sensor we usually add the modelling assumption that
the mean is constant in a *window* and work with the **autocovariance function**

$$C(\tau) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(X(t)-\mu\right)\left(X(t+\tau)-\mu\right)\right],$$

whose value at lag zero is the variance $\sigma^{2} = C(0)$ and whose normalised
form $\rho(\tau) = C(\tau)/\sigma^{2}$ is the **autocorrelation coefficient**.

### 2.2 Stationarity

**Strict-sense (strong) stationarity** means the joint distribution of
$X(t_1),\dots,X(t_n)$ is identical to that of $X(t_1+h),\dots,X(t_n+h)$ for
every $n$ and every shift $h$. It is rarely verifiable from data.

**Wide-sense (weak) stationarity** requires only

$$\mu(t) = \mu \quad\text{constant}, \qquad
C(t_1,t_2) = C(t_1-t_2) \quad\text{only a function of the lag.}$$

Wide-sense stationarity is what spectral analysis and filtering theory actually
need. Gaussian processes are special: for them wide-sense stationarity implies
strict stationarity, because a Gaussian is fully specified by its first two
moments.

### 2.3 Ergodicity

**Ergodicity in the mean** means the time average of a single realisation
converges to the ensemble mean:

$$\bar x_T \equiv \frac{1}{T}\int_0^{T} x(t)\,\mathrm{d}t
\;\longrightarrow\; \mu \quad \text{as } T \to \infty
\quad\text{(for almost every realisation)}.$$

Ergodicity is **not** implied by stationarity. It is a separate assumption, and
Section 6 builds a stationary process for which it fails.

## 3. Estimators and how noisy they are

### 3.1 The sample mean

For a record sampled at interval $\Delta t$, $\bar x = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}
x_n$ with $N\Delta t = T$. For independent samples $\operatorname{Var}(\bar x) =
\sigma^{2}/N$. For a correlated process the samples are not independent, and the
correct result is the classical one:

$$\boxed{\;\operatorname{Var}(\bar x)
= \frac{\sigma^{2}}{N_{\text{eff}}},
\qquad
N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{T}{\tau_{\text{int}}},
\qquad
\tau_{\text{int}} = \Delta t\left(1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\rho_k\right).\;}$$

$\tau_{\text{int}}$ is the **integrated autocorrelation time**: the width of a
rectangular window that carries the same "memory" as the actual autocorrelation.
The derivation is one line of algebra. Write

$$\operatorname{Var}(\bar x) = \frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{n,m} C(n-m)
= \frac{\sigma^{2}}{N}\sum_{k=-(N-1)}^{N-1}
\left(1-\frac{|k|}{N}\right)\rho_k
\;\approx\; \frac{\sigma^{2}}{N}\left(1 + 2\sum_{k\ge 1}\rho_k\right),$$

using $\rho_0 = 1$ and neglecting the triangular taper for $N \gg
\tau_{\text{int}}/\Delta t$. The bracketed factor is exactly
$\tau_{\text{int}}/\Delta t$, which gives the boxed result.

### 3.2 The AR(1) process in closed form

The first-order autoregressive process is the standard teaching model:

$$x_n = \varphi\,x_{n-1} + w_n, \qquad w_n \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_w^{2}),
\qquad |\varphi| < 1 .$$

Taking variances at stationarity, $\sigma^{2} = \varphi^{2}\sigma^{2} +
\sigma_w^{2}$, so

$$\sigma^{2} = \frac{\sigma_w^{2}}{1-\varphi^{2}},
\qquad \rho_k = \varphi^{|k|}.$$

The integrated time follows from the geometric sum,

$$\tau_{\text{int}} = \Delta t\left(1 + 2\sum_{k\ge1}\varphi^{k}\right)
= \Delta t\left(1 + \frac{2\varphi}{1-\varphi}\right)
= \Delta t\,\frac{1+\varphi}{1-\varphi},$$

and the lag at which the autocorrelation falls to $1/e$ is

$$\tau_{1/e} = -\frac{\Delta t}{\ln \varphi}.$$

Note that $\tau_{1/e} < \tau_{\text{int}}$: the integrated time counts the
entire exponential tail, not just the $1/e$ point.

## 4. Worked example: the demonstration model

The demonstration uses $\varphi = 0.90$, $\sigma_w = 1$, $\Delta t = 1\ \mathrm{s}$
and $N = 10\,000$ samples ($T = 10\,000\ \mathrm{s}$), with an ensemble of 400
realisations.

**Variance.** $\sigma^{2} = 1/(1-0.81) = 5.2632$, so $\sigma = 2.2942$. The
demonstration measures a mean per-record variance of $5.2732 \pm 0.2290$ (the
scatter is one standard deviation over the 400 records) — the estimator is
unbiased and the scatter is the estimator's own variance.

**Correlation time.**

$$\tau_{\text{int}} = 1\times\frac{1.90}{0.10} = 19.00\ \mathrm{s},
\qquad
\tau_{1/e} = -\frac{1}{\ln 0.90} = 9.49\ \mathrm{s}.$$

**Effective sample size and standard error.**

$$N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{10\,000}{19.00} = 526.3,
\qquad
\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N_{\text{eff}}}} = \frac{2.2942}{22.94} = 0.1000 .$$

The naive white-noise formula would give $\sigma/\sqrt{N} = 2.2942/100 = 0.0229$
— **too small by $\sqrt{\tau_{\text{int}}/\Delta t} = \sqrt{19} = 4.359$**. The
demonstration measures the actual scatter of the time average over the ensemble
as $0.1019$, which matches the corrected value $0.1000$ and contradicts the
naive one. For a $1/f$-like process, ignoring the correlation time can
understate the uncertainty of a mean by an order of magnitude.

## 5. Testing stationarity on a record

Stationarity is a property of the process, but you only ever see a record. The
practical test is a **split-half comparison**: compute the statistic on the first
half and on the second half and ask whether the difference is large compared with
the scatter you expect from finite-sample noise.

For the stationary AR(1), the demonstration measures a second-minus-first-half
mean of $-0.0009 \pm 0.2137$ and a variance difference of $-0.0084 \pm 0.4839$
(mean $\pm$ scatter over the ensemble). Both are consistent with zero: the
record has no way to tell the halves apart.

For a **random walk** $x_n = \sum_{k\le n} w_k$ the outcome is different. Its
variance grows linearly in time, $C(0) \propto n\sigma_w^{2}$, so the two halves
*differ systematically*. The demonstration measures first-half variance
$2364.4$ and second-half variance $6581.9$, a ratio of $2.784$: the record grows
in amplitude, and no amount of averaging removes that. A random walk is a
canonical **non-stationary** process — which is precisely why the colour of
noise matters, the subject of
[03 Colour of Noise](03-noise-colour.md).

## 6. A stationary process that is not ergodic

Take $X(t) = A + \eta(t)$ where $A \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ is drawn **once per
realisation** and held constant, and $\eta(t)$ is white noise of standard
deviation $0.3$. The process is wide-sense stationary: the mean is zero and the
autocovariance depends only on the lag. But the time average is

$$\bar x_T = A + \bar\eta_T \;\longrightarrow\; A \neq 0 =
\mathbb{E}[X(t)].$$

The time average converges to the per-record offset, not to the ensemble mean.
The demonstration confirms it: the ensemble mean is $-0.0418$ (consistent with
zero), while the scatter of the time average across records is $1.0431$, and
**halving the record length leaves it at $1.0431$**. The scatter does not shrink
with $T$ because it is set by $\operatorname{Var}(A) = 1$, which no averaging over
time can remove. This is the archetype of a process that is stationary but not
ergodic, and it is exactly what a slowly drifting sensor baseline looks like.

## 7. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Wide-sense stationarity within the analysis window.** Every estimator in
   Section 3 assumes $\mu$ and $C(\tau)$ do not change over the window. A trend,
   a seasonal cycle or a step violates it; see
   [08-01 Detrending and Deseasonalising](../08-timeseries-analysis-geophysics/01-detrending-seasonality.md).
2. **The integrated time is finite.** $\tau_{\text{int}}$ exists only if
   $\sum_k|\rho_k|$ converges. For exact $1/f$ noise the sum diverges
   logarithmically, and $N_{\text{eff}}$ must be replaced by a slower,
   $\ln T$-dependent expression. The formula is a good approximation only while
   $\tau_{\text{int}} \ll T$.
3. **The mean is the only statistic being averaged.** Ergodicity is a property
   *per statistic*: a process can be ergodic in the mean and not in the variance.
   The random-offset example is ergodic in the variance but not in the mean.
4. **The ensemble is exchangeable.** An ensemble built by re-running the same
   numerical generator proves the *estimator* behaves as predicted; it does not
   prove that a field recording is stationary. That is a physical claim requiring
   physical evidence.
5. **Gaussianity is not required** for the variance formulas, which use only
   second moments, but the interpretation of a single standard deviation as a
   68% interval does require approximate Gaussianity.

## 8. Units and dimensional analysis

| Quantity | Symbol | Unit | Check |
|---|---|---|---|
| sampling interval | $\Delta t$ | s | |
| record length | $T = N\Delta t$ | s | |
| variance | $\sigma^{2}$ | (signal unit)$^{2}$ | |
| autocorrelation time | $\tau_{\text{int}}$ | s | $\Delta t$ times a dimensionless sum |
| effective count | $N_{\text{eff}}$ | dimensionless | s / s |
| standard error | $\sigma/\sqrt{N_{\text{eff}}}$ | signal unit | (unit)$^{2}$ then square root |

$\tau_{\text{int}}$ carries the unit of time because $\rho_k$ is dimensionless;
if a computed $\tau_{\text{int}}$ comes out in $\mathrm{s^{2}}$ or in samples,
a factor of $\Delta t$ has been misplaced.

## 12. Common mistakes

1. **Using $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$ on correlated data.** It understates the
   uncertainty; the correction is a factor $\sqrt{\tau_{\text{int}}/\Delta t}$.
2. **Confusing stationarity with ergodicity.** The random-offset process is
   stationary and not ergodic; the two must be stated separately.
3. **Testing stationarity with one record and no scatter.** A split-half
   difference is only meaningful against its ensemble scatter.
4. **Averaging across a record that contains a trend.** The estimator is then
   estimating the mean of the trend, not of the process.
5. **Assuming a single realisation proves an ensemble property.** One record is
   one draw; the demonstration always reports a mean and a scatter.
