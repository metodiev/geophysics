# Project: Earthquake Catalogue Statistics and Hazard Inputs

## Learning objectives

By the end of this project you will be able to:

1. **Estimate** the Gutenberg-Richter $b$-value and the magnitude of completeness
   from a catalogue, with their uncertainties.
2. **Decluster** a catalogue with a Gardner-Knopoff window and **measure** what
   declustering does to the rate and to $b$.
3. **Demonstrate** that window declustering biases $b$ low by removing many small
   aftershocks while retaining their mainshocks.
4. **Compute** a recurrence rate $\lambda(M \ge M_0)$ by extrapolating the fitted
   frequency-magnitude relation, and **propagate** $\sigma_b$ into it.
5. **Convert** a rate into a Poisson probability of exceedance over a stated
   exposure period.
6. **State** the four assumptions that a Poisson hazard input makes, and
   **identify** which of them the project's own catalogue violates.
7. **Report** a hazard input as a rate with an uncertainty, a completeness
   statement, a declustering rule and an exposure period.

The catalogue in this project is **SYNTHETIC**, generated with chosen clustering
by [`code/catalogue_hazard_demo.py`](./code/catalogue_hazard_demo.py). Real
catalogues are available from the USGS ANSS Comprehensive Earthquake Catalog at
<https://earthquake.usgs.gov/data/comcat/>; no number below comes from one.

## 1. The question

*What recurrence rate does this catalogue support, and how much of it is a choice
about how the catalogue was processed?*

Every probabilistic seismic hazard calculation begins with a rate. The rate comes
from a catalogue, and the catalogue must first be made *stationary* (earthquakes
are clustered) and *complete* (small events are missing). Both steps are choices
with quantitative consequences, and the demonstration measures them: declustering
cuts the rate of $M \ge 6$ from $0.68690$ to $0.23416\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ — a factor
of $2.9$ — while the assumed completeness magnitude moves it from $0.23416$ to
$0.32758\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ as $M_c$ goes from 3.0 to 4.0.

## 2. The data

**Synthetic catalogue (SYNTHETIC).** 4000 background events over 50 years in a
$200\ \mathrm{km}\times200\ \mathrm{km}$ box with $b = 1.00$ and magnitudes above
$M_c = 3.0$, plus Omori-decaying aftershock sequences generated by the 200 largest
events with a magnitude-dependent productivity. The resulting catalogue holds
$16\,589$ events from $M = 3.00$ to $7.68$, with 173 events at $M \ge 5$ and 19 at
$M \ge 6$, and a mean rate of $331.8\ \mathrm{events\,yr^{-1}}$ above $M_c$.

**Provenance.** SYNTHETIC. The clustering law is chosen, so the "aftershocks" are
labelled by construction and the declustering algorithm's behaviour is measurable
rather than assumed.

## 3. The project specification

**Steps.**

1. Estimate $M_c$ by maximum curvature and the $b$-value by maximum likelihood with
   the binning correction; report both with their uncertainties.
2. Decluster with the Gardner-Knopoff time and distance windows, and report the
   fraction retained and the retention by magnitude band.
3. Refit $b$ and $a$ on the declustered catalogue; report the rate
   $\lambda(M \ge M_0)$ for $M_0 = 6$ and $7$, with the uncertainty propagated from
   $\sigma_b$.
4. Compute the Poisson probability of at least one exceedance over 30 and 50 years,
   with the interval implied by $\sigma_b$.
5. Refit with $M_c$ raised to 3.5 and 4.0 and report how $b$ and $\lambda$ move.
6. Write the report: the completeness statement, the declustering rule, the rate
   with its uncertainty, the exposure period, and the assumptions the Poisson
   model makes that the catalogue violates.

**Deliverable.** The $b$-value and $M_c$ estimates, the declustering table, the
rate table, the probability table, and the assumptions paragraph.

**Success criterion.** Every rate carries an uncertainty and a completeness
statement; the declustering rule is quoted; the probability is stated for a named
magnitude and exposure period; the Poisson assumption is discussed rather than
applied silently.

## 4. The frequency-magnitude relation and its estimator

$$\log_{10}N(\ge M) = a - bM,
\qquad
\hat b = \frac{\log_{10}e}{\overline{M} - (M_c - \Delta M/2)},
\qquad
\sigma_b \approx \frac{b}{\sqrt{N}},$$

and the recurrence rate above a threshold follows from the fitted $a$ and $b$:

$$\lambda(M \ge M_0) = 10^{\,a - bM_0}\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}},
\qquad
a = \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{N}{T}\right) + b\,M_c .$$

The Poisson probability of at least one exceedance in an exposure $T_e$ is

$$P(\ge 1) = 1 - e^{-\lambda T_e},$$

and the uncertainty from $\sigma_b$ alone propagates as
$\sigma_\lambda / \lambda = \ln 10 \cdot M_0 \cdot \sigma_b$.

Measured on the demonstration's catalogue:

| catalogue | $N$ | $\hat b$ | $\sigma_b$ | $\lambda(M \ge 6)$ (yr⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| raw | 16 589 | $0.8947$ | $0.0069$ | $0.68690$ (1 per 1.5 yr) |
| declustered | 3 794 | $0.8369$ | $0.0136$ | $0.23416$ (1 per 4.3 yr) |

## 5. What declustering does, and why $b$ falls

Keeping the time **and** distance windows, the catalogue collapses from $16\,589$
events to **3794** — $22.9\%$ retained, $77.1\%$ classified as aftershocks. The
retention is magnitude-dependent: 83 of 173 events at $M \ge 5$ survive, and 17 of
19 at $M \ge 6$.

That magnitude dependence is the reason $\hat b$ **falls** from $0.8947$ to
$0.8369$ rather than staying put. Window declustering removes everything inside a
mainshock's window, and aftershock populations are dominated by small events, so the
filter preferentially deletes small magnitudes while keeping the mainshocks that
caused the deletions. The result is a catalogue with proportionally more large
events and therefore a *shallower* frequency-magnitude slope. The effect is a
**known artefact of window declustering**, not a property of the seismicity, and it
is the reason declustered $b$-values are not directly comparable with raw ones.

Two further observations:

- The rate changes by a factor of **2.9**, far more than $b$ does. Declustering is
  primarily a **rate** operation; its effect on the slope is a side effect.
- Both estimates of $b$ are below the generating value of 1.00 — the raw one
  because magnitude-dependent productivity correlates large mainshocks with many
  aftershocks, and the declustered one because of the retention bias above. **The
  generating $b$ is known here only because the catalogue is synthetic**; on real
  data the residual bias is unmeasurable without a model.

## 6. Completeness moves everything

| assumed $M_c$ | $N$ | $\hat b$ | $\lambda(M \ge 6)$ (yr⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.0 | 3794 | $0.8369$ | $0.23416$ |
| 3.5 | 1263 | $0.7963$ | $0.25805$ |
| 4.0 | 418 | $0.7034$ | $0.32758$ |

Raising $M_c$ by one unit changes the rate by $+40\%$ and $b$ by $-16\%$, in
opposite directions, because the two enter $\lambda = 10^{a - bM_0}$ through both
$a$ and $b$. The maximum-curvature estimate on this catalogue is exactly the
generating $M_c = 3.0$, so the centre column is the correct one — but only because
the catalogue was generated with a sharp completeness threshold. Real catalogues
have a gradual detection fall-off, and the choice of $M_c$ is then the dominant
uncertainty in the whole calculation, which is why modern practice fits $M_c$ by
the goodness-of-fit or $b$-value-stability methods rather than by the mode of the
histogram.

## 7. The hazard input, and the assumptions in it

With the declustered fit ($b = 0.8369 \pm 0.0136$):

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| $\lambda(M \ge 6)$ | $0.23416 \pm 0.04395\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ (1 per 4.3 yr) |
| $\lambda(M \ge 7)$ | $0.03409 \pm 0.00747\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ (1 per 29 yr) |
| $P(\ge 1,\ M \ge 6,\ 30\ \mathrm{yr})$ | $0.9991$ ($0.9967$–$0.9998$) |
| $P(\ge 1,\ M \ge 7,\ 30\ \mathrm{yr})$ | $0.6404$ ($0.5501$–$0.7126$) |
| $P(\ge 1,\ M \ge 7,\ 50\ \mathrm{yr})$ | $0.8182$ ($0.7359$–$0.8748$) |

The $M \ge 6$ probabilities are saturated and therefore uninformative — a
consequence of extrapolating a shallow $b$ from a rate of
$331.8\ \mathrm{events\,yr^{-1}}$. The $M \ge 7$ numbers are the ones worth quoting,
and the interval from $\sigma_b$ alone already spans $0.55$ to $0.71$ for a 30-year
exposure.

**Four assumptions the Poisson input makes.**

1. **Independence.** Earthquake occurrence is clustered; the demonstration's
   catalogue is $77\%$ aftershocks. Declustering removes the clustering from the
   *rate* but not from the *process*, and a catalogue that has been declustered is
   the only kind for which a Poisson model is even approximately appropriate.
2. **Stationarity.** The rate must not change over the exposure; a catalogue whose
   detection improved, or whose region is still relaxing from a large event,
   violates this.
3. **Completeness.** $\lambda$ is extrapolated *below* the catalogue's detection
   threshold and *above* it; the first is the $b$-value's extrapolation and the
   second is bounded by the record length. A 50-year probability extrapolated from
   a 50-year catalogue is a fit, not a forecast.
4. **An infinite, homogeneous source region.** The box here is a choice; the real
   spatial distribution of sources, and the distance from each to the site, is what
   a full hazard calculation adds — and it dominates the result.

## 8. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **A single $b$-value for the region and period.** Mixing subduction, crustal and
   induced populations gives an average of different physics.
2. **One magnitude scale.** `mb`, `Ms` and `Mw` must not be mixed.
3. **A declared completeness threshold and its period of validity.** Detection
   improves with time, so $M_c$ is time-dependent in most real networks.
4. **Physical magnitude bounds.** The unbounded GR relation predicts absurd rates
   at the largest magnitudes, which is why it is always truncated by a maximum
   magnitude $M_\mathrm{max}$; the demonstration omits it and the $M \ge 7$
   probabilities are therefore upper bounds in the tail.
5. **A declustering rule with stated parameters.** GK's empirical windows are one
   of several; ETAS-based and nearest-neighbour methods give different rates.

## The honest summary

The project's product is a rate and its provenance. From a synthetic catalogue of
$16\,589$ events the declustered fit gives $b = 0.8369 \pm 0.0136$ and
$\lambda(M \ge 6) = 0.23416 \pm 0.04395\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$, against a raw
$0.68690\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$; raising $M_c$ from 3.0 to 4.0 raises the rate to
$0.32758\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}$ and lowers $b$ to $0.7034$. Declustering is a rate
operation whose side effect lowers $b$ by preferentially removing small
aftershocks, and the $M \ge 7$ Poisson probabilities — $0.6404$ in 30 years,
$0.8182$ in 50 — carry an interval from $\sigma_b$ alone that is a quarter of
their value. The transferable practice is to quote the rate with its completeness,
its declustering rule and its exposure period, and to name the assumptions the
Poisson model makes rather than inheriting them silently.

## Common pitfalls

1. **A rate without a completeness statement.** Here it moves the rate by $40\%$.
2. **A declustered $b$ compared with a raw $b$.** The declustering changed it.
3. **Extrapolating a rate below $M_c$ without saying so.** The extrapolation is the
   $b$-value's, and it is a model.
4. **Applying Poisson to a clustered catalogue.** $77\%$ of this one is
   aftershocks.
5. **Ignoring $M_\mathrm{max}$.** An unbounded GR law predicts more large events
   than a finite Earth can supply.

## Where this is used

The rate computed here is the first input of every probabilistic seismic hazard
analysis, and the same machinery appears in volcanic and induced-seismicity
monitoring. The GNSS strain rates of
[25-03-05](../03-advanced-projects/05-gnss-strain-project.md) and the InSAR rates of
[25-04-03](./03-insar-deformation-project.md) are the independent constraints that
a seismicity-derived moment rate must be compared against, and that comparison is
the standard check in a hazard study. In professional practice a hazard report's
first table is exactly the table of §7, with its declustering rule on the same
page.
