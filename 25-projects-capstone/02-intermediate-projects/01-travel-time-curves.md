# Project: Travel-Time Curves and 1-D Velocity Structure

## Learning objectives

By the end of this project you will be able to:

1. **Derive** the direct-wave and head-wave travel-time branches for a layered
   model from Snell's law and **state** each branch's validity.
2. **Compute** the crossover distance at which the head wave becomes the first
   arrival, and **explain** why it does not exist for a velocity inversion.
3. **Invert** a picked travel-time curve by least squares, recovering a layer
   velocity from the branch slope and an interface depth from its intercept.
4. **Propagate** the pick uncertainty into the recovered velocity and depth, and
   **distinguish** the statistical uncertainty from the model-assumption error.
5. **Measure** the velocity–depth trade-off explicitly, by re-deriving the depth
   for three values of the assumed overburden velocity.
6. **State** what a travel-time curve determines well (slope), badly (intercept)
   and not at all (the velocity of a layer that is never a first arrival).
7. **Report** an inverted 1-D model as a set of values with uncertainties, a
   stated forward model, and a stated depth range of validity.

The model and the picks used below are **SYNTHETIC**: a two-layer
crust-over-mantle model with chosen velocities, generated by
[`code/travel_time_curves_demo.py`](./code/travel_time_curves_demo.py). Real
travel times are freely available — earthquake origin and phase data from the
USGS FDSN event service at
<https://earthquake.usgs.gov/fdsnws/event/1/query>, and station metadata from
<https://service.iris.edu/fdsnws/station/1/query> — and the *project* is
performed on those, but no number in this lesson is a measured travel time.

## 1. The question

*Given arrival times as a function of distance, what can you say about the
velocity structure beneath the receivers?*

This is the first genuinely **inverse** project in the folder. The data are
times; the model is a set of velocities and thicknesses; and the mapping from
model to data is a set of straight-line branches. The project's result is not a
number but a *statement of conditioning*: the slope of a branch determines a
velocity to a fraction of a percent, while the intercept determines a depth only
if you already know the velocity above it. Both facts are visible in one
least-squares fit.

## 2. The data

**The model (SYNTHETIC).** A crust of velocity
$V_1 = 5.90\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and thickness $H_1 = 32.0\ \mathrm{km}$ over a
half-space of $V_2 = 8.05\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. These are round teaching values
in the range of a continental crust over a mantle lid.

**The picks (SYNTHETIC).** Sixteen first-arrival times from 200 to 500 km at
20 km spacing, generated from the head-wave branch with Gaussian noise of
standard deviation $0.05\ \mathrm{s}$ — a realistic hand-pick precision for a
clean local arrival.

**Real data for the project itself.** Broadband phase arrivals and hypocentres
are retrieved from the USGS FDSN event service at
<https://earthquake.usgs.gov/fdsnws/event/1/query>, with station coordinates from
<https://service.iris.edu/fdsnws/station/1/query>. The project's picks are made
by the reader on real waveforms; the demonstration's picks are synthetic.

## 3. The project specification

**Steps.**

1. State the forward model: what each branch is, and the offset range over which
   it is the first arrival.
2. For the synthetic model, tabulate $T(X)$ for both branches at a grid of
   offsets and identify the crossover.
3. Select the picks belonging to each branch by offset range, fit a straight line
   to each, and convert slope to velocity and intercept to depth.
4. Compute the parameter uncertainties from the fit residuals and the design
   matrix, and report each recovered parameter with its uncertainty.
5. Repeat the depth inversion with three different assumed values of the
   overburden velocity and report the spread.
6. Write the report: the model, the data selection, the recovered parameters, the
   uncertainty budget and the validity range.

**Deliverable.** A travel-time plot with fitted branches, a table of recovered
velocities and depths with uncertainties, and the trade-off table of step 5.

**Success criterion.** Each branch is fitted over a stated offset range; each
velocity is quoted with its uncertainty; the depth is quoted **with the assumed
overburden velocity**; the crossover and the validity range are stated.

## 4. The two branches

**Direct wave.** Along the surface at speed $V_1$:

$$T_\text{direct}(X) = \frac{X}{V_1}.$$

**Head wave.** A ray that grazes the interface at the critical angle
$\theta_c = \arcsin(V_1/V_2)$ travels down at $V_1$, along at $V_2$, and back up
at $V_1$. The two down-and-up legs have combined horizontal length
$2H_1\tan\theta_c$ and take $2H_1/(V_1\cos\theta_c)$, while the along-interface
leg takes $(X - 2H_1\tan\theta_c)/V_2$. Adding and simplifying gives

$$T_\text{head}(X) = \frac{X}{V_2} + \frac{2H_1\cos\theta_c}{V_1}
= \frac{X}{V_2}
+ \frac{2H_1\sqrt{V_2^{2} - V_1^{2}}}{V_1 V_2},$$

whose slope is the **reciprocal of the refractor velocity** and whose intercept

$$t_i = \frac{2H_1\sqrt{V_2^{2} - V_1^{2}}}{V_1 V_2}$$

carries the depth. With the demonstration's values,
$\theta_c = \arcsin(5.90/8.05) = \arcsin(0.7329) = 47.13°$ and
$t_i = 7.3797\ \mathrm{s}$.

## 5. First arrivals and the crossover

Setting $T_\text{direct} = T_\text{head}$:

$$\frac{X}{V_1} = \frac{X}{V_2} + t_i
\;\Longrightarrow\;
X_c = t_i\,\frac{V_1 V_2}{V_2 - V_1}
= 2H_1\sqrt{\frac{V_2 + V_1}{V_2 - V_1}}.$$

For the model, $X_c = 2 \times 32.0 \times \sqrt{13.95/2.15}
= 163.02\ \mathrm{km}$, at which both branches read $27.6310\ \mathrm{s}$. The
demonstration's 10 km grid shows the head wave as the first arrival from 170 km
outward, 34 of the 50 grid distances. The apparent slowness between 300 and
500 km is $0.124224\ \mathrm{s\,km^{-1}}$, an apparent velocity of
$8.0500\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ — the refractor velocity exactly, which is the
statement that the head-wave branch's slope *is* $1/V_2$.

Two consequences:

- **No crossover exists for a velocity inversion** ($V_2 < V_1$). The head wave
  never overtakes the direct wave, so the branch is absent and the layer is
  invisible to first arrivals.
- The crossover grows as $\sqrt{H_1}$ for fixed velocities, so a deeper interface
  requires a longer survey to detect — the practical constraint on any array
  design.

## 6. Inversion: slopes are well determined, intercepts are not

Fitting $T = a + bX$ to the 16 synthetic picks gives
$a = 7.4138\ \mathrm{s}$ and $b = 0.124112\ \mathrm{s\,km^{-1}}$, so

$$V_2 = \frac{1}{b} = 8.0573\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}
\quad (\text{true } 8.05,\ \text{fitted uncertainty } \pm 0.0114),$$

and from the intercept,

$$H_1 = \frac{a\,V_1 V_2}{2\sqrt{V_2^{2} - V_1^{2}}} = 32.11\ \mathrm{km}
\quad (\text{true } 32.0\ \mathrm{km}).$$

The rms residual is $0.0648\ \mathrm{s}$ against an input noise of
$0.05\ \mathrm{s}$, so the fit is consistent with the noise and no structure is
being missed. The velocity is recovered to $+0.09\%$; the depth to $+0.34\%$.

**But the depth verdict is conditional.** The formula for $H_1$ contains $V_1$,
and $V_1$ was *assumed*. Re-deriving $H_1$ for three overburden velocities:

| assumed $V_1$ (km s⁻¹) | recovered $H_1$ (km) | change |
|---|---|---|
| 5.60 | 28.87 | $-10.1\%$ |
| 5.90 | 32.11 | — |
| 6.20 | 35.99 | $+12.1\%$ |

A $0.60\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ change in the assumed crustal velocity — about the
real spread between a sedimentary and a crystalline upper crust — moves the
inferred thickness from $28.9$ to $36.0\ \mathrm{km}$, a $24.6\%$ change that
dwarfs the $0.34\%$ statistical precision. **The travel-time curve constrains the
slope far better than the intercept**, and the reason is geometric: the slope is
set by the along-interface travel, which is long, while the intercept is set by
the two short vertical legs, whose contribution to the total time is small.

## 7. What the curve does not determine

1. **The velocity of any layer that is never the first arrival.** A hidden layer
   is invisible; a low-velocity layer is invisible. Both are demonstrated at
   project [25-02-04](./04-refraction-interpretation.md).
2. **The depth of an interface without the overburden velocity.** §6 is the
   measurement.
3. **Whether the layering is flat.** A dipping interface changes both the slope
   and the intercept, and a reversed (two-direction) profile is required to
   separate dip from velocity.
4. **The absolute position in space.** A travel-time curve is one-dimensional by
   construction; lateral variation appears as scatter about the fitted branches,
   and the fit's residuals are the only evidence of it.

## 8. Failure modes and how to detect them

1. **Fitting a branch over an offset range where it is not the first arrival.**
   *Detect:* compare the crossover with your selection range; the demonstration's
   branch-2 analogue would be selected from 170 km outward, not from 50 km.
2. **Taking $V_1$ from the wrong branch.** *Detect:* the direct branch must pass
   through the origin; a fitted intercept of $0.5\ \mathrm{s}$ on the near-offset
   branch means the origin time is wrong or the picks are not first arrivals.
3. **Quoting a depth without its overburden velocity.** *Detect:* §6, the
   $24.6\%$ swing.
4. **Treating the residual rms as an uncertainty.** It measures *fit* quality, not
   parameter uncertainty; the parameter uncertainty comes from the covariance
   matrix, and the two differ by $\sqrt{n}$.
5. **Ignoring a two-layer model's degeneracy at long offset.** Past a few hundred
   kilometres the curvature of the Earth and the upper-mantle gradient matter,
   and the straight-line model is wrong regardless of fit quality.

## 10. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **Flat, homogeneous layers.** Dip, topography and lateral velocity change all
   break the branch structure; the residual scatter is the only warning.
2. **Isotropic, constant velocity within a layer.** A vertical velocity gradient
   turns each straight branch into a curve and makes the "layer velocity" an
   average along the ray.
3. **The head wave exists.** It requires $V_2 > V_1$ and an interface long enough
   for the ray to travel along; for a thin high-velocity layer the branch may be
   too short to fit.
4. **Picks are first arrivals.** A second arrival picked as the first shifts the
   slope and the intercept in opposite directions.
5. **A one-dimensional Earth.** The result is an average over the path, not a
   profile beneath a station; a tomographic treatment is required for the latter.

## The honest summary

The project delivers a two-parameter fit and a lesson about conditioning. From
16 picks with $0.05\ \mathrm{s}$ noise the demonstration recovers
$V_2 = 8.0573 \pm 0.0114\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and, *given*
$V_1 = 5.90\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, $H_1 = 32.11\ \mathrm{km}$; the crossover is
$163.02\ \mathrm{km}$; and re-deriving the depth for $V_1$ between 5.60 and
$6.20\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ moves it between $28.9$ and $36.0\ \mathrm{km}$. The
transferable result is not "the crust is 32 km thick" but "the slope is a
measurement and the intercept is an assumption-dependent inference". A report
that quotes the depth without the assumed $V_1$, and the uncertainty without
noting that the systematic term dominates it, has reported the wrong number to
the right precision.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Reporting the depth as if the overburden velocity were measured.** It is an
   assumption worth $\pm10\%$ in the depth for a plausible velocity range.
2. **Fitting both branches with one line.** A single fit through the kink
   produces a velocity that is neither $V_1$ nor $V_2$.
3. **Confusing the residual rms with the parameter uncertainty.** The rms
   concerns the fit; the uncertainty concerns the parameters.
4. **Extrapolating a straight branch to great distance.** Sphericity and mantle
   gradients bend the real curve; the linear model is a local approximation.

## Where this is used

The refraction interpretation of
[25-02-04](./04-refraction-interpretation.md) is the same inversion at
engineering scale, where the hidden-layer problem is the standard trap. At
regional scale the method becomes local-earthquake tomography
([25-05-01](../05-doctoral-projects/01-tomography-project.md)), where the
one-dimensional average is replaced by a three-dimensional model and the
conditioning argument of §6 becomes the resolution matrix. In professional
practice, the crustal velocity and Moho depth of §6 are inputs to every
hypocentre location and to every ground-motion model, which is why the
assumption behind the depth is quoted alongside it.
