# Project: Mapping Global Seismicity from a Real Catalogue

## Learning objectives

By the end of this project you will be able to:

1. **Obtain** an earthquake catalogue from the USGS FDSN event service and
   **state** the query parameters that define it (region, time window, magnitude
   floor, catalogue version).
2. **Estimate** the magnitude of completeness $M_c$ and **explain** how the
   estimate changes the fitted $b$-value.
3. **Derive** the maximum-likelihood $b$-value for an exponential
   Gutenberg-Richter distribution and **compute** it, with the Aki standard
   error, from a magnitude list.
4. **Produce** a seismicity map that shows epicentre density, and **explain**
   why a density map recovers a plate boundary while a scatter plot of a few
   hundred events does not.
5. **Measure** depth statistics from a catalogue and **state** what the
   Wadati-Benioff geometry does and does not prove.
6. **Diagnose** the four defects that invalidate a published $b$-value: an
   inflated $M_c$, a truncated time window, a wrap at $\pm 180°$, and a magnitude
   scale change mid-catalogue.
7. **Report** a $b$-value as an estimate with a unit convention, a completeness
   statement and an uncertainty.

The written numbers in this project come from a **SYNTHETIC** catalogue
generated by [`code/earthquake_map_demo.py`](./code/earthquake_map_demo.py). The
*project itself* uses a real open archive — the USGS ANSS Comprehensive
Earthquake Catalog, queried through
<https://earthquake.usgs.gov/fdsnws/event/1/query> — and the lesson keeps the two
strictly separate: no number below is a measured property of the Earth.

## 1. The question

*Where do earthquakes happen, and how often does an event of a given size occur?*

Two products answer it, and both are quantitative:

- a **map** — the spatial distribution, which is a statement about plate
  geometry and about where the catalogue is complete;
- a **frequency-magnitude relation** — the $\log_{10}$-linear Gutenberg-Richter
  law, summarised by a single number, the **$b$-value**, whose fiducial value for
  global seismicity is close to $1$.

The project is the first one in which the deliverable is a *claim about the
Earth* rather than a formatting exercise, so it is also the first one where an
assumption (the magnitude of completeness) can move the headline number by more
than the reported uncertainty.

## 2. The data

**Real archive.** The catalogue is the USGS ANSS Comprehensive Earthquake
Catalog (ComCat), available through the FDSN event service at
<https://earthquake.usgs.gov/fdsnws/event/1/query>. A typical query fixes a
region, a start and end time, a minimum magnitude and an ordering, and returns
a plain catalogue with origin time, hypocentre, magnitude and the magnitude
scale used. Two rules follow from the archive's own documentation: record the
**query URL** verbatim in the report, and record the **magnitude type** —
`mb`, `Ms`, `Ml` and `Mw` are different scales and must not be mixed in one fit.

**Synthetic teaching catalogue.** Every number quoted below comes from the
demonstration, which generates 3000 events with a chosen $b = 1.00$, a chosen
completeness $M_c = 4.0\ \mathrm{M_w}$, a chosen bin width
$\Delta M = 0.1$, and a synthetic plate line
$\text{lat} = 35° + 12°\sin(2.5°(\text{lon} + 60°))$ over
$-90° \le \text{lon} \le 30°$. It is **SYNTHETIC** and its geometry resembles
the eastern Pacific rim only in the sense that events cluster on a line.

## 3. The project specification

**Steps.**

1. Query ComCat for one region and one time window and save the raw response
   unchanged, together with the query URL and the retrieval date.
2. Plot epicentres, then replot as a density (counts per cell) or with symbol
   area scaled by magnitude. Decide which one answers your question and say why.
3. Compute the magnitude-frequency histogram, estimate $M_c$, and fit $b$ three
   ways: at the maximum-curvature $M_c$, at $M_c + 0.5$, and with $M_c$ from a
   published regional study. Report all three.
4. Compute depth statistics: median depth, the 90th percentile, and the median
   depth in two longitude halves of the boundary.
5. Write the report: query, map, $b$-value with uncertainty, and the one
   assumption that would change the answer most.

**Deliverable.** A map, a frequency-magnitude plot with the fitted line, a table
of the three $b$ estimates, and a paragraph naming the dominant assumption.

**Success criterion.** The $b$-value is reported with its $M_c$ and its standard
error; the map is labelled with the projection and the catalogue query; the
report states what the catalogue cannot constrain (see §8).

## 4. The Gutenberg-Richter law and the b-value

The empirical relation is

$$\log_{10} N(\ge M) = a - b M,$$

with $N(\ge M)$ the number of events of magnitude at least $M$. Equivalently the
magnitudes above $M_c$ follow an exponential density

$$p(M) = \beta\, e^{-\beta (M - M_c)}, \qquad \beta = b \ln 10,$$

so $b = 1$ means $\beta = 2.3026$ and a mean magnitude step above completeness
of $1/\beta = 0.4343$. The likelihood of $N$ independent magnitudes is
$\prod_i \beta e^{-\beta(M_i - M_c)}$, and differentiating
$\ln L = N\ln\beta - \beta\sum_i (M_i - M_c)$ with respect to $\beta$ gives the
maximum-likelihood estimator

$$\hat\beta = \frac{1}{\overline{M} - M_c},
\qquad
\hat b = \frac{\log_{10} e}{\overline{M} - M_c}.$$

A catalogue records magnitudes in bins of width $\Delta M$, and the binning
biases $\overline{M}$ upward by $\Delta M/2$; the corrected estimator is

$$\hat b = \frac{\log_{10} e}{\overline{M} - \left(M_c - \tfrac{\Delta M}{2}\right)}.$$

Its standard error, from the Fisher information of the exponential, is
$\sigma_b \approx b/\sqrt{N}$.

## 5. Worked example: the demonstration's catalogue

The demonstration prints $\overline{M} = 4.3841$ and
$\log_{10} e = 0.434294$. With $M_c = 4.0$ and $\Delta M = 0.1$,

$$\hat b = \frac{0.434294}{4.3841 - (4.0 - 0.05)}
= \frac{0.434294}{0.4341} = 1.0005,$$

against a true value of $1.00$ — an error of $+0.0005$, or $0.03\sigma$ with
$\sigma_b = 1.0005/\sqrt{3000} = 0.0183$. The measured mean step is $0.4341$
against the exact $0.4343$. **The estimator is essentially unbiased at this
sample size**, which is why a $b$-value of $1.20 \pm 0.02$ from a regional
catalogue is a claim worth investigating rather than rounding away.

## 6. Completeness is the assumption that moves the answer

The demonstration's maximum-curvature estimate — the mode of the magnitude
histogram — is $M_c = 4.0$, exactly the generating value. Refitting above a
*higher* threshold moves $b$:

| assumed $M_c$ | events used $N$ | fitted $\hat b$ |
|---|---|---|
| $4.0$ | 3000 | $1.0005$ |
| $4.5$ | 920 | $0.9672$ |

The drift is $-0.033$ for a $+0.5$ shift in $M_c$ — twice the standard error
$\pm0.0183$. This is the central practical lesson: **the $M_c$ used for the fit
is part of the result**. A $b$-value quoted without its $M_c$ cannot be compared
with another, and a $b$-value computed from all events in a catalogue is biased
low because the catalogue is incomplete below the network's detection floor.

## 7. Depth and the Wadati-Benioff zone

Depths in the synthetic catalogue have median $180.0\ \mathrm{km}$, 90th
percentile $278.8\ \mathrm{km}$, and maximum $650.0\ \mathrm{km}$ (the clip is
$650\ \mathrm{km}$ — a synthetic bound, not a physical one); 18 events fall
within 5 km of the surface because of the lower clip at $5\ \mathrm{km}$. Within
2° of the synthetic plate line the depth quartiles are $150.3$, $190.4$ and
$230.7\ \mathrm{km}$, and the median is $187.0\ \mathrm{km}$ east of longitude 0
against $191.0\ \mathrm{km}$ west.

A deepening band of hypocentres defines a **Wadati-Benioff zone**, and its dip is
a statement about the subducting slab. Two limits belong in the report: the
catalogue's magnitude floor controls which parts of the slab are sampled at all
(small slab earthquakes are detected only near the dense part of a network), and
hypocentre depth errors are typically several kilometres, so a 4 km difference
between two-half medians is not evidence of anything.

## 8. Making the map, and what it can say

1. **Projection.** A plate-scale map is drawn in an equal-area or conformal
   projection, not in raw longitude-latitude, or the density is distorted by up
   to a factor of $\cos(\text{lat})$.
2. **Longitude wrap.** Events at $+179°$ and $-179°$ are neighbours; a naive
   bounding box splits a cluster and destroys it. Handle the wrap explicitly.
3. **Density, not scatter.** With 3000 events, $2319$ (77.3%) fall within 2° of
   the synthetic plate line — a density contrast that is obvious when the map
   shows counts per cell and invisible when it shows a few hundred dots. The
   measured density is $16.1$ events per $10°\times10°$ box on the line.
4. **What it cannot say.** A catalogue is a record of *detected* events; it
   cannot distinguish "no earthquakes" from "no stations". Nothing in this
   project constrains seismic hazard, because hazard needs recurrence rates at
   magnitudes below the completeness level, which the catalogue does not
   contain.

## 9. Failure modes and how to detect them

1. **An inflated or unstated $M_c$.** *Detect:* refit above $M_c + 0.5$; the
   demonstration shows a $0.033$ shift. If your $b$ moves more than $2\sigma_b$,
   the completeness estimate is the controlling assumption.
2. **Mixing magnitude scales.** *Detect:* tabulate the magnitude-type column; a
   bimodal type distribution means the fit is over two scales.
3. **A truncated time window.** *Detect:* plot the annual event count. A step in
   detection, from a network upgrade or an archive cut-off, produces a fake
   change in rate.
4. **The $\pm 180°$ wrap.** *Detect:* the count in the bounding box is lower than
   the count of plotted points.
5. **Quoting $b$ to three decimals from 100 events.** *Detect:* compute
   $\sigma_b = b/\sqrt{N}$; for $N = 100$ and $b = 1$ that is $0.100$, so only
   the first decimal is meaningful.

| Criterion | 4 — exemplary | 3 — competent | 2 — developing | 1 — incomplete |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Map | Density, projection stated, wrap handled, legend | Density and labels | Scatter plot | No units or scale |
| $b$-value | $\hat b$, $M_c$, $\sigma_b$, three $M_c$ variants | $\hat b$ and $\sigma_b$ | $\hat b$ only | No uncertainty |
| Depth | Quartiles and a stated slab caveat | Median depth | Depth histogram | Absent |
| Limitations | Names the dominant assumption and what hazard is *not* constrained | Names one | Generic | Absent |

## 11. Assumptions and regime of validity

1. **The catalogue is complete above $M_c$.** Everything in §4–§5 depends on it;
   §6 measures what a violation costs.
2. **Magnitudes are on one scale.** The synthetic catalogue has one by
   construction; a real query must state the type filter.
3. **Events are independent.** Aftershocks violate this: one mainshock and 500
   aftershocks are not 501 independent draws, and the effective $N$ is much
   smaller than the row count. Declustering is the standard remedy and changes
   $b$ by an amount that must be reported.
4. **$b$ is a property of a region and a depth range.** A global fit mixes
   subduction, ridge and intraplate populations; the single $b$ is an average of
   different physics.
5. **The map reflects detection as much as tectonics.** Network density,
   magnitude floor and colonial-era archive gaps all imprint on the picture.

## The honest summary

This project produces two numbers and one picture: an $M_c$, a $b$-value with its
standard error, and a density map. On the synthetic catalogue the pipeline
recovers $b = 1.0005 \pm 0.0183$ for a true $b = 1.00$ at
$M_c = 4.0\ \mathrm{M_w}$, and refitting at $M_c + 0.5$ moves it to $0.9672$ —
a shift twice the statistical uncertainty. The transferable statement is
therefore not the digit but the triple: **$\hat b = 1.0005 \pm 0.0183$ at
$M_c = 4.0\ \mathrm{M_w}$**, with the declustering choice and the magnitude type
named. A $b$-value without those three is not a result.

## Mastery checkpoint

Without notes:

1. State the MLE $b$-value formula for binned magnitudes and explain each term.
2. With $\overline{M} = 3.72$, $M_c = 3.5$ and $\Delta M = 0.1$, compute
   $\hat b$.
3. The demonstration's $b$ moves from $1.0005$ to $0.9672$ when $M_c$ rises from
   $4.0$ to $4.5$. Is $0.033$ significant at $N = 920$? Justify with
   $\sigma_b = b/\sqrt{N}$.
4. Name the catalogue property that makes a $b$-value computed from *all*
   events biased, and state the direction of the bias.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Fitting every event.** Below $M_c$ the counts fall away from the line, and
   including them biases $b$ low.
2. **Reporting $b$ without $\sigma_b$.** With $N = 100$ events the standard
   error is $0.100$, so three decimals claim a precision that does not exist.
3. **Mixing magnitude scales.** `mb` and `Mw` differ systematically by up to
   several tenths; one fit across both is a fit to nothing.
4. **Treating a scatter plot as a map of tectonic structure.** Ten thousand dots
   hide the density contrast that a $1°$ grid makes obvious.
5. **Forgetting aftershocks.** They inflate $N$ and contaminate the small- and
   large-magnitude ends differently.
